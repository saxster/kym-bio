Chapter 4 left a box of carbon building units on the workbench. Link them one way and you have a store of starch; link them another way and you have the fibres of a cell wall. Enzymes help the chemistry run. But a plant cannot build a trunk from instructions alone. Where do the atoms come from?

Look at a shade tree beside a Mumbai pavement. Its roots disappear into soil. Its leaves spread into air. The tempting answer lies under your feet. The crucial missing ingredient is also passing your face.

## 5.1 The five-year willow tree

:::scene A willow experiment, reported in 1648

A seed has a little stored material. It can supply the beginning of a root and shoot; it cannot contain all the material of the eventual tree. Somewhere, matter must cross the growing plant's boundary.

Roots grip soil, so perhaps a tree eats the soil and turns it into wood. That is a respectable guess. It makes a prediction you could test with a balance: the earth should lose material as the tree gains it.

:::predict
Suppose a tree becomes much heavier while the dried soil loses very little mass. Which conclusion is justified?
- The tree's entire gain must be dried soil transferred into it
- Soil alone cannot account for the reported gain; other inputs must be investigated
- Soil and roots have no function
???
The second. A mismatch challenges the soil-only account. It does not identify every missing input or show that mineral nutrients are unnecessary.
:::

:::evidence A tree and its unbalanced ledger | Published posthumously, 1648
Jan Baptista van Helmont described putting 200 pounds of oven-dried earth into an earthen pot and planting a willow shoot of 5 pounds. He supplied rainwater or distilled water and covered the pot with a perforated tin-plated iron lid to limit incoming dust. After five years, he reported a tree of 169 pounds and about 3 ounces. The soil, dried again, had lost about 2 ounces.

The tree's gain was large beside the reported soil loss. But he had not weighed the fallen leaves, total water supplied or gases entering and leaving. Nor was the tree established to be dry at either weighing. He concluded that the new material came from water alone. His account appeared in *Ortus medicinae* in 1648, after his death; 1648 is not the date the five-year experiment began. We keep his reported units because their exact modern conversion is uncertain.
:::

:::fig c5_ledger() | Recorded measurements and missing entries belong in different parts of the ledger. The original tree measurements are not a modern dry-mass balance.

The balance helped expose a missing source. It did not finish the investigation. Water was a material input, but the pot was open to gas exchange too. Modern evidence shows that the carbon in plant carbohydrates comes from carbon dioxide. The gas enters leaves; its carbon atoms can become the carbon backbone of cellulose in wood. Water supplies matter as well as supporting chemistry and transport.

We must also ask *what kind of mass was weighed*. **Fresh mass** includes water present in the plant. ==Dry mass== is measured after water has been removed by a specified drying method. A leaf can gain fresh mass by taking up water without building the same amount of new organic material. Calling the willow's whole gain “new dry wood” would make the evidence stronger than it was.

:::note Dry mass
Mass after water has been removed by a stated drying procedure. It helps distinguish accumulated plant material from changing water content; it is not the same measurement as fresh mass.
:::

Most dry organic material in an ordinary green plant is assembled from carbon dioxide and water, rather than eaten as soil. Yet a small contribution can be essential. Soil supplies mineral ions: nitrate provides nitrogen for making amino acids, and magnesium is required for chlorophyll, the light-absorbing pigment we will meet inside the leaf. Roots also obtain water and anchor the plant. “Not most of the mass” never means “unimportant”.

:::five Plant mass
? What is it?
The matter in a plant, including water, carbon-containing molecules and mineral substances. Fresh and dry mass count different things.
? Why is it called this?
“Dry” states the preparation before weighing. It is a measurement description, not a claim that a living tree contains no water.
? What problem did it solve?
Weighing inputs and outputs tests where a growing plant's material could have come from.
? Why does it work?
Atoms must enter before they can be incorporated into new plant material. A balance can expose an incomplete account.
? How does it help?
It directs attention from visible soil alone to water and invisible gases, while keeping essential minerals in the picture.
?? How do we know?
Combine material measurements with controlled gas experiments and chemical tracing; the willow ledger alone is incomplete.
?? What it isn't
A tree creating matter, a seed containing its future trunk, or proof that soil contributes nothing.
?? Where does it stop working?
An unmeasured water content, missing fallen leaves and uncertain historical units prevent a precise modern dry-mass calculation.
:::

:::careful
> “Air weighs nothing, so solid wood cannot come from it.”

Gases consist of atoms and have mass. Incorporating carbon atoms from carbon dioxide into a solid changes their chemical partners and arrangement; it does not summon matter from nothing. Sunlight will supply energy, not those carbon atoms.
:::

:::

No. Increased water content can raise fresh mass. Establishing carbohydrate accumulation requires appropriate measurements and comparisons; weighing a wet plant alone does not identify the source or chemical form of its gain.

## 5.2 Bubbles in the sunlight

:::scene England, an experiment in 1771 and a book in 1779

If air contributes material, leaves should change the air around them. But “plants improve air” is too vague. What changes, under which conditions, and how could you tell?

A green leaf sitting quietly looks inactive. Put suitable green material under water in light, and gas may collect as bubbles. It is tempting to call every bubble a parcel of newly made oxygen, and every missing bubble proof that the chemistry stopped. First separate what you see from what you infer.

:::predict
A plant in weak light shows no net oxygen release. Must photosynthesis be absent?
- Yes; photosynthesis always produces a visible surplus
- No; production and oxygen consumption can balance
- Yes; plants respire only after sunset
???
The second. Photosynthesis can occur while respiration consumes oxygen. No net release does not by itself establish no production; invisible dissolved gas also complicates bubble observations.
:::

:::evidence The missing conditions | 1771, 1779 and 1804
Joseph Priestley reported placing mint in air in which a candle had burned out on 17 August 1771. On 27 August another candle could burn in that air. He also divided altered air, keeping one portion with a plant and another without, in the same exposure. The later candle tests differed. That was evidence of a change in the air, not an atom map.

Jan Ingenhousz's 1779 book investigated the conditions. He collected gas from leaves in water-filled transparent vessels, compared light and shade, and distinguished green tissues from other plant parts. Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure's 1804 work joined gas analysis to plant-material accounting: carbon dioxide mattered, and water also contributed material. These were different investigations. The molecular explanation developed later. Their hazardous historical gas tests are not activities to reproduce at home.
:::

:::fig c5_gas_evidence() | A comparison map of historical questions. The dates label the reported investigation or publication; these rows do not reconstruct one shared apparatus.

Today we explain the oxygen-producing process in green tissues using light, water and carbon dioxide. We also know that plant cells carry out respiration in light and darkness. In aerobic respiration, cells use oxygen while transferring chemical energy from nutrients into forms useful for cellular work. A lit leaf can therefore be making oxygen and consuming oxygen at once.

What an instrument detects leaving the leaf is the balance. ==Net gas exchange== means the result after contributions in opposite directions are combined. If oxygen production exceeds consumption, there is a net release. If consumption exceeds production, there is net uptake. At a balance, the net can be zero while both processes continue. Other processes, including photorespiration, can also affect a real leaf's gas exchange; a two-process classroom model leaves those out.

:::note Net gas exchange
The overall gas movement measured after simultaneous production and consumption are combined. A net measurement does not reveal each underlying rate separately.
:::

A bubble count is another layer removed. Large and small bubbles contain different amounts of gas. Some gas dissolves instead of forming bubbles; collection can lag, leak or include gas released as water warms. Counting without controlling conditions and calibrating gas volume cannot give an exact oxygen-production rate or photosynthetic efficiency.

In a **supervised school investigation**, a teacher can compare suitable aquatic plant material in light and darkness with hydrogencarbonate indicator, which responds to changes associated with dissolved carbon dioxide. Include indicator without a plant, matched starting conditions and repeats; control temperature, plant amount and exposure time. An indicator change concerns **net carbon dioxide exchange**, not a direct measurement of glucose or a separate respiration rate. A teacher chooses a lawful plant source and collects material for suitable disposal; never release it into natural waterways.

:::five Net gas exchange
? What is it?
The measured balance between gas entering and leaving a living system.
? Why is it called this?
“Net” means what remains after opposing contributions are accounted for.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why an active leaf may show a surplus, a deficit or no overall change in oxygen.
? Why does it work?
Oxygen-producing photosynthesis and oxygen-consuming respiration can occur simultaneously, so their contributions combine.
? How does it help?
It prevents a quiet bubble collector or unchanged indicator from being mistaken for a complete absence of metabolism.
?? How do we know?
Controlled gas measurements under varied light reveal changing net exchange; separate evidence establishes the contributing processes.
?? What it isn't
A rule that photosynthesis happens by day and respiration switches on only at night.
?? Where does it stop working?
A net reading alone cannot separate all contributing rates. Dissolution, temperature, leaks and other metabolism require attention.
:::

:::story p-ingenhousz

:::careful
> “Twice as many bubbles means exactly twice as much oxygen.”

Only if each bubble represented the same known amount, collection were reliable and the other conditions were controlled could such a conversion be justified. A bubble count is an imperfect proxy, not an automatic calibration.
:::

:::

:::check
A teacher obtains net oxygen release from a lit plant. Does this show that respiration has stopped? Explain using a balance.
???
No. Oxygen production can exceed oxygen consumption, leaving a surplus even while respiration continues. The net release gives the difference, not proof that the consuming process is absent.

## 5.3 Inside the green solar panel

A leaf does not catch carbon atoms falling from sunlight. Its material and energy arrive by different routes. Carbon dioxide diffuses in from air; water arrives through the plant. Light transfers energy to machinery inside certain cells.

The tempting shortcut is “chlorophyll makes food”. A pigment name cannot do the explaining. What does absorbed light actually change?

:::predict
You could mark oxygen atoms in the supplied water and follow them. Where should that mark appear during oxygen-producing photosynthesis?
- In the released oxygen gas
- Only in the carbohydrate carbon
- Nowhere; water only carries minerals
???
In released oxygen gas. Marked oxygen is still oxygen, and oxygen cannot become carbohydrate carbon through these chemical reactions. Water is a chemical participant as well as a transport medium.
:::

:::evidence Follow the oxygen, not the shape of an equation | 1941
Samuel Ruben, Merle Randall, Martin Kamen and James Logan Hyde reported experiments using heavy oxygen as a tracer in photosynthesis. An isotope is a form of the same element with a different nuclear mass. Its chemical behaviour makes tracing possible; it is not automatically radioactive.

In experiments with green algae, oxygen enriched in the supplied water appeared in the oxygen evolved. Comparisons and attention to oxygen exchange between water and dissolved carbon compounds were important. The evidence supported water as the source of released photosynthetic oxygen. This answers a question the familiar net equation cannot settle just by looking at its layout. Later molecular work identifies water oxidation in the light-dependent machinery. We are using the tracer result as evidence, not offering an isotope experiment or a complete history of every contributor.
:::

Inside a chloroplast are internal membrane sacs called thylakoids, surrounded by fluid called the stroma. Light-absorbing molecules are held with proteins in the thylakoid membranes. The green pigment ==chlorophyll== absorbs light and transfers excitation energy into a system where electrons can be passed between molecules. It does not turn into sugar or create energy.

:::note Chlorophyll
A green light-absorbing pigment in the photosynthetic machinery. In plant chloroplasts it helps transfer absorbed light energy into chemical processes.
:::

The machinery removes electrons from water; oxygen is released. Light-supported electron transfers help build a difference in proton concentration across a membrane. As in Chapter 3, a concentration and charge difference across a membrane stores energy. When protons return through an ATP-making enzyme, some of that gradient energy supports ATP formation. ATP transfers usable chemical energy to other reactions; another carrier, NADPH, supplies electrons for building carbon compounds. You do not need a parade of names to follow the connection: **absorb light → drive chemical transfers → supply the building reactions**.

In the stroma, enzyme-controlled reactions incorporate carbon dioxide into organic molecules. Small three-carbon products can leave the carbon-fixation cycle and supply larger sugars. So glucose is a useful overall school summary, rather than the immediate product of every step. The connected process is ==photosynthesis==: synthesis of carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light.

:::note Photosynthesis
In green plants, light-supported synthesis of carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water. Released oxygen comes from water; the carbohydrate carbon comes from carbon dioxide.
:::

:::fig c5_chloroplast() | Matter and energy have different jobs. This enlarged schematic separates water-derived oxygen from carbon incorporation; the fuller equation is an overall account, not a literal pathway for every atom.
:::live carbon_account {"lesson":true}

:::eq
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
:::

:::eq
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
:::

Write **light energy and chlorophyll** alongside this summary. Light is an energy transfer, not another collection of ingredient atoms. Chlorophyll is part of the machinery, not a substance used up in the quantities shown.

To keep the water account visible, a fuller summary is **6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O**. Cancelling equal water terms gives the net equation. Cancellation is arithmetic; it does not show oxygen gas coming from carbon dioxide. Count atoms in the fuller form: each side has 6 carbon, 24 hydrogen and 24 oxygen atoms. The source of released O₂ comes from the tracer evidence and mechanism, not from guessing which formula is nearest it on the page.

Carbohydrates can supply starch stores, cellulose walls, sucrose for transport and respiration. Sugars in nectar can attract pollinating insects. Building amino acids also requires nitrogen, so making carbohydrate never removes the requirement for mineral nutrients.

:::five Photosynthesis
? What is it?
A connected process using absorbed light energy to build carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water in green plants.
? Why is it called this?
Its Greek roots mean putting together with the help of light. The name identifies the task; the mechanism explains it.
? What problem did it solve?
It supplies organic building material and a chemical energy store using widely available carbon dioxide and water.
? Why does it work?
Light-supported transfers provide ATP and electrons; enzyme-controlled reactions incorporate carbon dioxide into small organic products.
? How does it help?
It connects invisible atmospheric carbon to plant sugars, stores and structural material, and supplies oxygen to the surroundings.
?? How do we know?
Gas measurements, isotope tracing and studies of chloroplast machinery give different, complementary kinds of evidence.
?? What it isn't
Energy creation, sunlight becoming carbon atoms, or a single collision producing a finished glucose molecule.
?? Where does it stop working?
The equation omits intermediate steps and cannot predict rate. Not every plant cell has chloroplasts, and some photosynthetic organisms use different chemistry.
:::

:::recall 4
If enzymes lower activation barriers, why does a leaf still require an energy input to synthesise carbohydrate from carbon dioxide and water?
???
A catalyst provides a lower-barrier route but does not supply the net energy needed for an unfavourable overall transformation. Light-supported processes provide that input; enzymes help the connected reactions proceed.
:::

Now turn up the light. Does production rise without limit? Only while the machinery can use the additional input. Carbon dioxide may arrive too slowly, or temperature may restrict the enzyme-controlled steps. A ==limiting factor== is a condition restricting the rate under the circumstances being examined. Changing another condition may achieve little until that restriction changes.

:::note Limiting factor
A condition that restricts the rate of a process in the conditions being studied. Which factor limits photosynthesis can change as light, carbon dioxide availability and temperature change.
:::

At low light, more carbon dioxide may make little difference. At adequate light, more carbon dioxide may increase rate until another restriction matters. Temperature affects enzyme activity, but each species, condition and exposure history matters; there is no single optimum for every plant. Excessive light, heat or water shortage can damage or restrict the system. Our explorer uses three chosen caps to make the logic visible. Its sharp corners and temperature peak are invented, not fitted plant measurements.

:::fig c5_limits() | Chosen model curves, not data. A lower carbon-dioxide capacity caps the benefit of increasing light. Other conditions, including temperature, can impose a different cap.
:::live limiting_lab {"lesson":true}

:::five Limiting factors
? What is it?
A way to identify which available condition currently restricts photosynthetic rate.
? Why is it called this?
The limiting factor sets a limit under the particular conditions, rather than a permanent ranking of importance.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why increasing one input sometimes helps and sometimes produces little change.
? Why does it work?
Connected steps must all function. Extra light cannot supply missing carbon dioxide or restore damaged machinery by itself.
? How does it help?
Change one factor, keep others comparable and measure an outcome; then test whether a different restriction has taken over.
?? How do we know?
Controlled comparisons across light, carbon dioxide and temperature reveal changing rate responses.
?? What it isn't
An unlimited growth promise or one universal temperature optimum.
?? Where does it stop working?
A simple cap model omits interacting responses, adaptation and damage history. It cannot predict an actual plant's rate.
:::

:::careful
> “A leaf is a solar panel, so brighter and hotter must always be better.”

A leaf's chemistry also requires supplies, functional enzymes and water balance. More of one input cannot replace the others. The analogy concerns energy capture; a leaf also makes and transports material and continually respires.
:::

At **school**, an investigation can compare illuminated and covered areas, or green and non-green regions of a suitable variegated leaf, with appropriate controls for prior starch, light exposure and plant condition. Carbon dioxide comparisons require matched plants, light, temperature, water and exposure conditions. A later starch result concerns accumulation over the interval, which also depends on use and transport; it is not an instantaneous photosynthesis meter. Repeats and reliable controls matter as in Chapter 4.

A leaf starch test involving hot ethanol belongs **only in a supervised school laboratory**, under the teacher's risk assessment. A teacher manages hot preparation; **never heat ethanol over a flame**. No tasting, mouth pipetting or household reagent substitutes. The teacher collects chemical waste. These are experimental-design ideas, not instructions for a home starch test.

Increasing carbon dioxide has no effect in a dim chamber. Increasing light at the same carbon dioxide concentration then increases the measured rate. What does that support, and what would it not establish?

It supports light being limiting in the original conditions. It does not establish that carbon dioxide never limits photosynthesis, that unlimited light will keep increasing rate, or that every plant shares the same response. Further controlled comparisons could reveal a new limit.

## 5.4 Engineering a flat solar factory

Turn a familiar houseplant leaf edge-on. Much of the surface you saw face-on almost disappears. A broad, thin blade can intercept light over a large area while keeping diffusion distances short. But a broad wet surface also risks losing water. How can the same organ admit gas and conserve liquid?

One tempting design would seal the leaf completely. Water loss would fall, but carbon dioxide delivery would suffer too.

:::predict
If most gas-exchange pores close on a water-stressed leaf in daylight, what trade-off is likely?
- Water loss can decrease, but carbon dioxide entry can also decrease
- Closing the pores supplies extra carbon atoms
- Water loss must rise because light is present
???
The first. Restricting a shared gas route affects both water vapour leaving and carbon dioxide entering. Actual rates also depend on the surrounding conditions.
:::

:::evidence A section through a leaf | Microscopy, not a flat-cell model
Microscope sections reveal layers within a typical dicot leaf. A waxy cuticle lies outside the epidermis. Below the upper epidermis, elongated palisade cells contain many chloroplasts; the looser spongy tissue has interconnecting air spaces. Veins contain different conducting tissues. Surface views reveal pores bordered by paired guard cells.

A cross-section and a surface view answer different questions. The section shows the path from a pore into internal air spaces and towards moist cell surfaces. The surface view shows the guard-cell pair around its opening. Neither view makes every plant cell a flat shape. Leaves vary with species and environment, so this representative arrangement is a useful specimen map, not a rule that every leaf has identical layers or lower-surface pores only.
:::

:::fig c5_leaf() | Follow light through the upper layers, gas through the pore and air spaces, and liquid through the vein. The lower inset changes viewing direction; its guard cells are enlarged and schematic.

The outer **cuticle** reduces water loss across the surface. The **upper and lower epidermis** form protective surface layers; in the representative leaf, the upper layer lets light reach the tissue beneath. The **palisade mesophyll** packs many chloroplasts into elongated cells. The **spongy mesophyll** also photosynthesises, while spaces between its cells provide routes for gas diffusion. Carbon dioxide must reach a moist cell surface, dissolve and move into cells before it reaches chloroplast chemistry.

The ==stomata== are pores through the epidermis. Each pore is a stoma; its neighbouring **guard cells** alter its opening. Veins contain **xylem**, delivering water and mineral ions, and **phloem**, carrying assimilated substances such as sucrose. Those two tissues connect the leaf to the rest of the plant.

:::note Stomata
Small epidermal pores providing routes for gas exchange. Changes in the shape of surrounding guard cells alter the openings and affect both carbon dioxide entry and water-vapour loss.
:::

Here is the opening mechanism. Signals affect ion transport and other solutes in guard cells. A lower water potential can draw water in by osmosis, raising turgor. Their wall structure, including differences in thickness and the arrangement of reinforcing fibres, converts swelling into a change in shape that can open the pore. Solute loss and water movement out can reverse the shape change. Guard cells do not decide which gas to admit; physical and chemical changes alter a route through which gases diffuse.

Many familiar dicot leaves have numerous stomata underneath, but some leaves have them on both surfaces or elsewhere. Nor do all stomata follow one day/night timetable. Plant type, light, carbon dioxide and water status matter. The same trade-off returns whenever carbon delivery competes with water conservation.

:::five Leaf structure and stomata
? What is it?
A thin photosynthetic organ whose layers and regulated pores connect light capture, gas exchange and transport.
? Why is it called this?
Stoma comes from Greek for mouth: an opening. The name describes a pore, not a tiny breathing animal.
? What problem did it solve?
The leaf provides a large light-catching area while maintaining short internal routes and controlling water loss.
? Why does it work?
Chloroplast-rich tissue intercepts light, spaces permit diffusion, veins carry supplies and products, and guard-cell water relations change pore width.
? How does it help?
You can explain the function of a visible structure instead of memorising an unconnected list of layers.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy reveals the structures; water-relation and gas-exchange measurements test their functions.
?? What it isn't
A universal leaf blueprint, a claim that every cell has chloroplasts, or a conscious valve choosing individual gas molecules.
?? Where does it stop working?
A simple dicot diagram does not represent every aquatic, desert or grass leaf, and a drawing alone cannot measure stomatal behaviour.
:::

:::careful
> “All plant cells are green factories, and every pore closes only at night.”

Root hair cells and many other plant cells lack developed chloroplasts. Stomatal behaviour varies. Even a green leaf with open pores still requires suitable conditions and functioning chemistry to photosynthesise.
:::

Use a familiar, safe houseplant already in your home. Leave the leaf attached. In diffuse daylight, look at its broad surface, thin edge, visible veins and both surfaces; gently move the pot only if an adult can do so safely. Do not climb, taste, pluck, cut or rub sap, and avoid unfamiliar or irritating plants. Keep it away from hot direct sun while inspecting it.

Sketch what you can actually see, then label which features would require magnification. Compare another attached leaf if available. This is an observation activity, not a physical trial performed for this book.

## 5.5 Pulling water upwards without a heart

The leaf now has an entrance for carbon dioxide. Water still has to reach it from roots below. If every part of a tree required a little heart to push water higher, where are the pumps?

The tempting answer is “roots suck it up”. That moves the mystery underground. Begin instead where liquid leaves the system.

:::predict
Which change can help drive the upward transpiration stream?
- Evaporation from moist leaf surfaces creates a pull transmitted through connected water
- An ATP-powered pump pushes water through every section of the trunk
- Each water molecule swims deliberately towards the light
???
The first. Water molecules attract one another, so tension created near evaporating surfaces can be transmitted through liquid in xylem. Roots replace the water lost along the route.
:::

:::evidence A connected liquid can transmit a pull | The cohesion–tension account
Measurements of xylem pressure and leaf water loss support water transport under tension during transpiration. Water evaporates from moist mesophyll walls into internal air spaces, then water vapour diffuses through stomata to the surroundings. As water is lost, the remaining air–water surfaces curve more strongly within tiny wall spaces. Surface tension pulls on the remaining liquid and lowers its pressure.

Attractions between water molecules allow that tension to be transmitted down connected liquid in the xylem. Water replaces the loss from lower parts and ultimately from the soil. A gas blockage can interrupt a conducting route: continuity matters. A drawing of a hollow tube alone would not establish this mechanism; the water relations, negative pressures and effects of interrupted pathways provide the physical evidence.
:::

Water-vapour loss from leaves is ==transpiration==. Liquid-to-vapour change occurs at moist surfaces; diffusion carries vapour through the spaces and pores. Evaporation, liquid flow and vapour diffusion are connected steps, not interchangeable names for one event.

:::note Transpiration
Loss of water vapour from leaves. Evaporation from moist internal surfaces and diffusion through stomata connect leaf water loss to the transpiration stream.
:::

==Cohesion== is attraction between molecules of the same substance. Hydrogen bonding gives water important cohesive properties. **Tension** means a pulling stress: the liquid column is under a pull rather than a sequence of ATP-driven upward shoves. Adhesion to walls also helps maintain the liquid connection. The **cohesion–tension** account explains how leaf evaporation can pull a continuous stream through the plant. It is not atmospheric pressure pushing water up an open drinking straw.

:::note Cohesion
Attraction between molecules of the same substance. In xylem, cohesion helps transmit tension through connected liquid water.
:::

:::fig c5_xylem() | Trace the causal chain from the leaf downwards, then trace the water's replacement route upwards. The arrows show a conceptual direction, not a measured height or velocity.
:::live sap_routes {"lesson":true}

Mature xylem vessel elements form hollow passages with no living cell contents. Their end walls have large openings called perforation plates, joining successive elements into connected water passages. Their thick, lignified walls provide support and resist collapse under tension; pits connect neighbouring routes. Water and dissolved mineral ions travel through this network. Some roots can generate **root pressure**, but this is not the general explanation for supplying the tops of tall trees. Cavitation, the formation or expansion of gas spaces, can break a liquid pathway and obstruct flow.

:::recall 3
Does every step of water movement from soil to air count as osmosis? Where does active ion transport fit?
???
No. Osmosis describes water movement across a partially permeable membrane down a water-potential gradient. Flow through xylem and diffusion of water vapour are different steps. Active ion transport can alter solute conditions and requires energy; that does not make the whole xylem stream an ATP-driven pump.
:::

In a transpiring plant, water moves along decreasing **total water potential** from soil through roots, xylem and mesophyll towards the atmosphere. The root route includes root hairs and cortex before xylem. Pressure as well as dissolved substances contributes to water potential; gravity matters over height. “Higher to lower” refers to the total, not salt concentration alone.

Warmer conditions can promote evaporation; wind can remove the humid layer beside a leaf. Greater surrounding humidity reduces the water-vapour difference driving loss. These tendencies depend on the plant's responses too. If water loss exceeds supply, cells lose turgor and the plant can wilt; stomatal closure may reduce further loss while restricting carbon dioxide entry. No single weather control guarantees a particular rate.

:::five Transpiration and cohesion–tension
? What is it?
Leaf water-vapour loss linked to a pull through cohesive liquid in xylem, with water replacing losses from roots.
? Why is it called this?
Transpiration has roots meaning breathing through; here it means water-vapour loss, not cellular respiration. Cohesion means sticking together; tension is a pull.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains an upward water stream without a heart or an ATP-driven pump throughout the trunk.
? Why does it work?
Evaporation changes tiny curved water surfaces and liquid pressure. Cohesion transmits the resulting tension through connected water.
? How does it help?
It connects leaf conditions, root water supply, vessel structure and wilting in one causal chain.
?? How do we know?
Leaf water-loss measurements, xylem-pressure evidence and interruptions of liquid pathways test parts of the account.
?? What it isn't
Water molecules choosing to climb, an open-straw atmospheric push, or root pressure explaining every tall tree.
?? Where does it stop working?
A broken liquid route cannot transmit the same pull; drought, gas blockages and restricted supply can limit transport.
:::

There is a second delivery problem. A leaf makes carbohydrate, but roots, developing fruits and growing shoot tips use it too. Xylem is not the whole distribution system.

In living **phloem** pathways, sap carries sucrose and amino acids from a **source**, which releases them, to a **sink**, which uses or stores them. This movement is ==translocation==. A mature leaf can supply roots below or a growing shoot above through different pathways. A storage organ can be a sink while filling, then become a source when it releases material to a new shoot. Position alone does not define the role.

:::note Translocation
Movement of assimilated substances, including sucrose and amino acids, in phloem from sources to sinks. The direction depends on the connected source and sink, not a rule that food travels downwards.
:::

How does it move? Loading sugar into source phloem lowers its water potential. Water enters by osmosis, often from neighbouring xylem, raising pressure. At the sink, unloading and use or storage remove sugar; water can leave and rejoin other pathways. The resulting pressure difference drives bulk flow of sap through sieve tubes, whose end plates have pores. Companion cells support the living sieve elements.

Some loading routes use metabolic energy. For example, an ATP-powered proton pump establishes a gradient that helps carry sucrose into cells—the energy relay from Chapter 3. Other species can load through passive routes; not every plant uses the same mechanism. Energy-dependent loading is also different from pumping the sap at every point along the tube. Opposing source–sink movements can occur in **different pathways**, not as two contrary bulk streams through the same tube at the same moment.

:::five Phloem translocation
? What is it?
Transport of assimilates such as sucrose and amino acids from sources to sinks in living phloem pathways.
? Why is it called this?
Translocation means moving from one place to another. Xylem comes from Greek for wood, phloem from Greek for bark; those names do not explain flow.
? What problem did it solve?
It connects places releasing organic material to places using or storing it, including organs without chloroplasts.
? Why does it work?
Loading promotes osmotic water entry and higher source pressure; unloading and use sustain a pressure difference that drives sap flow.
? How does it help?
It explains why roots receive sugars and how stored reserves can later supply an upward-growing shoot.
?? How do we know?
Tracing assimilated carbon and studying sieve pathways, loading and pressure relations tests the source–sink account.
?? What it isn't
The transpiration stream, food always travelling downwards, or opposite bulk flows in one tube simultaneously.
?? Where does it stop working?
A simple two-end diagram omits branching networks, changing sinks and species differences in loading. It cannot predict transport speed.
:::

:::careful
> “Xylem is water going up; phloem is food going down. That is all.”

You have remembered two arrows and lost the mechanisms. Xylem carries water and mineral ions in the transpiration stream. Phloem carries assimilates from sources to sinks; those connections can run upwards or downwards. Explain the pull in one and the pressure difference in the other.
:::

## The sealed-garden case

:::case A lid closes the matter account, not the energy account
This is a **fictional reasoning case**, not a report about a famous bottle with a verified sealing date. A transparent terrarium contains suitable plants, damp substrate and decomposers. Its lid stays closed, matter exchange is very limited, and suitable indirect light reaches it. Droplets sometimes condense on the glass and return to the substrate. For the period observed, the plants remain alive without added water.

Someone concludes: “It needs nothing from outside. Water, oxygen and energy all go round forever. The plants make oxygen by day, and only the decomposers respire.”

Find the mistakes. Where can water and carbon go inside? What must still cross the boundary? Does survival over the observed period guarantee survival indefinitely?
???
Water can enter roots, leave leaves as vapour, condense and return. Carbon can move from carbon dioxide into plant organic material and back through respiration by plants and decomposers. Decomposition can return mineral nutrients to available forms. These are material cycles, with finite supplies and biological constraints.

Light transfers energy through the glass. Photosynthesis can store some of that input in chemical form; respiration supports cellular work and energy ultimately disperses as heat. Energy does not cycle indefinitely as a reusable supply. Plants as well as decomposers respire, including in the light. Net oxygen depends on the balance of production and consumption.

A closed lid does not guarantee adequate nutrients, gas balance, light or long-term stability. Darkness removes the photosynthetic input while respiration can continue to draw on finite stores. Do not put animals in such a container or open or sniff a mouldy jar. This case asks for reasoning, not a home sealed-culture experiment.
:::
