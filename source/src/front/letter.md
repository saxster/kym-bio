## A letter to Kym

:::raw
<p class="salute">Dear Kym,</p>
:::

I should tell you something first: I am not a biologist. I don't know much biology at all. What I have tried to do is make the book I would have loved to read when I was your age: one that tells you who found each idea and how, that lets you try things in the kitchen, and that never just hands you a Latin word to learn by heart. Much of what is in here was new to me too. I also had help: I made this book with the help of AI, which searched out the research, wrote the chapters to my design, and then checked them. The explanations draw on the free courses and books that MIT and OpenStax share with the world, and on what researchers have learned about the ideas students most often get wrong. Every fact, date and number was checked against reliable sources, more than once, by checkers whose only job was to find mistakes, and every number was worked out again from scratch. Even so, a few mistakes may have slipped through. If something here doesn't match what you find somewhere else, or what your teacher says, or an experiment you do with your own hands, don't simply trust the book: tell me, and we will find out together which is right. And where the people who really know biology still argue about something, the book says so.

Go into the kitchen and hold a dry green *moong* bean in your palm. It feels like a tiny green pebble. It does not move. It does not breathe measurably. If you leave it in a closed glass jar on the shelf for five years, it sits there as still as a stone. Now think of a candle flame on a birthday cake. A flame moves, flickers, grows, takes in oxygen, gives out carbon dioxide and heat, and can even start new flames. By almost every everyday test, the flame looks alive and the dry bean looks dead.

Yet the flame is not alive, and the bean is. Drop that dry pebble of a bean into a bowl of tap water on a warm Mumbai evening, and by tomorrow night a white root has cracked the green coat and pushed out into the dark. Give it soil, air and sunlight, and it builds leaves, flowers and dozens of new beans. Somehow, inside that dry speck, a four-billion-year-old set of instructions was waiting—along with the microscopic machinery to read it.

Biology is the investigation of how non-living atoms—the very same carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus atoms you met in chemistry, obeying the very same laws of force and energy you met in physics—organise themselves into things that are alive. It asks how a hundred-tonne banyan tree at Horniman Circle builds almost its entire solid body out of thin air, how mangroves along Carter Road drink salty seawater that would kill a garden plant, why your *idli* batter rises overnight, how thirty trillion cells in your body work together without a boss, and how a four-letter chemical code inside a microscopic nucleus can build both a leopard in Sanjay Gandhi National Park and the brain you are reading this sentence with.

For a long time, biology in school has been taught backwards: as a giant dictionary of Greek and Latin names to memorise for a test. Memorising names in biology is like reading the last page of a detective novel—it spoils the mystery without letting you see how anyone solved the case. In this book we follow Richard Feynman's rule: see the thing first, understand how the mechanism works, and only then learn its name.

Almost every section starts with an idea that sounds completely sensible—and turns out to be wrong. Clever people once believed that rotting meat turns into maggots on its own, that trees eat soil to grow heavy, that your lungs are where respiration happens, and that bacteria mutate on purpose to beat antibiotics. Before each experiment, the book will ask you to **predict first**. Please really pick an answer before you read on. Discovering where your intuition gets tricked is how a detective learns to see.

The heroes of this story are real people: a Dutch cloth merchant who pointed a tiny glass bead at rainwater and tooth scrapings, a Flemish doctor who weighed a pot of dirt for five years, an Indian biochemist from Andhra Pradesh who discovered the rechargeable battery inside every living cell, a woman in London whose X-ray photograph revealed the twisted ladder of DNA, a botanist from Kerala who sweetened India's sugarcane by counting chromosomes, and a ten-year-old boy in Bombay whose curiosity about a yellow-throated sparrow launched conservation across India.

With love,

:::raw
<p class="sign">Amar</p>
