## How this book works

This book is one continuous detective investigation. It follows people across four centuries—from a physician in Florence covering jars of meat with fine veil, to a friar in Brno counting twenty-eight thousand pea plants, to scientists in Calcutta, Madras, Coimbatore and Mumbai working out how plants send electrical signals, how proteins fold, and how nerve cells fire. Each of them was asking the same two questions you will ask on every page: *how does this living machine work, and how do we know?*

There are four volumes and sixteen chapters, starting at Chapter 0 because every detective needs a toolkit before entering the crime scene. Each chapter opens with a biological paradox, follows the wrong turns and forensic experiments that cracked it, and builds the real mechanism from the ground up.

As you read, you will meet a few kinds of boxes. Each one has one job.

<dt>Margin notes</dt><dd>After you have seen how something works, its technical name is <mark class="term">marked like this</mark> and defined in the margin (along with where the Greek or Latin word came from). Every term goes into <a href="#glossary">Words worth knowing</a> at the back.</dd>
<dt>Predict first</dt><dd>A question with a few choices, placed right before the decisive experiment. Commit to one before you reveal the answer; your notebook remembers what you picked.</dd>
<dt>Careful</dt><dd>A common-sense idea ("the Red Herring") that tricks most people—and often tricked scientists for centuries—together with the exact evidence that disproves it.</dd>
<dt>Understand it</dt><dd>Knowing a biological word is not the same as understanding the mechanism. Four full-page explorers sit at the front of the book for structures you will return to again and again: <a href="#explore-scale">Powers of Ten in the Living World</a> (from a water molecule to the biosphere), <a href="#explore-cell">The 3D Cell & Nanocity Explorer</a> (interactive 3D animal, plant and bacterial cells), <a href="#explore-code">The Master Code: 3D DNA to Protein</a> (helix, codons and 3D protein folding), and <a href="#explore-map">the adventure map</a>.

Numbers in this book are real measured quantities. A millimetre (mm) is $10^{-3}\text{ m}$, athousandth of a metre. A micrometre ($\mu\text{m}$) is $10^{-6}\text{ m}$, a thousandth of a millimetre—the scale of cells and bacteria. A nanometre ($\text{nm}$) is $10^{-9}\text{ m}$, a thousandth of a micrometre—the scale of DNA, proteins and cell membranes.
