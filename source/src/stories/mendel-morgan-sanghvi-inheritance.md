In the summer of 1856, inside the walled courtyard of St Thomas’s Abbey in Brno (then Brünn in the Austrian Empire, now in the Czech Republic), an Augustinian friar in his thirties named **Johann Gregor Mendel** walked down rows of climbing garden peas (*Pisum sativum*) holding fine forceps and a camel-hair paintbrush. Before a pea flower could open and self-pollinate, **Mendel** pried apart the green keel petals, snipped off the immature anthers, and bagged the bud against passing bees. Days later, he brushed the stigma with pollen from a chosen partner plant and logged the cross.

### Part 1 · Johann Gregor Mendel's Brno ledger and Reginald Punnett's Cambridge grid (1856–1905)

Between 1851 and 1853, Abbot Cyril Napp had sent **Mendel** to the University of Vienna, where he studied experimental physics under Christian Doppler and combinatorial mathematics under Andreas von Ettingshausen. There he learned that hidden natural laws emerge only when an experimenter isolates one variable at a time and counts enough trials for random statistical noise to average out.

Nineteenth-century scientists had no agreed inheritance mechanism. A tempting **blending** model suggested that parental traits supposedly mixed like red and white paint, producing pink offspring that could never be unmixed. To investigate how characters reappear across generations, **Mendel** spent two years (1854–1856) verifying twenty-two pure-breeding pea lines across seven sharp characters: seed shape (round versus wrinkled), cotyledon colour (yellow versus green), flower colour (purple versus white), pod shape (inflated versus constricted), unripe pod colour (green versus yellow), flower position (axial versus terminal), and stem length (tall `1.8–2.1 m` versus dwarf `0.3–0.45 m`).

Between 1856 and 1863, across a `35 m × 7 m` monastery plot, **Mendel** cultivated roughly `28,000` pea plants and scored **`19,959` second-generation (`F₂`) outcomes**. Crossing pure round (`RR`) with pure wrinkled (`rr`) peas yielded `100%` round first-generation (`F₁`) hybrids (`Rr`). When those `F₁` plants self-pollinated, wrinkled seeds returned unblemished in `F₂`: out of `7,324` seeds, **`5,474` were round and `1,850` were wrinkled (`2.96 : 1`)**. Cotyledon colour split **`6,022` yellow to `2,001` green (`3.01 : 1`)**, and flower colour split **`705` purple to `224` white (`3.15 : 1`)**. Across all seven characters combined, his ledger totalled **`14,949` dominant to `5,010` recessive outcomes (`2.98 : 1`)**, converging on **`3 : 1`**.

Crucially, **Mendel** raised a third generation (`F₃`) from `565` round `F₂` seeds: **`193` plants** bred true (`RR`) while **`372` plants** segregated `3 : 1` again (`Rr`), proving the hidden **`1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr`** genotype ratio (`1 : 1.93`). His **test crosses** (`Rr × rr`) likewise confirmed a **`1 : 1`** split.

Published in 1866, the paper attracted little attention. Mendel also investigated hawkweed (*Hieracium*), encouraged by Carl Nägeli. Many hawkweeds form seeds asexually (**apomixis**), which made these experiments difficult to interpret; this does not explain by itself why the pea paper was overlooked. After Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak rediscovered Mendel’s laws in 1900, Cambridge biologist **William Bateson** coined **genetics**, **allele**, **homozygote**, and **heterozygote**. Around 1905, working with **William Bateson**, **Reginald Punnett** introduced the **`2 × 2` Punnett square**, turning Mendel’s binomial algebra (`¼ RR + ½ Rr + ¼ rr`) into a visual grid where maternal and paternal haploid gametes (`n`) combine into diploid zygotes (`2n`).

### Part 2 · Nettie Stevens's mealworm chromosomes and Thomas Hunt Morgan's white-eyed fly (1905–1910)

Where inside the cell did Mendel’s alleles sit? In 1902–1903, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri noted that homologous chromosomes segregate during **meiosis** just like alleles. In 1905 at Bryn Mawr College, cytologist **Nettie Stevens** provided decisive proof in the mealworm beetle (*Tenebrio molitor*): females carried `20` matched chromosomes (`XX`), producing uniform `X` eggs, whereas males carried `19` large chromosomes and one tiny partner (`XY`), producing `50%` `X`-sperm and `50%` `Y`-sperm. Sex determination was a Mendelian `1 : 1` chromosome cross.

At Columbia University in New York, embryologist **Thomas Hunt Morgan** remained sceptical until early 1910, when he spotted a single **white-eyed male fruit fly** (*Drosophila melanogaster*) among thousands of red-eyed flies in his banana-bottle **Fly Room**. Crossing that white-eyed male to red-eyed sisters produced `1,237` red-eyed `F₁` offspring and **three white-eyed males**, according to Morgan’s original report. An idealised cross to a homozygous red-eyed female predicts all red-eyed offspring, but that model should not erase the three exceptions in his notebook. In the `F₂` generation (`3,470` red : `782` white), **every single white-eyed fly was male (`XʷY`)**, whereas test-crossing the white-eyed father to `F₁` carrier daughters produced both white-eyed females (`XʷXʷ`) and males (`XʷY`). That proved the eye-colour gene sat physically on the **`X` chromosome**, while the **`Y` chromosome** lacked that locus—earning **Thomas Hunt Morgan** the 1933 Nobel Prize.

### Part 3 · L. D. Sanghvi and human genetic pedigrees in Mumbai (1948–1970)

After studying mathematics in Mumbai and completing his doctorate at Columbia University under L. C. Dunn and Theodosius Dobzhansky, Indian geneticist **L. D. Sanghvi** (1920–1985) founded the Human Variation Unit at the Indian Cancer Research Centre (now ACTREC, Tata Memorial Centre) in Parel, Mumbai.

From the late 1940s through the 1970s, **L. D. Sanghvi** and his colleagues mapped **`ABO` blood-group alleles (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`)**, **`Rh` antigens**, **X-linked red–green colour vision (`Xᴮ/Xᵇ`)**, **G6PD deficiency**, and autosomal recessive haemoglobin variants (**sickle-cell `Hbˢ`** and **beta-thalassaemia**) across communities in Mumbai, Maharashtra, and Gujarat. By combining family **pedigree charts** with carrier screening, **L. D. Sanghvi** showed how endogamous marriage patterns concentrate rare recessive alleles (`aa`) and how Punnett probabilities empower genetic counselling in Indian hospitals.

### What to remember

- **Johann Gregor Mendel (1866)** proved across `19,959` `F₂` pea outcomes (`2.98 : 1`) and `F₃` progeny tests (`1 RR : 2 Rr`) that alleles are discrete packets that do not blend.
- **William Bateson** and **Reginald Punnett (1905)** established core genetics terminology (*allele*, *homozygous*, *heterozygous*) and the `2 × 2` **Punnett square**.
- **Nettie Stevens (1905)** and **Thomas Hunt Morgan (1910)** proved that `XX`/`XY` sex determination (`1 : 1`) and **X-linked recessive inheritance** (`XᵇY`, `XʰY`) trace to specific chromosomes.
- **L. D. Sanghvi** pioneered `ABO` codominance (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`), X-linked screening, and autosomal carrier pedigree analysis in Mumbai.
