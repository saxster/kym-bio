:::scene Madras, protein structure work, 1954–1963

A molecular model can look persuasive and still be wrong. A line on paper can cross another line without complaint. Atoms in a proposed structure cannot simply occupy the same space. How do you turn that difference into a test that another researcher can use?

Gopalasamudram Narayana Ramachandran approached biological structures with a physicist's training. Born in Ernakulam in 1922, he worked with C. V. Raman at the Indian Institute of Science and later studied at Cambridge. In 1952 he became head of physics at the University of Madras. The city is now called Chennai. Here a physics department became an important centre for investigating the molecules of life.

The material at the heart of one problem was collagen. It helps support tissues such as skin and tendons. Calling it a protein did not specify how its long chains were arranged. Nor could an attractive drawing settle that arrangement. A model had to agree with evidence from X-ray diffraction and with what was known about chemical structures.

Ramachandran and Gopinath Kartha proposed a triple-helical structure in a paper published in *Nature* in August 1954. Three chains, rather than a single chain folded alone, were central to their proposal. In 1955, their revised model coiled the chains around a common axis, rather than keeping the individual helix axes parallel. Further refinements included contributions from other research groups. The familiar short statement that “Ramachandran solved collagen in 1954” compresses a developing investigation into one person's finished answer. Keeping Kartha and the later work visible gives a truer account.

One difficulty opened a broader question. A protein contains repeated backbone groups joined through carbon atoms. How freely can neighbouring groups turn? If every turn were an independent choice, a model-maker could propose a huge variety of arrangements. Chemical geometry removes many of them before the rest of a protein is even considered.

A peptide group is relatively planar. Important rotations are possible around neighbouring backbone bonds, but a rotation moves several atoms together. Turning one part can bring its atoms uncomfortably close to another part. The local arrangement must therefore be examined as a pair of turns, with the attached groups included, rather than as isolated hinges with nothing around them.

In their 1963 paper, Ramachandran, C. Ramakrishnan and V. Sasisekharan described a way to represent those turns and calculate which combinations avoided short contacts. Known small-molecule and peptide structures supplied information about dimensions and plausible nonbonded distances. The researchers considered more than one set of limiting distances, so the boundary between acceptable and crowded was tied to stated assumptions rather than drawn by taste.

The calculation produced regions of possible arrangements and regions ruled out by crowding. Known peptide and polypeptide structures could then be compared with the permitted regions. That connection was useful in both directions: the dimensions constrained a proposed structure, and structural observations tested how well the constraints described actual molecules.

The modern map bears Ramachandran's name, but the original paper carries all three authors' names. It also gives the University of Madras affiliation and a submission date in December 1962. Its publication year, 1963, marks when the paper appeared, not an invented afternoon on which all the calculations happened.

There is a powerful limit to this achievement. Local turns that avoid clashes do not determine a unique full protein fold. Distant parts of a chain can still meet, attract, repel or crowd one another. Water and other surroundings also matter. A map of possible turns helps rule out bad proposals; it does not provide every missing coordinate of the right one.

That is the historical question worth carrying back to Chapter 4. When a model looks convincing, ask what would make it impossible. The active site in the next section must satisfy spatial restrictions and chemical interactions together. A neat pocket drawn around a substrate is only a beginning.
