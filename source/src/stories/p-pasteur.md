:::scene Paris, 1859

Louis Pasteur was trained not as a doctor, but as a chemist and crystallographer. At twenty-five he noticed that crystals of tartaric acid made by living wine yeast were all "left-handed" in how they twisted polarised light, whereas tartaric acid made in a chemical factory was an equal mix of left- and right-handed crystals. That clue convinced him that microscopic living cells carry out specific chemical reactions that non-living solutions do not.

When French wine and beer makers asked him why vats sometimes turned sour into vinegar or lactic acid instead of alcohol, Pasteur looked through his microscope and saw that healthy vats contained budding round yeast cells, whereas sour vats were overrun by tiny rod-shaped bacteria. Heating the wine gently to $55\text{–}60^\circ\text{C}$ for a few minutes killed the spoilage bacteria without ruining the flavour—the process the world still calls **pasteurisation**.

To silence critics who claimed those bacteria arose spontaneously out of the liquid, Pasteur designed his famous **swan-neck flasks** in 1859. The long S-shaped glass neck stayed completely open to room air, yet gravity trapped dust and bacterial spores in the low bend of the tube. Several of Pasteur's original sealed and swan-neck flasks still sit at the Institut Pasteur in Paris today, crystal clear more than 160 years later.
