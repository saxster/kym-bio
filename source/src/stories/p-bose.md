:::scene Presidency College, Calcutta, 1901

If you have read Book 2 (*The Moving World*, Chapter 13), you already met Jagadish Chandra Bose ringing a bell and firing a pistol across the Presidency College hall in Calcutta in 1895 using invisible $5\text{ mm}$ electromagnetic waves. Yet around 1900, Bose noticed something strange: his delicate metal wave-detectors ("coherers") grew sluggish after repeated stimulation and recovered after a rest—almost like a tired muscle!

Curious whether living plant tissues and animal nerves shared the same physical laws of electrical response, Bose built the **crescograph**: a clockwork lever system using two smoked-glass plates and fine magnetic mirrors that magnified the tiny elongation of a growing plant tip by $10,000\times$, allowing him to measure growth over a few seconds instead of days. Using fine galvanometers on *Mimosa pudica* (*chui-mui*, the touch-me-not plant) and *Desmodium gyrans* (the telegraph plant), Bose showed that plants transmit measurable **electrical action potentials** along their vascular bundles when pinched or heated, and that these signals slow down under cold or chloroform vapour and stop permanently when poisoned. Today, plant neurobiologists studying glutamate and calcium waves in wounded leaves trace their field directly to Bose's Calcutta bench.
