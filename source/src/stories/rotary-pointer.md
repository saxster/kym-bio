:::scene Yokohama, Japan, a result published in 1997

How would you watch something turn when the moving part is too small to see clearly? A mark on its shaft would be too small as well. One possible solution is to attach a longer pointer and watch the pointer's changing direction.

In a paper published in Nature on 20 March 1997, Hiroyuki Noji, Ryohei Yasuda, Masasuke Yoshida and Kazuhiko Kinosita Jr reported using that principle with a protein machine. The authors' laboratories were at the Tokyo Institute of Technology and Keio University, both listed in Yokohama. Their result concerned a component of the machinery that links ion movement and ATP chemistry. It gave an otherwise invisible proposal a visible consequence.

The question had a history. Peter Mitchell's 1961 chemiosmotic proposal connected energy-releasing electron transfers to ATP formation through a membrane ion gradient. That supplied an energy route, but it did not reveal every moving part of the ATP-making enzyme. Knowing what powers a machine and knowing how its parts move are different questions.

Paul Boyer and colleagues developed a mechanism in which changes in the enzyme's catalytic sites alter how they bind and release substrates and ATP. Rotation of an internal shaft could coordinate those shape changes. John Walker and colleagues investigated molecular structure, providing a different kind of evidence about the arrangement of the parts. Biochemistry and structure could support a rotary mechanism without being a direct observation of turning.

#### Step 1: Make the motion report itself

The enzyme has a membrane-associated part and a protruding catalytic part called F₁. Inside F₁, a central component called the gamma subunit forms a shaft within surrounding protein units. If that shaft rotates relative to its surroundings, something attached to it should change direction too.

Noji and his collaborators attached a fluorescent actin filament to the gamma subunit. Actin is a protein that can form filaments; fluorescence made the attached marker visible. The filament served as a longer pointer. Observing its changing orientation supplied evidence about the movement of the part to which it was attached. The pointer did not make the shaft itself grow large enough to inspect with ordinary sight.

#### Step 2: Ask what supplied the energy

Here is the detail that a quick retelling often loses. The researchers supplied **ATP** to an isolated F₁ preparation. The turning they observed was driven by ATP breakdown. The paper's title calls the protein F₁-ATPase: an enzyme breaking ATP down.

This was the reverse energy direction from the mitochondrial synthesis account in Chapter 6. There, an ion gradient supports ion return through the complete membrane-linked enzyme, driving rotation and changes that make ATP available from ADP and phosphate. In the rotation experiment, supplied ATP supported turning in the isolated catalytic component. Reversible coupling makes both directions scientifically connected, but one experiment should not be described as though it directly filmed the other.

#### Step 3: Keep the conclusion as precise as the observation

The fluorescent pointer demonstrated motion consistent with a rotary shaft. It strengthened the mechanism by making a predicted physical action observable. It did not by itself measure every proton's route through a mitochondrial membrane, establish one speed for all ATP synthases or calculate the ATP yield of every cell.

The distinction is useful far beyond this protein. A detector records something specific: light from a marker, a gas volume, a change in mass. The explanation connects that signal to a less directly visible event. Good evidence depends on that connection being stated, including what was supplied to the apparatus and which direction the process ran.

Later in 1997, Boyer and Walker jointly received half of the Chemistry Nobel Prize for work elucidating ATP synthesis. Jens Skou received the other half for discovery of a different ion-transporting enzyme. The rotation paper and the prize recognise connected contributions; neither is a reason to replace the collaborators with a story about one lone discoverer.

Return to Chapter 6's membrane plate and trace its two arrows: H⁺ pumped out, then returning through ATP synthase. The pointer experiment helps explain why rotation belongs in that mechanism. Its ATP-driven reverse direction also gives you a question to carry to any moving apparatus: what went in, what moved, and what does the observation actually establish?
