:::scene Khetwadi & Apollo Street, Bombay, 1906

In 1906, a ten-year-old orphan named Salim Moizuddin Abdul Ali, growing up in a big family house in Khetwadi in central Bombay, shot a sparrow with his toy airgun. When he picked it up, he noticed something odd: although it looked like an ordinary house sparrow, it had a bright yellow patch on its throat.

His uncle, Amiruddin Tyabji, wrote a note of introduction and walked young Salim over to the rooms of the **Bombay Natural History Society (BNHS)**, where the honorary secretary, W. S. Millard, took the boy gently by the shoulder, identified the bird as a Yellow-throated Sparrow (*Gymnoris xanthocollis*), and opened cabinet after cabinet of Indian bird skins, teaching him how to observe, measure and record birds in the field without shooting them.

Thirty years later, watching Baya Weavers (*Ploceus philippinus*) weaving their hanging gourd-shaped nests on palm trees near Kihim across Mumbai harbour, Salim Ali carried out one of the world's first quantitative studies of tropical bird behaviour: numbering nests and banding individual birds to prove that the male weaves the nest frame and only when a female inspects and approves the engineering does he finish the egg chamber and move on to weave another! His ten-volume *Handbook of the Birds of India and Pakistan* and *The Book of Indian Birds* remain the foundation of Indian ecology.
