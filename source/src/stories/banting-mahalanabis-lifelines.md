:::scene Toronto, summer 1921 to January 1922, and Kolkata to Bongaon, 1959–1971

Walk into a hospital ward in 1920 where children with severe **Type 1 diabetes** lay waiting, or into a border refugee camp in 1971 during a monsoon **cholera** outbreak. At first glance, the two emergencies look like opposites. In untreated Type 1 diabetes, the blood plasma is flooded with dissolved glucose (`15–30 mmol/L`) that the body's own muscle and liver cells cannot take in rapidly enough, while liters of sweet urine drain away and the child wastes to skin and bone. In cholera, a comma-shaped bacterium in the gut drains so much water and salt out of the bloodstream within hours that the patient's radial pulse vanishes and the blood turns thick and dark.

Yet both emergencies were solved by the same kind of biological detective work: asking *which specific protein signal or membrane carrier is missing or still working*, and testing that mechanism quantitatively before claiming a cure.

### Part 1 · Toronto, 1921–1922: rescuing the islet messenger from trypsin

Since 1889, when Oskar Minkowski and Joseph von Mering removed the pancreas from a dog and noticed flies swarming around the animal's sugar-laden urine, physiologists had suspected that the tiny clusters of pale cells scattered through the pancreas—the **islets of Langerhans**—secreted an internal chemical messenger that controlled blood sugar. The trouble was that the rest of the pancreas is an exocrine factory packed with powerful digestive proteases such as **trypsinogen / trypsin** (which you met in Chapter 7). Whenever researchers ground up a whole pancreas to extract the hypothetical islet hormone, the pancreas's own proteases digested the protein hormone before anyone could test it.

In October 1920, a young Canadian surgeon and war veteran named **Frederick Banting** sat up late preparing a lecture for medical students and read a pathology report about a patient whose pancreatic duct had been blocked by a gallstone: the enzyme-making acinar cells had shrivelled away, while the islets of Langerhans remained intact. Banting scribbled a three-sentence laboratory plan in his notebook: tie off the pancreatic ducts in dogs for several weeks until the trypsin-making tissue degenerates, then extract the surviving islet cells in cold saline.

In May 1921, physiology professor **J. J. R. Macleod** at the University of Toronto gave Banting a small upstairs laboratory, surgical facilities, and a twenty-two-year-old student assistant named **Charles Best**, who had just finished his bachelor's degree in physiology and biochemistry and knew how to run quantitative blood-glucose and urinary-sugar assays. Through a stifling Toronto summer, **Frederick Banting** and **Charles Best** prepared pancreatic extracts and tested whether each batch lowered the blood glucose of diabetic dogs. They soon discovered that they did not even need to wait weeks for tied ducts to degenerate: extracting fetal or adult cow pancreases from the Toronto slaughterhouse with **acidified alcohol** inactivated the digestive proteases while leaving the hormone intact.

An extract that lowered blood sugar in a dog, however, was still too full of foreign beef proteins to inject safely into a human child. In December 1921, Macleod brought the biochemist **James Collip** into the team. Collip worked out a fractional alcohol precipitation protocol: at just the right alcohol concentration, most contaminating pancreatic proteins precipitated out while the active hormone stayed in solution; raising the alcohol level further precipitated the purified hormone itself.

On 11 January 1922, at Toronto General Hospital, fourteen-year-old **Leonard Thompson**—who weighed only 29.5 kg (`65 lb`), smelled of acetone, and was slipping toward diabetic coma—received an initial injection of Banting and Best's less pure extract. It lowered his blood sugar slightly, by about a quarter, but caused a sterile abscess at the injection site because of remaining protein impurities. Twelve days later, on **23 January 1922**, **Leonard Thompson** received **James Collip**'s purified pancreatic extract—now named **insulin**. Within twenty-four hours, Thompson's blood glucose dropped from `520 mg/dL` (`28.9 mmol/L`) to `120 mg/dL` (`6.7 mmol/L`), his daily urinary sugar loss plummeted from more than `100 g` to near zero, and he began regaining weight and strength.

Notice what that two-step January 1922 trial teaches about scientific evidence. A crude extract can contain a real biological signal and still fail clinically until biochemical purification separates the active molecule from harmful contaminants. And notice why insulin had to be **injected** under the skin rather than swallowed as a pill: insulin is a folded protein (two polypeptide chains linked by disulfide bridges, sequenced by Frederick Sanger in the 1950s). Swallowing insulin would expose it to the very stomach pepsin and intestinal trypsin that Banting, Best and Collip had worked so hard to avoid.

### Part 2 · Kolkata (1959) and Bongaon (1971): the intact door that cholera forgot to lock

Thirty-seven years after Toronto, in a quiet pathology laboratory at Nilratan Sircar Medical College in Calcutta (now Kolkata), **Sambhu Nath De** confronted an equally stubborn medical misconception. For decades, standard textbooks taught that *Vibrio cholerae* killed patients by physically stripping off the epithelial lining of the small intestine so blood plasma simply leaked through raw, denuded walls.

**Sambhu Nath De** tested that claim directly. He grew *Vibrio cholerae* in liquid culture, passed the broth through a bacteria-tight filter so **zero living bacteria** remained in the liquid, and injected that sterile cell-free filtrate into tied-off segments of rabbit small intestine (**ligated ileal loops**). Within hours, the loops ballooned with clear, watery fluid—yet when De sectioned and stained the intestinal wall under the microscope, the **absorptive epithelium and its brush border were completely intact**. In his 1959 *Nature* paper, De proved that cholera is driven not by mechanical tearing of the gut wall, but by a secreted bacterial protein—**cholera enterotoxin**—that switches the intact intestinal epithelial cells into actively pumping **chloride ions (`Cl⁻`)** out into the gut lumen. Because chloride and sodium ions pour into the lumen, the **water potential** of the intestinal fluid drops below that of the cells and blood plasma, and **osmosis** drags litres of water out of the bloodstream into the gut.

De's discovery contained a hidden clinical lifeline. If the small-intestinal epithelium is still intact during cholera, then the brush-border **`Na⁺`–glucose cotransporter** (which you met in Chapter 7 Section 7.4) is still sitting undamaged in the cell membrane. Drinking plain water during cholera fails because the low water potential of the chloride-rich gut lumen keeps pulling water outward, and drinking plain salt water fails because extra `NaCl` without glucose is not absorbed rapidly enough and worsens the osmotic draw. But what if a patient drinks clean water containing **both sodium chloride and glucose in matched molar proportions**? Every time a brush-border carrier protein binds one glucose molecule and two `Na⁺` ions, it pulls them together into the epithelial cell, raising the solute concentration inside the cell and tissue fluid so **water follows them back into the bloodstream by osmosis**.

In 1968, clinical researchers in Dhaka and Kolkata—including David Nalin, Richard Cash, Norbert Hirschhorn, and Nathaniel Pierce—measured intake and output in hospitalised cholera patients and showed that an oral glucose-electrolyte drink reduced intravenous fluid needs by up to 80 percent. Most senior health officials, however, still insisted that oral rehydration could work only under careful hospital supervision alongside intravenous drips.

Then came the monsoon of **1971**. During the Bangladesh Liberation War, millions of exhausted refugees crossed the border into West Bengal. At **Bongaon**, a frontier town about eighty kilometres north-east of Kolkata, cholera broke out in overcrowded camps where **Dilip Mahalanabis**, a thirty-six-year-old paediatrician from the Johns Hopkins Center for Medical Research and Training in Calcutta, was running a makeshift two-room field hospital. Intravenous saline bottles ran out within days, and patients were dying on the floor at a case-fatality rate approaching **30 percent**.

**Dilip Mahalanabis** made a bold, disciplined decision grounded in the physiology of the `Na⁺`–glucose cotransporter. In the library of the Calcutta research centre, he set technicians to weighing out dry packets of **22 g glucose, 3.5 g sodium chloride (table salt), and 2.5 g sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda)** per litre of clean boiled water, mixed the solution in clean plastic drums at **Bongaon**, and trained mothers, grandmothers, and brothers to spoon and cup the solution continuously into dehydrated patients' mouths as fast as fluid was lost. Over eight weeks in 1971, **3,703 severe cholera patients**—including more than a thousand children—were treated with this **oral rehydration solution (ORS)** in the **Bongaon** tents. The case-fatality rate fell from **~30 percent to 3.6 percent**, at a cost of a few paise per patient.

Together, Toronto 1922 and Kolkata–Bongaon 1959–1971 show why cellular mechanisms matter in real life. Knowing that **insulin** unlocks liver and muscle glucose uptake saved **Leonard Thompson**; knowing that **cholera toxin** leaves the intestinal **`Na⁺`–glucose cotransporter** intact allowed **Sambhu Nath De** and **Dilip Mahalanabis** to turn a cup of clean water, a pinch of salt, and a spoonful of sugar into one of the most effective medical interventions in human history.
