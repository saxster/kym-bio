:::scene Baltimore, a water-channel paper published in 1992

A red blood cell has a boundary that keeps many dissolved substances apart. Water nevertheless passes across it rapidly. Knowing the boundary's name does not explain the passage. A membrane is a structure, and a structure can contain several different routes.

Peter Agre's route into that problem began with a protein that his group had not set out to discover. While studying red blood cell membrane proteins, they found a component whose function was unclear. Its presence was a fact. Calling it a protein was another fact. Neither told them what it did.

A useful scientific question has to connect an observation with a comparison that might go differently. Does this component merely accompany water-permeable membranes, or does it help make them permeable? Removing or adding the suspected component could distinguish those possibilities.

Agre's account credits a conversation with the physiologist John Parker in 1991. Parker suggested that the mysterious protein might be a water channel. That suggestion did not settle its function. It supplied a hypothesis that could be tested. There was an important change in the question: from “What is this?” to “What difference would its presence make?”

Gregory Preston, working with Agre and their collaborator William Guggino, used frog egg cells. These cells were useful because their normal water permeability was relatively low. The team arranged for some eggs to produce the suspected protein. Control eggs did not receive the instructions for making it. The two groups could then face the same dilute surroundings.

The eggs making the protein swelled much faster. The controls changed little during the comparison. Agre dates the first experiment to October 1991; Preston and colleagues' paper appeared in *Science* in 1992. Keeping those dates separate avoids turning the year on a paper into an invented date for everything that led to it.

What did the comparison establish? Adding the protein greatly increased the membrane's water permeability. The result connected a molecular component with a physical function. It went beyond observing that some natural cells happened to gain water faster than others, because those cells might differ in many ways. Here the suspected component was deliberately changed.

There are limits to the conclusion. Low permeability is not zero permeability. A control egg that changes little over a short comparison has not been shown to have an absolutely waterproof membrane. And faster water movement is not evidence for an ATP-driven water pump. The dilute surroundings already favour net water entry; the protein provides a route through the boundary.

The name **aquaporin** means water pore. It is a useful reminder of the route, provided you keep the mechanism beside the name. A typical water-specific aquaporin allows water passage while excluding ions. Other membrane proteins can have other selectivities. A cell membrane is therefore a collection of chemically different pathways, not one coarse net that admits every sufficiently small object.

Chapter 3 uses this experiment for two purposes. First, it gives the membrane's “partial permeability” an actual molecular component. Second, it shows what a control does. Both sets of eggs encounter dilute water, but only one makes the added protein. The difference in response becomes evidence about that protein's role.

On the chapter's pressure balance, the channel cannot choose a direction. Water potential determines the net transfer; the pathway affects how readily it can happen. A convincing explanation needs both the route and the conditions on its two sides.
