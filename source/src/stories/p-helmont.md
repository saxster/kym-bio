:::scene An experiment published after its author's death, 1648

A tree's roots disappear into soil. It is tempting to picture that soil being eaten and rebuilt as trunk and branches. A balance offers a test: compare the tree's gain with the soil's loss. Jan Baptista van Helmont reported doing just that over five years.

His account describes 200 pounds of oven-dried earth in an earthen pot and a willow shoot weighing 5 pounds. He supplied rainwater or distilled water. A perforated tin-plated iron lid was intended to keep airborne dust from mixing with the soil. It did not seal the plant away from air. These are the reported historical quantities; their exact conversion into modern kilograms is uncertain.

After five years, he reported a tree weighing 169 pounds and about 3 ounces. The soil, dried again, had lost about 2 ounces. On those reported figures, a large tree gain accompanied a small soil loss. The comparison challenged the idea that the whole gain was simply soil transferred into the tree.

But the ledger had gaps. He had not recorded the masses of fallen leaves or all the water supplied. Gases entering and leaving were unmeasured. The soil was dried before weighing; the tree was not established to be dry at either weighing. The unrecorded water content prevents treating the whole gain as new dry wood.

Van Helmont concluded that the new wood, bark and roots came from water alone. Water really does contribute material, but that conclusion left out another source. Modern evidence shows that carbon dioxide supplies the carbon in plant carbohydrates. Soil still supplies essential mineral nutrients, and roots obtain water as well as anchoring the plant. A small mineral contribution can be indispensable without accounting for most of the plant's dry organic material.

Van Helmont also investigated gases and introduced the word gas. His gas sylvestre was later identified as carbon dioxide. Recognising a gas in one investigation did not by itself establish its role in building plant material. That connection required further evidence, rather than a cleverer reading of the willow's final weight.

The publication date is another entry to keep straight. Van Helmont died in 1644. His son, Franciscus Mercurius, issued the collection *Ortus medicinae* in 1648. That is when the account appeared, not the year the five-year experiment began. The sources used here do not establish its exact starting and finishing dates.

Carry the unfinished ledger into Chapter 5. A balance can show that an explanation is missing something without identifying every missing input. The next investigators must ask what leaves exchange with air, when light matters and how to follow particular atoms into plant material.
