:::scene Padua, London and Frankfurt, 1600–1628

How do you follow a river when its smallest channels are invisible to the naked eye? For more than a thousand years, learned physicians inherited a persuasive picture from the ancient Greek physician Galen. In that account, food from the gut was turned into dark nutritive blood by the liver and sent drifting outward through the veins to nourish the tissues, while a separate supply of bright vital blood and spirit moved through the arteries. Most of the blood was thought to be consumed where it arrived, rather than returning to start the journey again.

It is tempting to mock that older view by claiming Galen thought the liver manufactured a person's entire body weight in fresh blood every hour. He did not: in Galen's system, blood moved much more slowly, ebbing and seeping to match tissue nourishment. When William Harvey challenged the consumption model, he did not win by inventing a silly version of his predecessors. He combined earlier anatomical discoveries, deliberate underestimate arithmetic and simple mechanical tests on living vessels.

#### Step 1: Inherit the valves and the lung crossing

Harvey did not work in isolation. Around 1242, the Arab physician Ibn al-Nafis had already argued in his commentary on Avicenna that blood cannot pass through invisible pores in the thick septum between the heart's right and left ventricles; instead, blood from the right ventricle must travel through the lungs to reach the left chambers. In sixteenth-century Italy, Realdo Colombo (1559) likewise described the pulmonary transit from anatomical and vivisection observations.

When Harvey studied medicine at the University of Padua—graduating in 1602—his anatomy teacher Hieronymus Fabricius of Aquapendente showed him the cup-shaped pockets lining the insides of veins, which Fabricius published in *De Venarum Ostiolis* (1603). Fabricius thought those venous valves merely slowed blood down to prevent it pooling in the limbs. Harvey noticed something sharper: the pockets all point **towards the heart**, never away from it.

 Harvey began presenting his new lectures on the movement of the heart and blood to the College of Physicians in London from 1616. Twelve years later, his concise Latin book *Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus* (*De Motu Cordis*) was printed in **Frankfurt** in 1628 by the English bookseller William Fitzer. Studying in Padua, lecturing in London and publishing in Frankfurt were distinct stages of a long investigation.

#### Step 2: Choose an underestimate that no critic can brush aside

In Chapter IX of *De Motu Cordis*, Harvey asked a quantitative question: how much blood does the left ventricle eject into the aorta over half an hour?

Instead of claiming an exact universal stroke volume, he deliberately chose numbers **far smaller** than the heart actually holds, so that even a sceptic would have to grant the premise. Suppose, he wrote, the left ventricle holds about two ounces of blood, and ejects only a quarter, a fifth, or a sixth of that—say half an ounce (four drachms), three drachms, or even just **one drachm** (about 3.9 grams in apothecaries' weight) at each beat.

In half an hour the heart beats more than a thousand times (and in some people two, three or four thousand times). Multiply even the tiniest assumed ejection—one drachm per beat—by 1,000 beats: that is 1,000 drachms, or **10 pounds 5 ounces** Troy (~3.9 kilograms) of blood pumped out of the left ventricle in thirty minutes. If the heart ejects half an ounce (4 drachms) per beat over 1,000 beats, the half-hour total is **41 pounds 8 ounces** Troy (~15.5 kilograms).

That conservative lower bound was already larger than the entire quantity of blood contained in the body, and far more than any meal or drink could supply in half an hour. Arithmetic alone could not show how arteries and veins connect, nor could it prove that flow is steady. What it **ruled out** was one-way production and complete tissue consumption: unless the vessels burst, the same blood had to return to the heart and be pumped around again.

#### Step 3: Test direction with ligatures and a finger on a forearm vein

If blood circulates, which vessels carry it outward and which bring it back? In Chapters X and XI, Harvey described seventeenth-century surgical and ligature observations—pinching the great vessels in a live snake and tying bandages of different tightness around a human arm (historical procedures that are never to be repeated on animals or with tight bands at home).

When he compressed a snake's vena cava just before it entered the heart, the space between his forceps and the heart quickly emptied. When he compressed the artery just beyond the heart, the vessel between the heart and the pinch swelled up purple. In a human arm, a **very tight** ligature blocked the deep arteries as well as the veins: the hand grew pale and cool, and no pulse beat below the band. Loosening the band to **medium** tightness let high-pressure arterial blood push past into the hand while still blocking the lower-pressure surface veins: now the hand flushed warm and the veins below the band swelled up—never the veins above it.

Then, in Chapter XIII (illustrated by four famous engravings of a forearm), Harvey described a three-part test on a prominent surface vein of a bound arm:

1. Pressing one fingertip down on a swollen forearm vein just below a valve node H (near the hand) and sliding a second finger **upward** (towards the shoulder) past the next valve O emptied the vein segment between H and O, which stayed flat because valve O blocked backward flow from above.
2. Pushing against the swollen vein above O toward the wrist made the valve puff out as a small knot, refusing to let blood pass toward the hand.
3. Lifting the lower finger at H allowed blood to refill the empty segment immediately from the hand side, **towards the heart**.

#### Step 4: Leave the unseen bridge for the microscope

Even after arithmetic, ligatures and valves converged on a closed loop, one link remained unseen. Without a powerful microscope, Harvey could not see the tiny vessels connecting the finest arteries to the finest veins in the tissues; he had to infer passage through invisible tissue spaces or pores. Nor did anyone in 1628 know about **oxygen**, which was identified only in the 1770s.

In 1661—four years after Harvey's death—the Italian physician Marcello Malpighi turned a microscope onto the thin, translucent lung of a living frog (*De Pulmonibus*). There he saw blood remain inside branching, hair-thin walled channels—**capillaries**—as it crossed from the smallest arteries into the smallest veins.

Return to Chapter 8 with Harvey's toolkit in mind. Deliberate lower-bound arithmetic can rule out an impossible supply claim; mechanical experiments with valves and pressure establish direction; and direct microscopy later fills in the structural bridge that calculation had predicted.
