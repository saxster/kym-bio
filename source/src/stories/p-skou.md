:::scene Aarhus, a crab nerve membrane study, 1957

A living nerve cell contains different concentrations of sodium and potassium from the fluid around it. A membrane can slow the leakage that would erase those differences. Slowing leakage, however, leaves an accounting problem. If ions continue to leak, what maintains the distribution?

The answer requires more than calling the membrane a boundary. A boundary can contain both passive routes and machinery coupled to a chemical energy supply. Distinguishing those functions was a problem for experiments.

Jens Christian Skou investigated enzymes in nerve membranes from crabs. Working with membrane preparations allowed him to ask a chemical question: which conditions changed the rate at which ATP was broken down? ATP breakdown could be measured, even when a whole living cell's behaviour was much harder to disentangle.

His 1957 paper connected the activity of a membrane enzyme with the ions in the mixture. Sodium and potassium affected that activity. This made the enzyme a candidate for involvement in active ion transport. Its reaction was associated with the same kinds of ions whose distributions living nerve cells maintained.

The word **candidate** matters in following the evidence. Detecting ATP breakdown in a membrane preparation did not display a complete pumping cycle. A broken-cell chemical assay was not a miniature film of an intact nerve. The experimental response connected pieces of the problem and helped guide the identification of the transport machinery.

Later work established the sodium–potassium pump's role and mechanism. The protein couples a cycle of structural changes to ATP chemistry and ion movement. The maintained ion distributions have consequences for functions such as nerve signalling. Chapter 9 will return to those distributions when an electrical impulse travels along a cell.

For Chapter 3, the important advance is the link between an energy account and a transport account. A concentration gradient can store energy. Passive movement tends to dissipate an available gradient; maintaining one in the face of leakage requires an ongoing process that supplies energy. A living membrane can contain proteins that perform that work.

This distinction also stops a common mistake. A protein that allows an ion through is not automatically an active pump. An open ion channel can permit passive movement down an electrochemical gradient. The pump's defining feature is its coupling to an energy source that can sustain transport against the otherwise favourable direction.

The root in Chapter 3 uses this principle with different machinery. A root proton pump can use ATP to export hydrogen ions. Their electrochemical gradient can then supply energy for another transporter, which couples proton return to nitrate entry. That is a relay between two proteins. Skou's crab nerve enzyme should never be relabelled as the root's nitrate transporter.

Nor should the account quietly become an explanation of water being pumped uphill. Maintaining ion distributions can change the conditions for water movement, but water through a passive channel still follows its water-potential gradient. That distinction is what lets the same chapter explain a root collecting scarce mineral ions and a mangrove taking up water from salty soil.

The story's useful habit is to ask for two ledgers. What material crosses the boundary, and what supplies the energy associated with that transfer? “There is a protein” answers neither question by itself. Skou's experiment helped connect them through measurable chemistry.
