:::scene Florence, summer 1668

Francesco Redi was chief physician to the Grand Dukes of Tuscany, Ferdinand II and Cosimo III de' Medici, in the city where Galileo had lived a generation earlier. When courtiers argued about whether scorpion venom kills only when injected or also when swallowed, or whether viper fangs poison through their teeth or through yellow bile in their gums, Redi never quoted ancient Greek books—he ran side-by-side tests.

In 1668 he published *Esperienze intorno alla generazione degl'insetti* (*Experiments on the Generation of Insects*). Having read William Harvey's suggestion that insects grow from seeds or eggs too small to notice, Redi placed dead snakes, fish, eels and veal into sets of wide-mouthed flasks—first leaving half open and sealing half with paper and string, and then covering a third set with fine *velo di Napoli* (Naples veil) so air could circulate freely while flies were kept out. Watching flies lay eggs on top of the gauze—and tracking the maggots from the open jars through their hard pupal cases until adult flies emerged—Redi gave biology its first modern controlled experiment.
