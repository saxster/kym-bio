:::scene Boston, 1967

A mitochondrion is part of a cell. It also has inherited DNA of its own. That combination is a question, not an explanation. Why would one compartment retain its own genetic machinery?

In 1967, Lynn Margulis published *On the origin of mitosing cells* under her name at the time, Lynn Sagan. She developed an older proposal: important parts of complex cells descended from organisms that had lived inside other organisms.

The proposal connected observations that might otherwise have stayed in separate files. Mitochondria and chloroplasts had bacterial similarities. They divided from existing organelles. Evidence placed DNA outside the nucleus. Ris and Plaut's 1962 work on a green alga's chloroplast was one such piece: microscopy and chemical tests supported the presence of DNA there.

A theory had to do more than collect intriguing outlines. It had to explain inheritance and generate further tests. Comparisons of genes later provided particularly strong evidence for bacterial ancestry. Chloroplasts connect with cyanobacteria, while mitochondria connect with another bacterial lineage.

The history did not end with tiny independent bacteria trapped inside us. Gene transfer and gene loss helped integrate the partners. The organelles we have now depend on their host cells. Their bacterial ancestry explains their unusual machinery; their dependence explains why they are parts of a larger cellular system.

Margulis did not invent every part of the idea, and one paper did not prove the whole account at once. Her contribution shows how an explanation can gather strength: make a connection, state what it predicts, and compare those predictions with independent evidence. Some details of her broader proposals have not been accepted. The bacterial origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts rests on the evidence that has survived testing.
