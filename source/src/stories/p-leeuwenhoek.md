:::scene Delft, the Netherlands, September 1674

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek never learned Latin, the language every European scholar in the seventeenth century used to write books. He was the son of a basket-maker in Delft, apprenticed at sixteen to a linen draper in Amsterdam, and returned home to run a cloth shop and serve as chamberlain to the sheriffs of Delft.

What Leeuwenhoek had was patience, steady hands, and an obsession with seeing smaller than anyone had ever seen. Cloth merchants used small magnifying glasses to count the warp and weft threads in linen. In 1665, Robert Hooke's book *Micrographia* had become the talk of Europe for its dramatic engravings of flea legs, fly eyes and cork "cells". Hooke used a compound microscope—two lenses in a tube—which magnified about $30\times$ to $50\times$. When Hooke tried to push two lenses to higher power, every edge blurred into rainbow fringes.

Leeuwenhoek realised that a **single**, tiny spherical lens of very short focal length could magnify five to九 times more sharply than two large lenses, provided you didn't mind holding the metal plate right up against your eyelashes. He heated thin soda-lime glass rods in a flame, pulled them into threads, and melted the thread tip into a tiny glass bead barely $1.5\text{ mm}$ across—or ground and polished tiny biconvex lenses by hand. Between two brass or silver plates the size of a matchbox, he mounted the lens in a pinhole and attached a pointed brass screw to position a glass capillary tube or a speck of wax less than a millimetre behind the bead.

In 1673, the Delft physician Reinier de Graaf wrote to the Royal Society in London that a local shopkeeper had built instruments far surpassing anything then known. Leeuwenhoek began sending long, conversational letters in Dutch to London—more than 300 letters over the next fifty years.

In September 1674, crossing the lake of Berkelse Mere, he scooped up a vial of summer water clouded with green streaks. Through his lens he saw spiral green ribbons and tiny creatures darting and spinning through the water. When London scholars doubted that thousands of living creatures could swim in a single drop of water, Robert Hooke himself followed Leeuwenhoek's instructions, infused peppercorns in water, and confirmed every word before the Royal Society in November 1677.

In September 1683, Leeuwenhoek scraped white matter from between his own teeth—even though, as he proudly told the Royal Society, he rubbed his teeth every morning with salt—mixed it with clean rainwater, and discovered rods, spheres and corkscrews: the first sight of **bacteria**. He kept grinding lenses and writing letters until he was ninety years old, even dictating letters from his deathbed to describe the exact structure of the diaphragm whose spasms were killing him.
