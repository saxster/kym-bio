:::scene Mackinac and later research stations, 1822–1833

Food disappears from a string. Does that mean it has been chemically digested? It sounds like a small question. In William Beaumont's published experiments, asking it carefully changes what the evidence can establish.

The investigation depended on Alexis St. Martin, a French Canadian voyageur who survived an accidental gunshot injury at Mackinac in 1822. Beaumont, an army surgeon, treated him. As the injury healed, an opening into St. Martin's stomach remained. This was a continuing feature of a person's life, not a window built for a scientist's convenience.

Beaumont's introduction says that he began gastric experiments in May 1825 at Fort Mackinac, then continued after transfer to Fort Niagara. Some early results appeared in 1826. His book, *Experiments and observations on the gastric juice, and the physiology of digestion*, was published at Plattsburgh in 1833. Accident, experimental work and publication are three different events. Calling the whole story “a discovery in 1825” hides how much happened before and afterwards.

#### Step 1: Notice what the apparatus really measured

In the First Series of the book, Experiment 1 is dated 1 August 1825. Beaumont described attaching several foods to a silk string and introducing them through the opening. The foods included differently prepared meats, bread and thinly sliced cabbage. He withdrew the string at intervals and inspected what remained.

The arrangement let him retrieve material and observe its visible condition. But the result was not a direct count of broken chemical bonds. A piece could lose its structure, soften, melt or come off the string. Those possibilities matter when the observation is “the food is no longer attached”.

Beaumont's later remarks on this experiment recognise that problem. The cabbage had been cut into thin pieces, exposing much surface. The bread was porous and could admit fluid; it might have fallen away after softening before all its material was chemically digested. Fat becoming oil could also explain disappearance from the string. Comparing such foods did not hold physical form constant.

This is a useful lesson in what scientists call a confounded comparison. Several differences changed together: the material, its preparation, its texture and its exposed surface. The observations were real, but a simple ranking of disappearance times would not isolate one chemical cause. We should not turn that record into a universal timetable for digestion.

#### Step 2: Move part of the process out of the organ

Other experiments asked a more focused question. Was breakdown caused only by the stomach's moving wall, or could its secreted juice do chemical work away from the organ?

In Experiments 2–3, Beaumont described drawing off gastric fluid through a tube, placing beef with it in a vial, and keeping the vial warm in a water bath. A similar piece of beef was suspended inside the stomach. He reported breakdown in both settings, with different progress in the two environments.

That comparison separates one part of the system: the collected juice could act without being enclosed by the stomach's muscular wall. It does not make movement irrelevant. Mixing can renew contact between fluid and food surfaces. Nor does it establish that hydrochloric acid alone causes every digestive reaction. The collected fluid contained several substances; a comparison using that whole mixture does not isolate a single component.

Today's enzyme account helps distinguish chemical roles. Pepsin catalyses protein hydrolysis under suitable acidic conditions. Acid helps provide those conditions and activate its precursor. Further digestion occurs in the intestine. None of those claims should be credited to one vial as though it measured all the steps, and observing breakdown is still different from measuring absorption into tissues.

#### Step 3: Read the participant's experience in the record

The First Series also records St. Martin reporting pain and distress. During Experiment 1, Beaumont eventually withdrew the remaining foods and did not return them. These details prevent a retelling in which the procedures were simply effortless observation through a convenient opening. The investigator's own record makes clear that participation could be uncomfortable.

St. Martin was essential to the work. He was also someone with employment, a family and decisions about where to live. The research was interrupted when he returned to Canada. Beaumont's introduction describes his efforts to find him again. A story that treats those interruptions only as lost laboratory time gives us the investigator's priorities as though they were the whole human situation.

The relationship combined medical care, employment and research. Later agreements tied St. Martin's experimental service to payment and provisions such as food, clothing and lodging. The Becker Medical Library preserves a catalogue of four articles of agreement from 1832–1833. Scholarship based on that collection reproduces the terms of an October 1832 contract.

A written agreement is evidence of an arrangement. It is not, by itself, proof that someone could freely refuse a procedure without losing essential support. Dependence on an investigator who is also a doctor and employer complicates that freedom. We do not have to invent St. Martin's private thoughts to recognise the imbalance; the documented roles and contractual obligations are enough to make it a serious question.

The surviving book gives extensive access to Beaumont's descriptions and conclusions. It does not give equally complete access to St. Martin's own account. That limit belongs in the history. Scientific value and the acceptability of how evidence was obtained are separate questions, and the first cannot automatically settle the second.

Return to Chapter 7 with two habits. When something disappears, ask what the apparatus actually detected and which alternative explanations remain. When an experiment depends on a person, ask whose contribution made it possible and what freedom that person had over the work.
