:::scene London and Cambridge, 1950–1953, and Raipur, Lahore, Bethesda and Madison, 1961–1968

Walk into two very different basement laboratories twelve years apart. In the first—at King's College London in May 1952—a thirty-one-year-old physical chemist named **Rosalind Franklin** and her doctoral student **Raymond Gosling** are threading a single microscopic fibre of calf-thymus DNA, no thicker than a spider's silk, across a pinhole camera while bubbling saturated salt solution through the chamber to hold the relative humidity near 92 percent. In the second—at the University of Wisconsin in Madison in the mid-1960s—an organic chemist born in a tiny village in Punjab, **Har Gobind Khorana**, is linking nucleotide letters one by one in glass flasks to build artificial genetic messages of exact, repeating sequence.

Between those two benches lies one of the greatest detective investigations in science: first discovering the three-dimensional geometry of the DNA molecule, and then cracking the four-letter triplet cipher that every living ribosome reads to print proteins.

### Part 1 · Erwin Chargaff's chromatogram and Rosalind Franklin's Photo 51 (1950–1953)

Until the mid-1940s, most biochemists dismissed **DNA** as a dull structural scaffold inside the nucleus. A widely taught textbook idea—Phoebus Levene's "tetranucleotide hypothesis"—assumed that DNA was just a monotonous repeating string of the four bases (**adenine `A`**, **thymine `T`**, **guanine `G`**, and **cytosine `C`**) in equal 25 percent shares. If a molecule repeats `A–T–G–C–A–T–G–C` forever, it can carry no more specific information than a page printed with a single repeating word.

That assumption collapsed after Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty proved in 1944 that pure DNA—and only DNA—carries hereditary transformation in bacteria. At Columbia University in New York, the biochemist **Erwin Chargaff** read Avery's paper and decided to measure the actual proportions of the four bases in DNA from different organisms using paper chromatography and ultraviolet spectrophotometry. By 1950, **Erwin Chargaff** had uncovered two striking facts across humans, cattle, salmon, sea urchins, yeast, and bacteria:

1. DNA is **not** a monotonous 25-25-25-25 repeat. Human DNA is rich in `A` and `T` (~30 percent each, ~20 percent `G` and `C`), whereas certain bacteria are rich in `G` and `C`. The base composition varies from species to species, giving DNA the freedom to store complex biological instructions.
2. Yet inside every double-stranded DNA sample from every species, the number of **adenine (`A`)** molecules always equalled the number of **thymine (`T`)** molecules (`%A ≈ %T`), and the number of **guanine (`G`)** molecules always equalled **cytosine (`C`)** (`%G ≈ %C`).

At the time, **Erwin Chargaff** did not know *why* `A` matched `T` and `G` matched `C`. The physical clue came from X-ray crystallography in London.

When **Rosalind Franklin** arrived at King's College London in January 1951 after years of pioneering X-ray diffraction work on coals and carbons in Paris, earlier X-ray images of DNA fibres had been blurry because researchers had unknowingly mixed two different hydration states of the molecule. **Rosalind Franklin** solved that experimental bottleneck by controlling the water content of the air around the DNA fibre. At lower humidity (~75 percent), the fibre formed a shorter, crystalline **A-form**; when hydrated at ~92 percent humidity, the fibre lengthened by about a quarter and relaxed into a wet paracrystalline **B-form**—the conformation DNA adopts inside watery living cells.

Between 1 and 6 May 1952, **Rosalind Franklin** and **Raymond Gosling** exposed a single hydrated B-form DNA fibre to a fine-focus X-ray beam for sixty-two hours. The resulting photograph—labelled **Photo 51** in their laboratory log—showed a bold, unmistakable X-shaped cross of dark diffraction spots. To a trained crystallographer, that X-cross was the direct mathematical signature of a **helix**. From the spacing of the layer lines and the dark meridional arcs at the top and bottom of **Photo 51**, **Rosalind Franklin** calculated the core dimensions of B-DNA:

- One complete helical turn repeats every **`3.4 nm`** (`34 Å`) along the fibre axis.
- Adjacent stacked bases are separated vertically by **`0.34 nm`** (`3.4 Å`), meaning exactly **10 base pairs** stack in each full turn.
- The missing fourth layer line on the X-cross showed that two coaxial sugar–phosphate chains are offset from each other along the cylinder, creating an unequal **major groove** and **minor groove**.
- Because the charged phosphate groups avidly bind water molecules when the fibre hydrates, the **sugar–phosphate backbones must sit on the outside** of the **`2.0 nm`** (`20 Å`) cylinder, with the less water-soluble bases tucked inside.

At the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, **Francis Crick**—a physicist who had co-developed the mathematical theory of helical X-ray diffraction—and the young American biologist **James Watson** were trying to build wire-and-tin molecular models of DNA. An early three-stranded model they built in late 1951 had failed because it placed the phosphate groups on the inside and ignored the water content Franklin had measured. In late January 1953, without **Rosalind Franklin**'s knowledge or prior permission, **Maurice Wilkins** at King's College showed **Photo 51** to Watson, and in February 1953 Max Perutz shared an internal Medical Research Council (MRC) progress report containing Franklin's precise unit-cell measurements and space-group symmetry (`C2`, which told **Francis Crick** that the two sugar–phosphate chains run in opposite, **antiparallel** directions).

One chemical piece remained. When Watson initially tried pairing identical bases (`A` with `A`, `C` with `C`), the textbook drawings he used showed the bases in a rare *enol* form, and two large two-ring purines (`A` or `G`) bulged wider than two smaller one-ring pyrimidines (`T` or `C`). The American structural chemist Jerry Donohue, who shared their Cambridge office, pointed out that in living cells the bases exist in the **keto** form. The next morning, shifting cardboard cutouts on his desk, Watson saw that a two-ring **adenine (`A`)** forms two hydrogen bonds with a one-ring **thymine (`T`)** (`A=T`), and a two-ring **guanine (`G`)** forms three hydrogen bonds with a one-ring **cytosine (`C`)** (`C≡G`), producing two rungs of identical **`2.0 nm` width** and identical symmetry.

On 25 April 1953, *Nature* published Watson and Crick's model alongside two experimental papers from King's College—including **Rosalind Franklin** and Raymond Gosling's paper presenting **Photo 51**. Complementary base pairing (`A` with `T`, `C` with `G`) explained **Erwin Chargaff**'s ratios at a stroke, left the sequence of bases along one strand completely free to spell out genetic instructions, and showed how each strand could act as a template to build its partner. Tragically, **Rosalind Franklin** died of ovarian cancer in April 1958 at age thirty-seven—after doing further landmark X-ray studies on tobacco mosaic virus and poliovirus—four years before the 1962 Nobel Prize was awarded to Crick, Watson, and Wilkins.

### Part 2 · From Raipur to Bethesda and Madison: Marshall Nirenberg and Har Gobind Khorana crack the 64 codons (1961–1968)

Knowing that a gene is a long sequence of `A`, `T`, `C`, and `G` bases raised the next great riddle: *how do four nucleotide letters specify twenty different amino acids?*

Simple arithmetic showed that single letters (`4¹ = 4`) or pairs of letters (`4² = 16`) cannot name 20 amino acids, whereas **triplets of three bases** yield **`4³ = 64` combinations**. In 1961, **Francis Crick**, Sydney Brenner, Leslie Barnett, and R. J. Watts-Tobin proved genetically in bacteriophage T4 that the cell reads genes in non-overlapping **triplets** from a fixed starting point: inserting or deleting one or two bases (`+1` or `+2`) scrambles every triplet downstream, whereas inserting or deleting **three bases (`+3` or `−3`)** restores the reading frame.

But which of the 64 triplets codes for which amino acid? In May 1961 at the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda, Maryland, **Marshall Nirenberg** and Heinrich Matthaei prepared a cell-free extract of *Escherichia coli* bacteria containing **ribosomes**, transfer RNAs, ATP, and amino acids, added DNase to destroy the bacteria's own DNA, and fed the ribosomes an artificial **messenger RNA (mRNA)** strand made purely of uracil bases: **`UUUUUUUUUUUU...` (`poly-U`)**. The ribosomes printed a polypeptide chain made purely of **phenylalanine (`Phe–Phe–Phe...`)**. The first word of the genetic dictionary had been read: **`UUU = Phenylalanine`**. Soon `CCC` was shown to code for proline and `AAA` for lysine.

Homopolymers like `poly-U`, however, could only crack the four all-identical triplets (`UUU`, `CCC`, `AAA`, `GGG`). When researchers mixed two bases at random, they could tell that a triplet contained two `U`s and one `C`, but not whether the spelling was `UUC`, `UCU`, or `CUU`.

Solving the exact spelling of all 64 codons required someone who could synthesise nucleic acids of **known, repeating sequence** letter by letter. That chemist was **Har Gobind Khorana**. Born in 1922 in Raipur, a village of about a hundred people in Punjab (then British India), **Har Gobind Khorana** was the youngest of five children of a village agricultural taxation clerk (*patwari*). His father insisted that all the children learn to read and write, and young Har Gobind studied under a tree with the village teacher before winning scholarships to D.A.V. High School in Multan and Punjab University in Lahore, where Mahan Singh supervised his master's degree in chemistry. A Government of India fellowship took Khorana to Liverpool, Zurich, and Cambridge—where he worked alongside Alexander Todd on nucleotide chemistry—before he established his own laboratories in Vancouver and then at the University of Wisconsin–Madison.

**Har Gobind Khorana** combined precision organic synthesis with enzymatic polymerisation. First, he chemically synthesised short DNA segments of exact alternating sequence—such as a two-base repeat `TC TC TC TC` or a three-base repeat `TTC TTC TTC`. Next, he used DNA polymerase to copy those short templates into long double-stranded DNA polymers, and RNA polymerase to transcribe them into long **messenger RNA (`mRNA`)** strands with an exact repeating sequence. When he added those synthetic mRNAs to cell-free ribosomes, the results were crystal clear:

- The repeating dinucleotide mRNA **`(UC)ₙ = UCUCUCUCUCUC...`** can only be read by a ribosome in triplets as alternating **`UCU | CUC | UCU | CUC`**. In Khorana's tubes, the ribosomes printed a polypeptide of strictly alternating **serine and leucine (`Ser–Leu–Ser–Leu`)**. Combined with binding assays developed by **Marshall Nirenberg** and Philip Leder, this proved that **`UCU` codes for serine (`Ser`)** and **`CUC` codes for leucine (`Leu`)**.
- Similarly, **`(UG)ₙ`** (`UGU | GUG`) yielded alternating **cysteine and valine (`Cys–Val–Cys–Val`)**, **`(AG)ₙ`** (`AGA | GAG`) yielded alternating **arginine and glutamic acid (`Arg–Glu–Arg–Glu`)**, and **`(AC)ₙ`** (`ACA | CAC`) yielded alternating **threonine and histidine (`Thr–His–Thr–His`)**.
- Repeating trinucleotides such as **`(UAG)ₙ`** confirmed that **`UAA`, `UAG`, and `UGA`** do not code for any amino acid at all: they are the **stop (nonsense) codons** discovered genetically by **Obaid Siddiqi**, Sydney Brenner, and Alan Garen (which you met in Chapter 9).

By 1966, **Marshall Nirenberg** and **Har Gobind Khorana** had completed the entire 64-codon genetic table—and proved that the same dictionary is read by bacteria, plants, fruit flies, and human cells alike. In 1968 they shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Robert Holley, and two years later Khorana's team went on to achieve the first total chemical synthesis of a complete, functional gene in a laboratory flask.
