:::scene Colaba, Mumbai & Pasadena, 1921–1976

For more than a century after Luigi Galvani made a frog's leg twitch with metal wires in 1791, people pictured nerves as living telegraph cables. It was an easy comparison to make, and a misleading one. In a copper wire (Book 2, *The Moving World*, Chapter 12), free electrons drift through solid metal while the electric field races along at nearly the speed of light. A nerve fibre, by contrast, is a thin tube of salty water wrapped in an oily lipid membrane. It contains no metal core and no free electrons waiting to run along it. Worse still for a simple wire model, Santiago Ramón y Cajal's silver-stained microscope slides in the 1880s and 1890s showed that one nerve cell does not even fuse into the next: between every neurone and its neighbour lies an open microscopic gap, named the **synapse** by Charles Scott Sherrington in 1897.

Two fundamental questions therefore stood in the way of understanding how your brain and spinal cord control your body:

1. **Across the gap:** If two neurones do not touch, how does an impulse cross the 20 nm space from one cell to the next?
2. **Along the fibre:** How does a leaky membrane tube of sodium (`Na⁺`) and potassium (`K⁺`) ions conduct a fast, non-fading electrical spike without losing strength over a metre of arm or leg?

### 1. Otto Loewi's two frog hearts and the chemical courier (1921)

In 1921, at the University of Graz in Austria, the pharmacologist **Otto Loewi** tested whether a nerve speaks to its target organ by sending electricity across the junction or by releasing a dissolved chemical substance. In a laboratory experiment on isolated amphibian tissue (a historical physiological procedure never to be repeated on animals at home), Loewi placed two beating frog hearts in separate glass chambers filled with Ringer's salt solution.

Heart 1 still had its **vagus nerve** attached; Heart 2 had all its nerves removed. Loewi stimulated the vagus nerve of Heart 1 electrically for several minutes, and watched Heart 1's beat slow down as expected. Then he drew up some of the salty liquid bathing Heart 1 with a pipette and transferred it into the chamber holding Heart 2. Even though no nerve had been stimulated on Heart 2, **Heart 2 slowed down as well**. When he instead stimulated the sympathetic accelerator nerve on Heart 1 and transferred the bathing fluid, Heart 2 sped up.

Loewi called the vagus-released substance *Vagusstoff* ("vagus stuff"). Working with Henry Hallett Dale in London, researchers showed that *Vagusstoff* is the small organic molecule **acetylcholine**. An electrical impulse arriving at the end of a neurone releases packets of neurotransmitter molecules that diffuse across the synaptic gap and bind to receptor proteins on the next cell.

### 2. Hodgkin and Huxley's giant squid axon (1939–1952)

Knowing that a chemical bridges the gap between cells still left the mystery of the electrical impulse travelling *along* each neurone. Vertebrate nerve fibres are typically only 1 to 20 µm wide—too narrow for 1930s researchers to thread a glass voltage-measuring electrode *inside* the cell without tearing the membrane.

In 1936, the zoologist J. Z. Young pointed out that the North Atlantic squid (*Loligo forbesii*) possesses a **squid giant axon** up to **0.5 to 1.0 mm** wide—thick enough to see with the naked eye—which triggers the rapid mantle contraction that jet-propels the squid backwards away from predators. Working at the Marine Biological Association laboratory in Plymouth in the summer of 1939 (again in historical laboratory work on marine tissue), **Alan Hodgkin** and his student **Andrew Huxley** slid a fine glass capillary electrode down the inside of a single squid giant axon.

Everyone expected that during an impulse the membrane's negative resting voltage (about **−70 mV** inside relative to outside) would simply drop to 0 mV as the membrane briefly became leaky. Instead, Hodgkin and Huxley's oscilloscope trace shot right past zero to **+30 mV** before plunging back down to −70 mV in less than two milliseconds.

Returning to Plymouth after the Second World War, Hodgkin, Huxley and Bernard Katz used an electronic feedback circuit called a **voltage clamp** (published in five landmark papers in *The Journal of Physiology* in 1952) to hold the axon membrane at any chosen voltage and measure the separate currents carried by sodium (`Na⁺`) and potassium (`K⁺`) ions:

- At rest (**−70 mV**), the sodium–potassium pump later identified chemically by Jens Christian Skou in 1957 keeps `Na⁺` concentrated outside the cell and `K⁺` concentrated inside.
- When a neighbouring patch of membrane depolarises past a **threshold** (about **−55 mV**), **voltage-gated Na⁺ channels** snap open first. Sodium ions rush *in* down their steep concentration and electrical gradient, flipping the inside voltage to **+30 mV**.
- Within a millisecond, those `Na⁺` gates automatically inactivate while **voltage-gated K⁺ channels** open. Potassium ions rush *out* down their gradient, restoring the negative inside voltage (**repolarisation**) and briefly overshooting to **−75 mV** (**refractory period**), which guarantees that the domino wave travels forward rather than backward.

### 3. Obaid Siddiqi and the warm-paralysed fruit flies of TIFR Colaba (1962–1976)

Hodgkin and Huxley's 1952 equations predicted that the axon membrane must contain distinct molecular gates for `Na⁺` and `K⁺`, yet nobody had seen those protein channels or knew which genes built them. That link was forged with the help of fruit flies (*Drosophila melanogaster*) and a physicist-turned-biologist working on the southern tip of Mumbai.

Born in Basti in Uttar Pradesh in 1932, **Obaid Siddiqi** studied plant genetics in Aligarh and Delhi before completing his PhD with Guido Pontecorvo in Glasgow and postdoctoral research on the genetic code with Alan Garen in the United States. In 1962, at the invitation of Homi Bhabha (prompted by physicist Leo Szilard), Siddiqi came to Mumbai to establish the **Molecular Biology Unit** at the **Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR)** in Colaba, right beside the Arabian Sea.

In the early 1970s, Siddiqi turned from bacterial genetics to a bold new question he shared with his friend **Seymour Benzer** at Caltech: can you dissect the electrical machinery of the nervous system one gene at a time by finding single-gene mutants whose nerves fail under specific conditions?

In 1971, David Suzuki and his colleagues in Vancouver had isolated a remarkable temperature-sensitive *Drosophila* mutant called **`para`** (short for *paralytic*). At an ordinary cool room temperature of **22 °C**, *para* flies walk, climb and fly just like wild-type flies. Warm the glass vial to **29–32 °C**, however, and within five seconds every *para* fly tumbles to the bottom, completely motionless. Cool the vial back to 22 °C, and within seconds the flies stand up and walk away as if nothing had happened.

Why did warming by a few degrees switch off the nervous system so cleanly? Working between Pasadena and his neurogenetics laboratory at TIFR Colaba, **Obaid Siddiqi and Seymour Benzer** (published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* in September 1976) placed microscopic glass electrodes into the cervical nerve and thoracic flight muscles of living fruit flies while raising and lowering their temperature:

:::evidence Pinpointing axonal versus synaptic failure in a living circuit | Siddiqi & Benzer, 1976
Siddiqi and Benzer compared three different temperature-sensitive paralytic mutants—**`para`** (*paralytic*), **`shi`** (*shibire*), and a new mutant they isolated called **`com`** (*comatose*)—by stimulating the neck nerve and recording both the axonal action potential and the flight-muscle response:

- **In `para` (*paralytic*):** As soon as temperature rose past the threshold (~29–32 °C), **action potentials along the nerve axon vanished abruptly**, even though stimulating the flight muscle directly still made the muscle contract. When cooled by a few degrees, the axonal action potential reappeared within seconds.
- **In `shi` (*shibire*) and `com` (*comatose*):** By contrast, the nerve axon **kept firing normal action potentials** above the paralysis temperature, but the signal failed to cross the **synapse** (neuromuscular junction) because synaptic vesicle recycling and neurotransmitter release were blocked.
:::

Siddiqi and Benzer's 1976 recordings proved that single genes separately build the axon's action-potential gates and the synapse's chemical-release machinery. Thirteen years later, in 1989, Barry Ganetzky's team cloned and sequenced the *Drosophila* **`para` gene**: it encodes the main pore-forming protein of the **voltage-gated sodium (`Na⁺`) channel** itself. Temperature-sensitive changes can impair sodium-channel function on warming and allow recovery on cooling. The physiological recordings located a failure of axonal signalling; identifying the channel protein required the later molecular work. Neither the paralysis observation nor a simple channel drawing establishes the exact structural change for every mutant.
