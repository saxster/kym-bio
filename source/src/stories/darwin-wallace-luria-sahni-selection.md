In September 1835, when the twenty-six-year-old naturalist **Charles Robert Darwin** stepped ashore on the volcanic rock of the Galápagos Archipelago from HMS *Beagle*, he did not experience the cinematic eureka moment that simplified textbooks often invent. Looking at the small, dull-feathered birds hopping among arid thorn scrub and prickly-pear cacti (`Opuntia`), Darwin did not realise they were all finches, nor did he suspect that each island housed its own species. In his field notes he jotted some down as blackbirds and others as grosbeaks, warblers, or wrens, tossing many skins into shared bags without recording which specific island each bird came from. Only when vice-governor Nicholas Lawson remarked that he could tell from a giant tortoise's shell which island it inhabited did Darwin begin to wonder whether island isolation mattered.

The real turning point arrived in London in March 1837, when Darwin handed his bird skins to the Zoological Society's ornithologist **John Gould**. Within days Gould reported an astonishing anatomical fact: the supposed blackbirds, grosbeaks, and warblers were thirteen closely allied species of a single endemic ground-finch group, differing systematically in the depth, curvature, and mechanical leverage of their beaks. To recover missing island information, Darwin consulted to Captain Robert FitzRoy, servant Syms Covington, and shipmate Harry Fuller, who had labelled their private specimen bags island by island. The specimens and locality information became part of a broader argument: an ancestral South American flock had colonised the islands and diversified as different beak shapes gave survival advantages on tough seeds, cactus flowers, or wood-boring insects. In July 1837, on page 36 of his private *Notebook B*, Darwin drew a small branching tree beneath two quiet words: *"I think."*

For twenty years Darwin assembled evidence from living species, fossils and breeding. Half a world away, the collector **Alfred Russel Wallace**, remembered here as **Wallace in Ternate**, independently reached natural selection in `1858`. In his later recollection, an attack of intermittent fever and his memory of Malthus's population argument helped the idea come together. The precise scene should be read as Wallace's retrospective account, rather than a witnessed bedside transcript. If inherited variants differed in their ability to survive and leave offspring, population composition could change without any foresight. Wallace sent Darwin his essay, *On the Tendency of Varieties to Depart Indefinitely from the Original Type*.

When the packet reached Down House in June 1858, Darwin saw his own unpublished theory closely reflected. At that very moment his household was in grief: his infant son Charles Waring Darwin died of scarlet fever on 28 June 1858. Rather than let Darwin's twenty-year priority vanish or suppress Wallace's breakthrough, Charles Lyell and Joseph Dalton Hooker arranged a joint reading of Darwin's 1844 sketch, an 1857 letter to Asa Gray, and Wallace's Ternate essay at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Neither author attended—Darwin was mourning his child, and Wallace was still in New Guinea—yet natural selection was now public, followed in November 1859 by *On the Origin of Species*.

Darwin and Wallace knew selection required heritable variation, yet neither knew whether the environment *caused* organisms to mutate on demand. For decades, many microbiologists assumed that pouring an antibiotic or bacteriophage onto bacteria actively taught cells to develop resistance. In 1943, **Salvador Luria** and Max Delbrück tested that claim with their fluctuation test on *Escherichia coli*, showing that resistant colonies varied in jackpot bursts across parallel tubes—the statistical signature of random mutations occurring *before* the selective agent was added. Nine years later, at Wisconsin in 1952, **Joshua Lederberg** and **Esther Lederberg** made that proof visible to the naked eye. Pressing a wooden block wrapped in sterile velveteen cloth onto an antibiotic-free master agar plate of bacteria, they stamped exact spatial replicas onto plates containing streptomycin. Resistant colonies appeared at identical `(x, y)` coordinates on every replicate plate. Returning to those matching spots on the antibiotic-free master plate, the Lederbergs isolated pure resistant cultures whose ancestors had never touched a single molecule of the drug. The experiment showed that resistant variants could exist before selection. Later research also showed that some antibiotics increase mutation rates: this changes the supply of variation without specifying the mutation a cell needs.

While the Lederbergs worked in Petri dishes, field biologists investigated selection outdoors. **Bernard Kettlewell** released marked pale and dark peppered moths in polluted Birmingham and cleaner Dorset woods during the `1950s`. Recaptures favoured the less conspicuous morph in each setting, supporting camouflage and bird predation. Researchers later questioned whether release methods represented natural resting behaviour closely enough. **Michael Majerus** improved the test: across six release seasons (`2002–2007`) he used `4,864` moths at low densities, placing them in branch sleeves at night and removing the sleeves before dawn. Birds were observed taking moths, and the results supported differential predation. His observations also included naturally resting moths on trunks. Science became stronger by testing the vulnerable details of its own explanation.

Human blood provided another important comparison. **J. B. S. Haldane** proposed that infectious disease could help maintain red-cell variants; **Anthony Allison** investigated sickle-cell trait and malaria in East Africa in `1954`. In one sample of `290` children, parasites were detected in `12` of `43` children with the trait (`27.9%`), compared with `113` of `247` without it (`45.7%`). These were parasite observations, not direct measurements of severe-malaria mortality or reproductive fitness. Subsequent studies established strong protection against severe malaria in many settings. **L. D. Sanghvi** and colleagues investigated the distribution of sickle-cell trait in Indian populations. Together these studies motivate the balancing-selection puzzle: a variant can be harmful in one genotype yet beneficial in another under particular conditions.

Finally, fossils and molecules let biologists investigate changes across deep time. In `1948`, **Birbal Sahni** described *Pentoxyleae*, unusual extinct seed plants from the Rajmahal Hills. His paper called the material Jurassic; later dating assigned the relevant Rajmahal deposits to the Early Cretaceous. Their combination of anatomical features raises questions about relationships among ancient seed plants; it does not alone prove a direct line of ancestry to modern conifers. *Glossopteris* fossils across southern continents provide a different geographical clue to Gondwana.

At Urbana in `1977`, **Carl Woese and George Fox** used ribosomal RNA comparisons to recognise an archaeal lineage distinct from ordinary bacteria. In `1990`, Woese, **Otto Kandler** and **Mark Wheelis** formally proposed three domains. Homologous sequences can retain shared history when outer shapes mislead. But no short DNA string is a perfectly ticking clock: rates vary, dates need calibration, and reconstructions carry uncertainty. Darwin's small branching sketch had become a testable programme of research, continually corrected by new evidence.
