During his graduate research in Minnesota in the late 1930s, **Raymond Lindeman** studied Cedar Creek Bog, with help from his wife and fellow biologist Eleanor Hall. Measuring an ecosystem meant working across very different scales: plants, tiny grazers, fish and the organic material accumulating below them. Instead of treating each as an isolated collection, he asked how feeding and energy connected the whole system.

In 1927, the English ecologist **Charles Elton** described food chains and a “pyramid of numbers”: many small organisms can support fewer larger consumers. Counting, however, did not tell the whole story. A single tree can support thousands of insects; to understand the food supply, ecologists also needed to measure biomass and the rate at which it was produced.

Lindeman’s Cedar Creek work helped turn a food web into an energy account. His 1942 paper drew together measurements and estimates of production and respiration from Cedar Creek and other aquatic systems. Energy incorporated into new biomass at one level was not all incorporated into new biomass at the next: some organisms were not eaten, some swallowed material was not absorbed, and respiration transferred energy as heat. Waste and dead material could still feed decomposers. The familiar 10% figure is a useful stated assumption for a classroom calculation, not a universal value he proved for every transfer. Compare the same quantity—production—with production at the next level, using the same area and time.

When Lindeman submitted his manuscript, *"The Trophic-Dynamic Aspect of Ecology"*, to the journal *Ecology* in 1941, two senior referees initially rejected it as too theoretical. His advisor, G. Evelyn Hutchinson at Yale, defended the bog data line by line until the editor reversed the decision. Lindeman never held the printed issue. His liver failed in June 1942 at the age of twenty-six, four months before his paper appeared in print and transformed ecology into a quantitative science of one-way energy flow and matter moving between biological and environmental reservoirs.

Yet if energy flows through the living world only once before radiating away as heat, why doesn't life run out of carbon and nitrogen atoms? Half a century before Lindeman's bog audit, two microbiologists in Europe had uncovered the hidden bacterial engines that recycle nitrogen between air and soil. In the late 1880s, the Russian microbiologist **Sergei Winogradsky**, working in Strasbourg and Zurich, showed that soil nitrification is a two-step relay run by chemoautotrophic aerobic bacteria in well-aerated soil: *Nitrosomonas* oxidises ammonium ions into nitrite (`NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻`), and *Nitrobacter* oxidises nitrite into nitrate (`NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻`), the exact ion that plant root hairs absorb by active transport. In the same years in Delft, the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck isolated *Rhizobium* from the pink root nodules of leguminous pea and bean plants (`1888`) and free-living *Azotobacter* from garden soil (`1901`), proving that specialized nitrogen-fixing bacteria crack the triple covalent bond of atmospheric nitrogen gas (`N₂ → NH₄⁺`).

During the second half of the twentieth century, ecologists realized that human industry was pushing both global nutrient cycles and living food webs out of balance. In March 1958, the American geochemist **Charles David Keeling** installed a high-precision infrared gas analyser high on the volcanic slopes of Mauna Loa in Hawaii. Within two years, Keeling discovered that the planetary atmosphere breathes once a year—carbon dioxide (`CO₂`) drops every northern summer as temperate and boreal forests photosynthesise, and rises every autumn and winter as cellular respiration and microbial decomposition return `CO₂` to the air. Underneath that annual sawtooth wave lay a relentless upward climb: from `315` parts per million (`ppm`) in 1958 to over `425 ppm` today, driven by fossil-fuel combustion and deforestation.

Four years after Keeling began his Mauna Loa record, in 1962, the marine biologist **Rachel Carson** published *Silent Spring*, bringing evidence about persistent pesticides and ecological harm to a wide public. Microorganisms break DDT down slowly, sometimes into products such as DDE that also persist and accumulate. Later research connected DDE exposure with eggshell thinning and reproductive failure in susceptible birds. In India, scientists and campaigners including E. K. Janaki Ammal, Salim Ali and **Madhav Gadgil** helped draw attention to the biological importance of Silent Valley’s evergreen forest. The proposed hydroelectric project was abandoned in 1983, and the forest was formally notified as a national park in 1984. Conservation required more than one discovery or one person: field evidence, public campaigning and government decisions all mattered.

While ecologists studied microbes outdoors, other scientists learned to culture them at scale. In Oxford between 1939 and 1941, **Ernst Chain**, **Howard Florey**, Norman Heatley and colleagues developed penicillin as a treatment, initially growing mould in many shallow vessels and solving difficult extraction problems. Scale-up then involved USDA researchers at Peoria and industrial engineers: aerated deep tanks, improved media and productive mould strains enabled much larger supplies. Modern fermenters combine sterility, nutrient feeds, mixing, gas exchange, temperature and pH control, and waste handling. Cooling water removes microbial respiratory heat. Hindustan Antibiotics was incorporated at Pimpri in 1954 and its plant commissioned in 1955–56. **Yellapragada SubbaRow**, whose work we met earlier, died in 1948; he did not help launch that plant in 1954.

A carefully controlled fermenter cannot by itself give a bacterium a new inherited instruction. For decades after the first successful human insulin treatment in 1922, patients depended on insulin extracted from pig and cattle pancreases. Animal insulin differed slightly in amino acid sequence and some patients had immune reactions; supply also depended on animal glands. Could microbes make a product with the human sequence?

In 1973, **Herbert Boyer**, **Stanley Cohen** and colleagues demonstrated functional recombinant plasmids in bacteria by combining DNA fragments. Restriction enzymes could make defined cuts, compatible sticky ends could pair, and DNA ligase could seal the DNA backbones. The 1973 demonstration combined bacterial DNA; it was not an insulin experiment. In 1978, Genentech and City of Hope researchers instead used chemically synthesised DNA instructions for insulin’s A and B chains, expressed them separately in *E. coli*, and recovered and joined the chains. The resulting mature insulin has two chains totalling 51 amino acids. A raw human genomic insulin gene, with introns, would not simply work in ordinary bacteria: scientists need suitable coding DNA and expression controls. Other processes use a precursor that is processed afterwards. In every route, making the protein is followed by recovery, correct folding or processing and purification. Humulin received US approval in 1982.

Four decades after those recombinant-plasmid experiments, in 2012, **Jennifer Doudna**, **Emmanuelle Charpentier** and colleagues demonstrated programmable DNA cutting with **CRISPR-Cas9**, adapted from a bacterial defence against viruses. A guide RNA contains a target-matching region that directs Cas9 to suitable DNA beside a PAM sequence. Cas9 cuts; the cell’s repair machinery then changes the DNA. A cut alone does not guarantee correction of a chosen letter, and unintended outcomes must be checked. An approved sickle-cell treatment uses editing to raise fetal haemoglobin rather than directly repairing the HbS letter. The shared genetic code makes transferable instructions possible; the experiments also show how much careful engineering and testing is needed to turn those instructions into a useful treatment.
