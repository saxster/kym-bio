:::scene London, 1665

Cut cork thin enough and light passes through it. Under Robert Hooke's microscope, that ordinary material showed a pattern of little compartments. In *Micrographia*, published in 1665, he described them as cells: small rooms.

There is a useful trap in the story. Cork was not a window into a complete, busy living cell. Hooke was looking at dead tissue, where the surrounding walls remained. The spaces and walls supplied the name before scientists understood the machinery that living cells contain.

A modern school drawing puts a nucleus, mitochondria and other labelled structures inside a boundary. It combines findings made through many methods over many years. You could not have read that inventory from Hooke's cork alone.

The same caution belongs beside your own microscope. First record what you can see. Then distinguish it from what other observations allow you to infer. A pale region may contain structures that your instrument cannot resolve. An empty-looking compartment may once have been alive. Better evidence comes from comparing preparations and using methods suited to the question.

The name *cell* survived because the pattern was memorable. The explanation grew far beyond the pattern. When you call an onion's little compartments “cells”, keep both parts of the history: the visible rooms that supplied the word, and the later work that revealed what living cells do.
