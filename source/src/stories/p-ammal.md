:::scene Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, 1934

In the 1920s, India—the ancestral home of sugarcane—was ironically importing refined white sugar from Java because native Indian canes were hardy against drought and frost but thin and low in sucrose, whereas tropical Javanese canes (*Saccharum officinarum*) were sweet and thick-stemmed but died in northern Indian winters.

Edavalath Kakkat Janaki Ammal, born in Thalassery in Kerala in 1897, turned down an arranged marriage to study botany at Queen Mary's College and Presidency College in Madras, then sailed to the University of Michigan on a Barbour Scholarship, becoming one of the first Indian women to earn a doctorate in botany in the United States (1931).

Joining the Imperial Sugarcane Breeding Station in Coimbatore, she peering through her microscope at the **chromosomes** inside dividing sugarcane cells (Chapter 12). By crossing tropical sweet canes with wild Indian grasses (*Saccharum spontaneum*) and even bamboo and maize—and selecting **polyploid** hybrids that carried multiple sets of chromosomes—she helped create the famed `Co` (Coimbatore) sugarcane varieties that combined high sugar yield with rugged Indian disease and cold resistance. During World War II in London (working through nightly air raids at the John Innes Horticultural Institution and later at Kew Gardens), she co-authored the landmark *Chromosome Atlas of Cultivated Plants* (1945) before returning to India at Jawaharlal Nehru's invitation to reorganise the Botanical Survey of India and fight to save Kerala's Silent Valley rainforest.
