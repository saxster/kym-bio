:::scene Kiel, 1879–1882, Woods Hole and Pasadena, 1954–1958, and Oxford, Mumbai and Kyoto, 1962–2006

In the spring of 1879, German anatomist **Walther Flemming** leaned over a brass microscope at the University of Kiel, staring into the translucent tail fin of a fire salamander larva (*Salamandra maculosa*). For decades, biologists had argued over where new cells came from. Some still suspected that nuclei crystallised spontaneously out of featureless cell sap, like sugar crystals forming in cooling syrup. The trouble was that a living cell nucleus is almost completely transparent; under an ordinary light microscope, its interior looks like clear glass.

### Part 1 · Walther Flemming's crimson threads in Kiel (1879–1882)

**Walther Flemming** tried a chemical tool from the German dye industry: synthetic **aniline stains**. When he soaked fixed salamander gill and fin tissues in crimson basophilic dyes, a hidden architecture blazed into view inside the nucleus. One granular substance drank up the red stain so greedily that **Walther Flemming** named it **chromatin**—from the Greek *khroma*, meaning colour. Because aniline fixing and staining froze each cell at a single instant, he reconstructed the sequence of cell division by sorting hundreds of fixed cells into chronological order, like shuffling still photographs back into a film reel.

What his sequence revealed in his 1882 book *Zellsubstanz, Kern und Zelltheilung* (*Cell Substance, Nucleus and Cell Division*) astonished every microscopist in Europe. Before a cell divided, the diffuse chromatin condensed into distinct threads. Then—the crucial step—every single thread split lengthwise down its middle into two identical twin threads. Fine spindle fibres radiating from two poles caught those twin threads and pulled one complete set to the left and the other complete set to the right, forming two daughter nuclei that were exact structural copies of the parent nucleus. **Walther Flemming** named this thread-splitting dance **mitosis**, from the Greek *mitos* for a weaver’s warp thread. All cell nuclei, he concluded in a Latin aphorism echoing Rudolf Virchow, arise from pre-existing nuclei: *omnis nucleus e nucleo*.

### Part 2 · Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl's heavy-nitrogen centrifuge in Pasadena (1954–1958)

Yet **Walther Flemming** died in 1905 without knowing *how* a chromatin thread could split lengthwise into two identical copies. That molecular mystery waited until April 1953, when James Watson and Francis Crick ended their *Nature* paper on the DNA double helix with a quiet sentence: *"It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material."* Because adenine (`A`) pairs with thymine (`T`) by two hydrogen bonds and cytosine (`C`) pairs with guanine (`G`) by three hydrogen bonds, unzipping the two strands of a parental double helix should let each old strand serve as a template for a new complementary partner.

Not everyone was convinced that a two-metre tangle of DNA could unzip cleanly without snarling. Biologists debated three rival possibilities. In Watson and Crick’s **semi-conservative** model, each daughter DNA molecule keeps one intact parental strand paired with one newly built strand. In a **conservative** model, the original double helix stays intact as a closed master copy while a brand-new double helix is assembled beside it. And in 1954, physicist Max Delbrück proposed a **dispersive** model: perhaps enzymes chop the parental double helix into fragments during copying, so every daughter strand ends up as a patchwork mosaic of old and new DNA pieces.

In the summer of 1954 at Woods Hole, Massachusetts, a twenty-four-year-old Caltech chemistry graduate student named **Matthew Meselson** met a twenty-five-year-old biologist named **Franklin Stahl** while **Franklin Stahl** sat under a giant shade tree serving gin and tonic to seminar attendees. **Matthew Meselson** had just heard a lecture by Jacques Monod and was pondering how to weigh newly synthesised molecules using stable heavy isotopes. Before the afternoon ended, **Meselson and Stahl** agreed to team up at Caltech in Pasadena to test how DNA copies itself.

Their experimental design, published in July 1958, became famous as "the most beautiful experiment in biology." First, **Meselson and Stahl** grew *Escherichia coli* bacteria for fourteen generations at `36 °C` in broth where the only nitrogen source was heavy ammonium chloride (`¹⁵NH₄Cl`). Nitrogen-15 (`¹⁵N`) is not radioactive; it simply carries one extra neutron (`8` instead of `7`), making every purine and pyrimidine ring slightly denser. Then they abruptly switched the bacteria into ordinary light nitrogen (`¹⁴NH₄Cl`) broth and harvested samples every twenty minutes as the cells doubled.

How do you separate DNA carrying `¹⁵N` from DNA carrying `¹⁴N` when the density difference is less than one percent (`1.724 g/cm³` versus `1.710 g/cm³`)? **Matthew Meselson** invented **equilibrium density-gradient ultracentrifugation**. When concentrated **caesium chloride (`CsCl`)** is spun in an analytical ultracentrifuge at `44,770` revolutions per minute—roughly `140,000 × g`—for twenty-four to forty-eight hours, the heavy caesium ions settle against diffusion into a smooth density gradient from top to bottom of the tube. DNA molecules float or sink until their density matches the surrounding `CsCl` solution, forming a sharp band visible under ultraviolet light at `260 nm`.

At Generation `0`, all bacterial DNA settled in one heavy band at `1.724 g/cm³`. After one doubling in light `¹⁴N` broth (Generation `1`), the `1.724 g/cm³` heavy band vanished; in its place stood a single sharp band halfway up the tube at **`1.717 g/cm³`**—100 percent **hybrid (`¹⁵N/¹⁴N`) DNA**. In a single twenty-minute generation, the **conservative** hypothesis was falsified, because conservative copying predicted two separate bands (`50%` heavy at `1.724` and `50%` light at `1.710`) with zero hybrid DNA. Then came the decisive Generation `2` tube after forty minutes: the DNA split into **two equal bands**—`50%` hybrid (`1.717 g/cm³`) and `50%` pure light (`1.710 g/cm³`). That clean split eliminated Delbrück’s **dispersive** hypothesis, which predicted a single blended band at `1.7135 g/cm³`. Semi-conservative unzipping—paired with the proofreading **DNA polymerase** enzyme isolated by Arthur Kornberg in 1956—was proved beyond doubt.

### Part 3 · John Gurdon, Kamal Ranadive, and Shinya Yamanaka (1962–2006)

Yet solving how DNA copies itself in mitosis sharpened a deeper developmental riddle. If mitosis deals an identical copy of all chromosomes to every daughter cell, why does an intestinal cell differ so dramatically from a brain neurone or a pancreatic beta cell? For decades, many embryologists suspected that as embryonic **stem cells** specialise, they permanently discard or ruin the genes they no longer need.

In 1962 at the University of Oxford, zoologist **John Gurdon** tested that assumption in the African clawed frog (*Xenopus laevis*). Using microscopic glass pipettes, **John Gurdon** destroyed the haploid nucleus of an unfertilised frog egg with ultraviolet light and injected in its place the diploid nucleus from a specialised **tadpole intestinal epithelial cell**. Earlier experimenters in leopard frogs had failed and concluded that adult nuclei were irreversibly locked. **John Gurdon** persisted with serial nuclear transfers and watched reprogrammed eggs divide by mitosis into normal swimming tadpoles and fertile adult frogs. A single gut-cell nucleus still held every gene needed to build a brain, a beating heart, an eye lens, and a skeleton. Specialisation had not deleted a single gene; it had merely closed unused chapters of the nuclear library.

In Mumbai during the same era, Indian cell biologist **Kamal Ranadive** established India’s first mammalian tissue-culture laboratory at the Indian Cancer Research Centre in Parel, cultivating human and mouse cells to investigate how mitotic division and tissue specialisation switches go awry in cancer and leprosy. And in 2006 at Kyoto University, former orthopaedic surgeon **Shinya Yamanaka** and Kazutoshi Takahashi asked which molecular switches in the egg reset an adult nucleus. Screening twenty-four candidate genes, **Shinya Yamanaka** discovered that adding just four master transcription-factor genes—**`Oct4`, `Sox2`, `Klf4`, and `c-Myc`**—to adult mouse (and in 2007, human) skin fibroblasts rewound them into **induced pluripotent stem cells (`iPSCs`)** able to form beating heart cells or neurones in a dish. Together, **Walther Flemming**’s crimson threads, **Meselson and Stahl**’s spinning caesium tubes, and **John Gurdon** and **Shinya Yamanaka**’s reprogrammed nuclei proved that every nucleated body cell carries the same master blueprint—copied faithfully before every mitotic division, and read selectively one tissue at a time.
