Chapter 5 followed carbon from air into a leaf's carbohydrates, then through phloem towards roots. Now follow a sugar molecule into a root cell beneath the soil. No sunlight reaches it. There are no lungs. Yet the cell can grow, build proteins and move mineral ions across its membrane.

The fuel has arrived, but delivery is only the beginning. We must follow two accounts together: what happens to its atoms, and how its chemical energy becomes available for particular jobs. Then the same account will help us read a bowl of rising idli batter and a plant standing in waterlogged soil.

## 6.1 Breathing is only the delivery service

Your chest moves as you breathe. A root tip appears still. The tempting wrong idea is “My lungs do respiration; plants have no lungs, so they do something else.” It bundles a delivery system together with the chemistry it supplies.

:::predict
A root cell underground is growing and actively taking up mineral ions. Must it have lungs to obtain usable energy from nutrients?
- Yes; moving air through lungs is the chemical source of ATP
- No; gas delivery and energy-transferring cell reactions are different processes
- No; roots receive finished energy from soil instead of using nutrients
???
The second. A root can receive sugars from other parts of the plant and oxygen from its surroundings without having lungs. Its own enzymes carry out the chemical reactions. Soil is not a supply of finished ATP for the root.
:::

:::evidence A reaction system separated from an animal | From Chapter 4's evidence to this chapter's question
Eduard Buchner's 1897 yeast-juice experiments already gave us a useful clue. A preparation separated from intact yeast cells could still ferment suitable sugar. Neither lungs nor a chest movement were required for that chemical activity.

Enzyme preparations and isolated mitochondrial machinery let researchers investigate chemical steps separately from an animal's breathing. Each preparation contains only part of the living process. Air movement supplies conditions; molecules and enzymes carry out the energy-transferring reactions. For a root, ask how substances reach its cells and what those cells do with them.
:::

In a human, muscles change the volume of the chest, moving air into and out of the lungs. This is **ventilation**. Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross thin, moist surfaces: **gas exchange**. Blood transports gases between lungs and tissues. Then, inside cells, enzyme-controlled reactions break down nutrients and transfer some of the available chemical energy into processes that replenish ATP. These cellular reactions are ==cellular respiration==.

:::note Cellular respiration
Chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen; moving air into lungs is a separate process.
:::

:::fig c6_routes() | Follow the human delivery route, then separate it from the cellular reactions. Roots and microbes can carry out energy-transferring chemistry without lungs. The drawings are enlarged schematics, not a scale comparison.

Taking a breath does not turn sugar into ATP in the lung's air space. Oxygen must reach cells whose proteins connect fuel reactions to ATP formation. Carbon dioxide can travel back to the lungs and leave in exhaled air. Chapter 8 investigates the transport arrangements; here we follow the chemistry.

ATP and its less-phosphorylated partner ADP cycle. Reactions involving ATP can be coupled to muscle contraction, synthesis of proteins, cell division and active transport. Restoring ion differences helps nerve cells keep signalling. Metabolism also releases heat, contributing to temperature maintenance in your body. Food supplies chemical material and fuel; ATP is a repeatedly regenerated participant in energy transfer, rather than a replacement meal.

Respiration does not create energy. Some chemical free energy available from the overall fuel reaction supports work through linked processes; energy also disperses as heat. Recall the sealed garden: matter can return to earlier forms, while light enters and heat eventually leaves.

Glucose will be our first fuel because its carbon is convenient to follow. Cells can also use other nutrients. Nor does every cell have the same equipment. Mature mammalian red blood cells lack mitochondria and obtain ATP through glycolysis, the sugar-splitting route we meet next. Many microbes use routes that do not use oxygen. “Living cells require usable energy” is a sound starting point; “every cell consumes oxygen in a mitochondrion” is too broad.

:::five Cellular respiration
? What is it?
Cell chemistry that breaks down nutrients and releases energy for metabolism, including ATP-supported work.
? Why is it called this?
Respiration comes from a Latin word connected with breathing. Biology uses cellular respiration for the chemistry, so the older everyday meaning can mislead.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains how supplied nutrients support work inside a cell, including a root cell without lungs.
? Why does it work?
Enzymes connect favourable fuel reactions to energy transfers, ATP regeneration and particular cellular tasks.
? How does it help?
You can distinguish a delivery failure from a failure of the chemistry that uses the delivered substances.
?? How do we know?
Cell preparations, isolated enzymes and organelle studies reveal chemical activity apart from an animal's breathing movements.
?? What it isn't
Ventilation, energy creation or a process confined to animals.
?? Where does it stop working?
The broad definition does not specify one pathway. Oxygen use, organelles and fuels vary between cells and conditions.
:::

:::recall 5
A lit green leaf releases oxygen overall. Does that mean its respiration has stopped?
???
No. Photosynthesis can produce oxygen faster than respiration consumes it, leaving a net release. The leaf can carry out both processes at once. Roots supplied by the leaf also continue their metabolism without receiving light.
:::

:::careful
> “Plants make food by day and only start respiration at night.”

Living plant cells require a continuing usable energy supply in light and darkness. Photosynthesis adds another process in suitable illuminated tissues; it does not switch respiration off. Root cells can use carbohydrates delivered from those tissues.
:::

:::school
0610 Topic 12 distinguishes cellular respiration from breathing and asks for uses of released energy, including contraction, synthesis, division, active transport, growth, nerve impulses and temperature maintenance. Use “releases energy” with its source understood: chemical reactions transfer energy; they do not create it.
:::

:::check
Explain how a growing root can respire without lungs. Include both a supply route and a cellular job.
???
Sugars can reach the root through phloem, and molecular oxygen can diffuse towards root cells from their surroundings when available. In aerobic respiration the cells use nutrients and oxygen in linked reactions that support ATP formation. ATP-supported processes include active mineral-ion transport and synthesis for growth. Lungs are one gas-delivery arrangement, not a requirement for the chemistry itself.
:::

## 6.2 A membrane between the fuel and the work

“Mitochondria make ATP” gives us an address. How can changing the chemical partners of carbon atoms drive a protein elsewhere?

The tempting picture is a miniature fire: oxygen hits sugar, heat flashes out, and a spinning part collects it. There is no flame. Enzymes control small chemical steps, with carriers and a membrane linking reactions.

:::predict
Suppose the inner mitochondrial membrane becomes freely leaky to hydrogen ions. Proteins still transfer electrons and pump those ions. What happens to the useful difference across the membrane?
- Pumping guarantees the same maintained difference however quickly ions leak back
- The difference becomes harder to maintain, so normal ATP production through that relay is impaired
- The leaking ions turn directly into carbon dioxide
???
The second. Pumping separates ions; unrestricted return undoes the separation. Electron transfer and normal ATP production need not remain coupled if the membrane cannot hold the difference.
:::

:::evidence The missing link was a membrane difference | 1961 and 1997
Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961 that fuel oxidation and ATP formation could be coupled indirectly. Electron transfers could establish a hydrogen-ion difference across a membrane; return through suitable machinery could then support ATP formation. The stored difference includes both concentration and electric potential. His chemiosmotic theory was recognised by the 1978 Chemistry Nobel Prize.

The moving machinery required further evidence. In a paper published in 1997, Hiroyuki Noji, Ryohei Yasuda, Masasuke Yoshida and Kazuhiko Kinosita Jr reported attaching a fluorescent actin filament to the central shaft of an isolated ATP-breaking part of the enzyme. The visible filament turned. Supplied ATP drove this demonstration in the ATP-breakdown direction: it was evidence for rotation, rather than a film of an entire mitochondrion making ATP from proton flow.
:::

Begin outside the mitochondrion, in the cytoplasm. A six-carbon glucose is processed into two three-carbon molecules called **pyruvate**. The pathway first invests ATP and later forms ATP: four formed minus two invested leaves **two net ATP per glucose** in the route we are teaching. Some fuel electrons are transferred to a reusable carrier. This sugar-splitting sequence is ==glycolysis==.

:::note Glycolysis
An enzyme-controlled pathway in the cytoplasm that processes glucose into two pyruvate molecules. The usual taught route yields two net ATP and transfers electrons to NAD⁺, forming NADH.
:::

The carrier comes in two forms. **NAD⁺** can receive electrons during these reactions; the electron-carrying form is **NADH**. Think of two states of the same reusable chemical participant, rather than two substances supplied without limit. NADH must later pass electrons onwards so that NAD⁺ becomes available again. We will test that requirement in section 6.4.

:::five Glycolysis
? What is it?
A cytoplasmic sequence processing one glucose into two pyruvates, with a net ATP gain and electron transfer to a carrier.
? Why is it called this?
The name joins a sugar-related part with a Greek part meaning loosening or splitting.
? What problem did it solve?
It begins transferring energy from glucose without requiring a mitochondrion or an oxygen-using step in the pathway itself.
? Why does it work?
Linked enzyme reactions invest ATP, rearrange carbon compounds, transfer electrons and subsequently form more ATP than they invested.
? How does it help?
It supplies a common starting point for the aerobic and fermentation routes, so their differences become easier to follow.
?? How do we know?
Studies of enzyme reactions and intermediates establish the pathway and its material and ATP accounts.
?? What it isn't
A single sugar snapping in half, complete oxidation to carbon dioxide or proof that carriers never need recycling.
?? Where does it stop working?
Continued operation requires substrates and a supply of the reusable carrier form. An ATP count alone does not supply those conditions.
:::

For our aerobic eukaryotic cell, pyruvate enters a mitochondrion. Reactions in its inner fluid compartment, the **matrix**, remove one carbon from each three-carbon pyruvate as CO₂. The remaining two-carbon unit joins a repeating reaction cycle; the entry stage is called **pyruvate oxidation**, and the cycle is the **citric acid cycle**. These reactions transfer more electrons to carriers.

Further processing releases the remaining fuel carbon as CO₂. Across complete glucose oxidation, its six carbons can therefore reach six CO₂ molecules. This is the eventual carbon account, not a claim that every newly arriving carbon leaves during its first turn through the cycle. Carbon release and electron collection are the jobs to understand before memorising the pathway names.

Here “oxidation” means loss of electrons from a substance. It does not require an oxygen molecule to collide directly with that substance. Follow the electrons separately from the carbon: fuel carbon can reach CO₂ in one part of the system while carriers convey electrons towards the inner membrane.

:::fig c6_respiration() | The carbon route and electron route answer different questions. The net equation balances material, while the pathway map shows intermediate connections. It does not assign the origin of each product oxygen atom.

Proteins in the inner membrane pass electrons along a chain of chemical transfers. At its end, molecular oxygen accepts electrons and hydrogen ions, forming water. Oxygen is therefore a participant at the end of the **electron transport chain**, rather than a substance breathed in solely to turn into carbon dioxide. Without an available final recipient, this aerobic chain cannot keep passing electrons onwards normally.

Some transfers along the chain release enough free energy to drive proteins that move hydrogen ions, H⁺, **from the matrix into the intermembrane space**. That space lies between the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes. The inner membrane restricts free H⁺ passage, allowing pumping to maintain a difference.

H⁺ carries positive charge. Its concentration difference and the electric potential difference both affect its movement; together they form an **electrochemical gradient**. Chapter 3 used such a gradient to help transport substances; here its energy serves ATP formation.

Now provide a controlled return route. H⁺ can move **from the intermembrane space back into the matrix** through the membrane part of **ATP synthase**. This movement drives rotation linked to shape changes in the enzyme's catalytic head on the matrix side. Those changes support binding, formation and release of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate, Pᵢ. The head does not have to spin as a whole: rotating and stationary parts interact.

The link from downhill ion movement to ATP synthesis is ==chemiosmosis==. Read the causal chain aloud: **fuel transfers electrons → electron transfer supports H⁺ pumping → the membrane holds a difference → H⁺ return drives changes in ATP synthase → ATP becomes available**. The arrows connect different physical events. A proton is not an electron, and neither turns into an ATP molecule.

:::note Chemiosmosis
Coupling ion movement down an electrochemical gradient across a membrane to ATP synthesis. In this mitochondrial example, H⁺ returns to the matrix through ATP synthase after electron-transfer reactions support outward pumping.
:::

:::fig c6_membrane() | Matrix below, intermembrane space above. Trace pumping outwards, then return through ATP synthase. Electron transfer and carbon dioxide formation have separate routes. Positions and tokens are schematic; they give no speed or proton-to-ATP ratio.
:::live respiration_relay {"lesson":true}

The explorer's manual stages change explanatory focus; real reactions overlap. Its sustained lack-of-oxygen comparison is **after residual gradients have dissipated**. Removing oxygen does not instantly erase all ATP; glycolysis can still supply some if its carriers are regenerated.

:::five Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase
? What is it?
An energy relay using an ion difference across a membrane and controlled ion return to support ATP formation.
? Why is it called this?
“Synthase” names an enzyme associated with synthesis. Chemiosmosis names the membrane-linked coupling described here; the mechanism is in the pumping and return steps.
? What problem did it solve?
It connects fuel reactions to ATP formation without requiring one direct collision between fuel and the ATP-making enzyme.
? Why does it work?
Electron transfers support H⁺ pumping. The membrane preserves electrochemical energy, and H⁺ return drives rotation and catalytic shape changes.
? How does it help?
It explains why oxygen supply, an intact inner membrane and working proteins can each affect aerobic ATP production.
?? How do we know?
Membrane and biochemical studies establish coupling; structural evidence and the fluorescent pointer experiment support the rotary mechanism.
?? What it isn't
All ATP production, H⁺ turning into ATP or a fixed-speed turbine in every cell.
?? Where does it stop working?
Leaks, unavailable electron recipients or damaged machinery impair this relay. The schematic does not calculate a universal ATP yield.
:::

:::story rotary-pointer

For the overall **aerobic glucose** account, we can now write:

:::eq
glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
:::

:::eq
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O
:::

There are six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and eighteen oxygen atoms on each side. This is a net material summary. It leaves out carrier recycling, membranes, intermediate reactions and ATP/ADP cycling. Its layout cannot tell you the path of each individual oxygen atom, just as Chapter 5's net photosynthesis equation could not establish the source of released oxygen.

:::recall 4
Does an ATP-using enzyme obtain energy simply by breaking one special bond?
???
No. Breaking an isolated chemical bond requires energy. The whole ATP hydrolysis reaction can release free energy because the resulting products and their interactions in water are more stable under the relevant conditions. Coupling that overall change to another process can support work; the enzyme does not create the energy.
:::

:::careful
> “Oxygen meets sugar in the lungs, and all the energy comes out when a bond snaps.”

Fuel processing happens through cellular pathways. Carbon dioxide is released during carbon-processing reactions; oxygen accepts electrons and protons to form water at the chain's end. ATP-supported work depends on complete reaction and coupling accounts, not energy released by snapping an isolated bond.
:::

:::school
0610 Topic 12.2 uses oxygen-dependent nutrient breakdown and the aerobic word equation; Supplement adds the balanced glucose equation. The membrane relay explains how that overall account supports ATP formation, without assigning one fixed ATP yield to every cell.
:::

:::check
An inner membrane becomes freely leaky to H⁺. Explain why observing continued electron transfer would not prove that normal ATP synthesis continues.
???
Electron transfer can still provide energy for pumping, but a leak lets H⁺ return without using ATP synthase. The membrane cannot maintain the usual useful electrochemical difference. The link between oxidation and ATP synthesis is therefore impaired even if some electron transfer continues; glycolytic ATP is a separate contribution.
:::

## 6.3 The atoms do not turn into heat

Put a representative fat molecule into our chemical account. After complete oxidation, the original fat is no longer there. Where is its material?

One tempting answer is “It became energy.” Warm surroundings encourage that picture. But transferring energy is not an explanation for missing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms. In the chemistry being described, those atoms have new partners. They must appear in the products.

:::predict
A mathematical sample containing 1 kg of fat is completely oxidised. Can the total products weigh more than 1 kg?
- No; that would create matter
- Yes; incoming oxygen contributes mass to the products
- Yes; ATP turns heat into extra carbon atoms
???
The second. Compare every input with every output. Weighing the fuel alone leaves oxygen off the input side. The mass of all products can exceed the starting fuel mass while the complete account still balances.
:::

:::evidence Two different questions behind “where did it go?” | Meerman and Brown, 2014
Ruben Meerman and Andrew Brown examined this misconception in a 2014 BMJ article. They represented triglyceride fat by C₅₅H₁₀₄O₆ and balanced its complete oxidation. The products contain the fuel atoms plus substantial material from incoming oxygen.

They then asked a different question: how much of the **original fat's mass** is assigned to the carbon dioxide route and how much to water? Their approximate calculation gave 84% and 16%. That is not the ratio of total CO₂ to total water produced. Their oxygen allocation also uses an oxygen-exchange argument involving body water; it cannot be deduced from the balanced equation alone. Keeping the two questions separate prevents a striking percentage from becoming a new misconception.
:::

A triglyceride contains glycerol linked to three fatty-acid components, as in Chapter 4. Actual fats are mixtures of different triglycerides. Here C₅₅H₁₀₄O₆ is one **representative model**, and ==complete oxidation== means processing its carbon to CO₂ in the overall account, with hydrogen contributing to water. We are following a sample, not prescribing a change to anyone's body.

:::note Complete oxidation
For the carbon fuels modelled here, an overall reaction yielding carbon dioxide and water. It is a material endpoint account, not one reaction step or a measurement of instantaneous body-mass change.
:::

:::eq
C₅₅H₁₀₄O₆ + 78O₂ → 55CO₂ + 52H₂O
:::

Count first. There are 55 carbon atoms on either side. The 104 hydrogen atoms supply 52 waters. Oxygen requires more care: the fuel contributes six oxygen atoms, and 78 O₂ molecules add 156. The total is 162, matching 110 in CO₂ plus 52 in water. No carbon has become heat. Energy transfer belongs alongside this material account.

For transparent school arithmetic, choose nominal relative atomic masses C = 12, H = 1 and O = 16. These rounded inputs give a representative fuel formula mass of **860**, rather than the slightly different value obtained with more precise atomic weights.

:::work
Representative fuel: 55 × 12 + 104 × 1 + 6 × 16 = 860.
Oxygen input: 78 × 32 = 2496.
Carbon dioxide output: 55 × 44 = 2420.
Water output: 52 × 18 = 936.
Total input: 860 + 2496 = 3356.
Total output: 2420 + 936 = 3356.
:::

Divide these proportional masses by 860 for a **1 kg mathematical fuel sample**: about **2.9023 kg oxygen enters**, **2.8140 kg total CO₂** forms and **1.0884 kg total water** forms. Unrounded input and output totals are equal. Separately rounded entries may differ in the final decimal place when added; calculate totals before rounding.

:::fig c6_mass() | Two ledgers share one reaction. Total products include incoming oxygen. The separate 84%/16% allocation concerns only the original fuel and uses the paper's oxygen-exchange assumption; it is not a unique tagged-atom route.
:::live oxidation_ledger {"lesson":true}

Now close the first ledger and ask only about material originally in the fuel. Its 55 carbon atoms all reach CO₂ in this complete-oxidation model. Its hydrogen contributes to water. Oxygen atoms exchange between body water and respiratory carbon dioxide, so assigning the original six fuel oxygen atoms requires more than balancing symbols.

Meerman and Brown's approximate account assigns **four of those six oxygen atoms to CO₂ and two to water**. With our nominal masses, the original-fuel contribution assigned to CO₂ is (55 × 12 + 4 × 16) / 860, about **84.2%**. The contribution assigned to water is (104 + 2 × 16) / 860, about **15.8%**. The familiar rounded statement is 84% and 16%: roughly 840 g and 160 g per kilogram of original fuel. More closely, our arithmetic gives about 842 g and 158 g.

That 840 g is **not total CO₂**, which contains incoming oxygen too. The allocation is an approximate accounting model informed by oxygen exchange, rather than a film of six marked oxygen atoms on predetermined paths. The equation fixes amounts, not every atom's origin.

Water formed in metabolism joins body water and can leave later through several routes, including urine, sweat and water vapour. Carbon dioxide can reach the lungs and be exhaled. Choosing to breathe faster does not force a matching increase in fuel oxidation. The model predicts neither a person's scale reading nor a timescale. Fat is also essential biological material, with storage and structural roles; this calculation is a lesson about matter, not a body target.

:::five Fuel mass and its products
? What is it?
A complete material account of fuel plus incoming oxygen becoming carbon dioxide and water, with a separate original-fuel allocation.
? Why is it called this?
An input is material entering the chosen account; a product is material formed. “Original fuel” deliberately excludes incoming oxygen.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains where fuel atoms go without claiming that mass becomes heat or confusing fuel contribution with total product mass.
? Why does it work?
The balanced equation conserves atoms. Formula masses convert those counts into proportional mass amounts.
? How does it help?
You can test a missing-mass claim by looking for omitted inputs and distinguish the two meanings of a product's mass.
?? How do we know?
Chemical stoichiometry establishes the total account. The approximate oxygen-origin assignment additionally draws on oxygen exchange described in the paper.
?? What it isn't
A diet, a prediction of body-mass change, a breathing exercise or a claim that total CO₂ is only 84% of fuel mass.
?? Where does it stop working?
Real fuel mixtures differ. Partial metabolism, water storage and changing boundaries require fuller accounts than this representative complete-oxidation model.
:::

:::careful
> “One kilogram becomes 840 g of carbon dioxide, so the rest of that equation is unnecessary.”

You have substituted an original-fuel contribution for a total product. Incoming oxygen is necessary in the material account. For this model, total CO₂ is about 2.8140 kg per kilogram of fuel, while the assigned original-fuel contribution to that CO₂ is about 0.842 kg. The two quantities answer different questions.
:::

:::school
The 0610 aerobic equation is a useful balanced starting point. Whenever a question asks where matter goes, account for gases as well as solids and liquids; energy release does not remove the requirement to balance the atoms.
:::

:::check
Someone labels 0.84 kg “total CO₂” for complete oxidation of a 1 kg representative fat sample. Identify the mistake and name the missing input.
???
About 0.84 kg is the rounded portion of the original fuel assigned to the CO₂ route in the approximate allocation. It is not the whole CO₂ product. Incoming oxygen contributes additional mass; the complete model gives about 2.9023 kg oxygen input and 2.8140 kg total CO₂, with water completing the balanced ledger.
:::

## 6.4 Keeping the carrier ready for another round

Suppose the aerobic electron route cannot keep pace with glycolysis, or oxygen is unavailable. Why should that affect sugar-splitting enzymes elsewhere?

The tempting answer is “There is still sugar, so splitting can continue forever.” But glycolysis also transfers electrons to NAD⁺. A finite supply cannot keep accepting electrons if it remains in the NADH form.

:::predict
A cell has glucose and functioning glycolytic enzymes, but NADH cannot pass its electrons onwards. What eventually restricts continued glycolysis?
- The available NAD⁺ form becomes too scarce
- Carbon atoms change into oxygen
- ATP manufacture becomes unlimited because electrons have stopped moving
???
The first. Electron collection converts NAD⁺ to NADH. Passing electrons onwards regenerates NAD⁺. A pathway using a reusable participant depends on its return, just as a delivery route depends on containers becoming available again.
:::

:::evidence Bubbles are one clue; tracing carbon gives another | Idli studies and a lactate tracer
A 1965 study by Mukherjee and colleagues investigated the microbes in idli batter and identified Leuconostoc mesenteroides as a principal gas-producing and souring organism in the batters studied. Later starter-culture research used several bacteria and a yeast. The evidence supports microbial fermentation and varying communities, not a claim that every bowl contains one identical organism or follows one yeast equation.

Human metabolism needs a different clue. In a 1986 study, Mazzeo and colleagues followed labelled lactate carbon and detected its contribution to expired carbon dioxide at rest and during exercise. Lactate could therefore be used as fuel. Seeing it form does not establish that it is useless waste, that oxygen is entirely absent or that it explains next-day soreness.
:::

One solution transfers NADH's electrons to an organic recipient made from the fuel pathway. NAD⁺ becomes available again. These recycling reactions are ==fermentation==. They keep glycolysis supplied with its electron-accepting carrier form. They do not add another ATP harvest at the recycling step: in the glucose route described here, **four ATP formed − two invested = two net ATP**, all belonging to glycolysis.

:::note Fermentation
Pathways that restore NAD⁺ by passing electrons from NADH to organic recipients. This allows glycolysis to continue without using an oxygen-dependent electron chain; the recycling step itself adds no further ATP in the routes taught here.
:::

There are several possible recipients and products. In the familiar **yeast alcoholic-fermentation route**, pyruvate is processed with CO₂ release, and the remaining organic compound accepts electrons to form ethanol. Two pyruvates from one glucose supply two ethanols and two CO₂ molecules. Much chemical energy remains in the ethanol, so this route captures less usable energy per glucose than complete aerobic oxidation.

:::eq
glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
:::

:::eq
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂
:::

Four carbons reach ethanol and two reach CO₂. This product summary omits ATP and carriers; it does not show one collision. Gas trapped in dough expands its volume. That explains the observation more specifically than saying yeast “makes energy”.

:::fig c6_fermentation() | Every route must restore the carrier form used by glycolysis. The two net ATP belong to glycolysis; recycling adds no extra ATP here. Yeast products and lactate products differ, and mixed-community idli is not assigned one universal equation.
:::live carrier_routes {"lesson":true}

:::five Fermentation and carrier recycling
? What is it?
Electron transfer to organic products that regenerates NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis and its small ATP gain to continue.
? Why is it called this?
Fermentation comes from a Latin word connected with leavening or causing to rise. Some fermentation releases gas, but the name does not mean all routes bubble.
? What problem did it solve?
It supplies an onward electron route when an oxygen-dependent chain is unavailable or cannot meet the pathway's demand for carrier regeneration.
? Why does it work?
Passing electrons from NADH to an organic recipient restores NAD⁺ for another round of glycolytic electron collection.
? How does it help?
It links a molecular recycling requirement to yeast products, food fermentation and lactate formation.
?? How do we know?
Enzyme studies establish carriers and products; microbial studies test which organisms and pathways contribute in particular foods.
?? What it isn't
An extra two ATP added after glycolysis, a rule that all fermentation produces alcohol or a species test based on bubbles.
?? Where does it stop working?
Continued activity depends on fuel, functional enzymes and suitable conditions. Product accumulation and the organism's other requirements also matter.
:::

In rice–dal idli batter, trapped gas can cause rising and microbial acids contribute to souring. Different bacteria and yeasts can contribute under different conditions. The 1965 finding gives one real bacterial example.

Height reflects **retained gas and batter structure**. It does not measure total gas production, identify microbes or prove alcoholic fermentation alone. Ingredients, preparation and temperature change the outcome; no particular overnight rise is guaranteed.

:::home Watch the batter, keep the inference small
An adult must manage this observation during an ordinary preparation of familiar idli batter. Ask them to set aside a small observation portion in a clean transparent container with room above it and a loose cover. Put a clean mark outside at the initial batter level. Record the starting time and later observations during the adult's normal preparation schedule; look from outside for a level change or visible gas pockets.

The observation portion is not for eating. Do not taste raw batter, add experimental starter cultures, seal the container, sniff it closely or deliberately keep it beyond the adult's normal preparation period. Leave cooking equipment and the main food preparation to the adult. If there is unexpected colour, mould or another sign that concerns the adult, stop and let them discard it without further investigation. They should dispose of the observation portion and clean the container; wash your hands afterwards.

Sketch the changes; no visible rise is a valid observation. Could gas have escaped or remained dissolved? The marked level helps compare expansion, without identifying an organism or calibrating a rate. This proposed observation was not physically trialled for the book.
:::

Muscle has another route. Pyruvate accepts electrons from NADH and becomes ==lactate==, restoring NAD⁺. The glucose-to-lactate route releases **no CO₂**. The contrast with yeast is useful: both restore the carrier, but their products and gas accounts differ.

:::note Lactate
An organic product formed when pyruvate receives electrons during NAD⁺ regeneration. It can be transported and used metabolically, including oxidation as fuel; its production does not require complete absence of oxygen.
:::

During vigorous activity, glycolysis can operate rapidly and lactate formation can help restore the carrier. Aerobic processing can continue alongside it. There is no universal moment when a muscle switches wholly from oxygen use to fermentation. Lactate can travel to other tissues and be oxidised, or contribute to glucose production. Its fate depends on the surrounding metabolic conditions.

:::five Lactate
? What is it?
A product of electron transfer to pyruvate, linked to restoration of NAD⁺.
? Why is it called this?
Lactate is the ion corresponding to lactic acid. At usual cellular pH, lactate is the main form; the school word equation often says lactic acid.
? What problem did it solve?
The reaction provides a rapid carrier-regeneration route so glycolysis can continue supplying ATP.
? Why does it work?
Pyruvate receives electrons from NADH, leaving NAD⁺ available for further glycolytic reactions.
? How does it help?
It explains continued rapid sugar processing without treating lactate as merely poison or assuming oxygen has vanished completely.
?? How do we know?
Biochemical measurements establish the reaction. Labelled-carbon studies show that lactate carbon can later appear in expired CO₂.
?? What it isn't
The CO₂-producing yeast route, proof of zero oxygen or an explanation of next-day muscle soreness.
?? Where does it stop working?
This one reaction does not describe all exercise, fatigue or recovery. Lactate transport and use involve other tissues and pathways.
:::

A teacher-led school investigation can examine temperature using food-grade baker's yeast and sugar. Compare fresh equal portions at teacher-selected safe temperatures, keeping yeast amount, sugar concentration, liquid volume and observation interval the same. Include a room-temperature comparison and a no-yeast control, measure temperature rather than calling everything “warm”, and repeat trials. A teacher can choose suitable vented gas-collection apparatus; avoid pressure build-up, flames, tasting or household chemical substitutes.

Collected gas volume over an interval is more useful than foam height, but leaks, dissolution and gas temperature still matter. CO₂ collection alone cannot establish that fermentation is the only pathway or that oxygen is absent. The design gives no universal optimum. Rewarming heat-damaged yeast differs from testing a fresh sample at that temperature: Chapter 4's distinction between current conditions and previous damage applies again.

:::careful
> “Without oxygen, cells either stop instantly or produce the same gas and waste everywhere.”

Carrier-regenerating routes can support continued glycolysis. Yeast's taught route releases CO₂; the glucose-to-lactate route does not. Lactate can also form while oxygen is present and can be reused. Neither observation establishes a universal all-or-nothing oxygen switch.
:::

:::school
0610 Topic 12.3 groups the yeast and muscle examples under **anaerobic respiration** and uses “glucose → alcohol + carbon dioxide” and “glucose → lactic acid”; Supplement adds the balanced yeast equation. It also uses **oxygen debt**, with continued rapid heart action carrying lactic acid towards the liver and deeper, faster breathing supplying oxygen for its aerobic breakdown there. Learn that exam account, while retaining the fuller correction: lactate has other destinations, can be oxidised in other tissues, can form with oxygen present, and does not account for every part of recovery oxygen use.
:::

In wider biochemistry, **anaerobic respiration** can also mean electron transport using a final acceptor other than oxygen. Fermentation restores carriers through organic recipients without that respiratory electron chain. The distinction concerns the mechanism; the school's equations remain useful.

:::check
Two pathways regenerate NAD⁺ after glycolysis. One forms ethanol and CO₂; the other forms lactate. Does seeing no gas prove there was no ATP supply? Explain the shared requirement and the different carbon outcomes.
???
No. Glycolysis can supply two net ATP per glucose while the lactate route restores NAD⁺ without releasing CO₂. Yeast's taught alcoholic route restores the carrier too, but splits carbon between ethanol and carbon dioxide. The shared function is carrier regeneration; visible gas depends on the pathway and on collection, so its absence is not a direct ATP measurement. In broader terminology, anaerobic electron-chain respiration is a separate mechanism from these fermentation routes.
:::

## The waterlogged-root case

:::fig c6_roots() | Flooded soil can restrict oxygen delivery to roots, despite abundant water. Rice provides a contrasting example of internal gas routes; these are representative schematics, not a prediction of survival time or a universal plant design.

:::case Water everywhere, oxygen in short supply
This is a fictional reasoning case. After prolonged rain, a potted plant stands in waterlogged soil. Its leaves remain above water in daylight. A pupil says: “The roots have sugar from the leaves and plenty of H₂O. Each water molecule contains oxygen, so aerobic respiration cannot be short of oxygen.” Another says: “Then fermentation will supply exactly the same ATP and the plant will be fine indefinitely.”

Identify the missing distinction in each claim. Why might waterlogging impair ATP-supported root functions? Why should a comparison with rice make you cautious about predicting the same outcome for every plant?
???
Roots require a route for **molecular oxygen, O₂**, to reach the reactions that use it. Oxygen chemically bound within H₂O is not free O₂ for the aerobic chain. O₂ diffuses much more slowly through water-filled paths than through connected air spaces. When water fills soil pores, delivery can fall behind the continuing oxygen use by roots and microbes. Sugar alone cannot maintain the usual aerobic relay without its final electron recipient.

Fermentation can restore carriers and support continued glycolysis, but the taught glucose route gives a much smaller ATP return than complete aerobic processing. It does not guarantee that every root function or long-term survival can be maintained. Active ion transport and growth can suffer when the usable energy supply is restricted.

Plants differ. Rice has internal gas spaces and other root features that can improve oxygen movement towards submerged tissues. Such adaptations change the delivery problem; they do not abolish respiration's chemical requirements. Water depth, duration, temperature, plant condition and species matter. No fixed survival time follows from the drawing. This case is for reasoning, not a request to flood a houseplant or grow a sealed culture.
:::
