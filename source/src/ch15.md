At the close of Chapter 14, we stood at the edge of two worlds: the four-billion-year filter of natural selection (**Charles Robert Darwin**, **Alfred Russel Wallace**, **Salvador Luria**, and **Birbal Sahni**) shaping wild populations across forests, creeks, and hospital wards, and the new human power to steer evolution deliberately. Every organism we have met since Chapter 0—from the dry chickpea seed waking on a monsoon window-ledge to the spotted deer, mangroves, and dividing bacteria of Mumbai—lives inside a shared planetary household.

In this final chapter of *The Living World*, we follow two ecological rules that govern that household (`Cambridge 0893 Stages 7–9` and `Cambridge 0610 Topics 19, 20 & 21`): **energy flows one way** from the Sun to heat, **matter moves between reservoirs and can be reused** through air, water, soil, and cells, ; we then explore how the **nearly universal genetic code** that evolved over billions of years can now be spliced into bacterial plasmids and edited letter by letter.

## 15.1 Sunlit chains & an energy budget: food webs, ecological pyramids and population growth

:::scene Sanjay Gandhi National Park (SGNP), Borivali & Oval Maidan, Mumbai

It is early October in **Sanjay Gandhi National Park (SGNP)** in Borivali, at the northern edge of Mumbai, just after the monsoon rains have triggered the once-in-eight-years mass flowering of the *Karvi* shrub (*Strobilanthes callosa*). Purple-blue blossoms carpet the basalt hillsides below the Kanheri Caves. Honeybees and carpenter bees hum from corolla to corolla; caterpillars chew the tender green leaves; a praying mantis waits motionless on a stem; a rufous treepie and a spotted owlet watch from a teak branch; and in the dappled shade below, a herd of spotted deer (*Axis axis*) grazes while an Indian leopard (*Panthera pardus fusca*) rests on a warm boulder overhead. Beneath their hooves, white threads of bracket fungi and billions of soil bacteria quietly digest fallen petals, shed antlers, and deer droppings back into dark forest humus. Across town at Oval Maidan, a single centuries-old **banyan tree** (*Ficus benghalensis*) spreads its aerial prop roots like a miniature forest of its own, feeding thousands of fig wasps, caterpillars, and fruit bats from one massive woody trunk.

A group of organisms of the **same species**, living in the same area at the same time, is a ==population==: for example, the spotted deer in SGNP. The populations of different species living and interacting there form the ==community==.

Now add the community’s surroundings—sunlight, basalt rock, rainwater, soil minerals and air—and their interactions. Together these form an ==ecosystem==. An ecosystem has a study boundary, but water, organisms and matter can move across it.

The particular place where an organism lives is its ==habitat==: the damp litter beneath the trees or the twigs high in the canopy. Habitat describes a place; population, community and ecosystem describe different ways of studying what lives and interacts there.

To trace how an ecosystem stays alive, we must distinguish two quantities that never behave the same way: **energy** and **matter**. The **Sun** is the principal source of energy input to biological systems. In Chapter 5 (`5.1–5.3`), after **Jan Baptista van Helmont** weighed his willow tree and **Jan Ingenhousz** watched sunlit leaves bubble oxygen, we saw how chlorophyll inside green plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria traps **light energy** from the Sun and transfers it into **chemical energy** stored in the covalent bonds of glucose (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`). When animals eat plants, or predators eat prey, that stored chemical energy is passed from one organism to the next. At every step, organisms oxidise glucose in **aerobic respiration** (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`, Chapter 6 `6.2`) to power muscle contraction, active transport, nerve impulses, and protein synthesis, transferring energy to the surroundings as **thermal energy (heat)**. Once energy has radiated into the air as low-grade heat, **no plant or chloroplast can ever trap it again**. Energy therefore flows through ecosystems **in one direction only** (`non-cyclical: Sun → producers → consumers and decomposers → dissipated heat`). By contrast, the **atoms of elements** such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus and magnesium move between organisms and their surroundings. Chemical forms change along the way: nitrogen may travel as `N₂`, `NH₄⁺`, `NO₃⁻` or part of a protein. An individual ecosystem exchanges matter with other places; Earth is approximately closed to matter on this scale.

### Food chains, food webs, and the exact meaning of the arrow

A ==food chain== is a diagram showing the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer:

**Karvi shrub (Producer) → Spotted deer (Primary consumer) → Indian leopard (Secondary consumer)**

Look closely at the arrows (`→`). In ecology and in every Cambridge examination, **an arrow in a food chain or food web points in the direction of energy transfer**—from the organism being eaten into the body of the organism that eats it (`Grass → Deer`, never `Deer → Grass`). Because most animals eat more than one kind of food and are eaten by more than one predator, real ecosystems are woven into a ==food web==—a network of interconnected food chains. An organism’s feeding position can differ between chains in a food web. Each position is called a ==trophic level== (`from the Greek trophē, meaning nourishment`):

| Trophic Level & Role | Syllabus Definition (`0610 19.2`) | SGNP & Mumbai Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1: ==Producer==** | An organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis. | *Karvi* shrubs, grasses, *Ficus benghalensis* (banyan tree), creek phytoplankton. |
| **Level 2: ==Primary consumer==** | An organism that gets its energy by feeding on producers; classified by diet as a ==herbivore== (an animal that gets its energy by eating plants). | Spotted deer, caterpillars, fig wasps, fruit bats, zooplankton. |
| **Level 3: ==Secondary consumer==** | An organism that gets its energy by feeding on primary consumers; may be a ==carnivore== (an animal that gets its energy by eating other animals) or an ==omnivore== (eating both plants and animals). | Praying mantids, tree frogs, rufous treepies, barn owls, Indian leopards (when eating deer). |
| **Level 4: ==Tertiary consumer==** / **Level 5: ==Quaternary consumer==** | Predators that feed on secondary consumers (`Level 4`) or tertiary consumers (`Level 5`, apex predators). | Crested serpent eagles eating tree frogs or rat snakes; Indian pythons. |
| **==Decomposer==** | An organism (principally saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material (`faeces`, `urea`, `dead leaves`, `carcasses`). | Bracket fungi, soil mould, putrefying bacteria in forest humus and creek mud. |

### Comparing pyramids of numbers, biomass, and energy

Ecologists use three different ecological pyramids to quantify trophic levels (`Level 1` always at the bottom bar, stepping upward to `Level 2`, `Level 3`, and `Level 4`):

1. **==Pyramid of numbers==:** Bar width shows the **number of individual organisms** at each trophic level in a fixed area. *Advantage:* Fast and non-destructive to count in the field. *Disadvantage:* Ignores organism size (`1` banyan tree counts the same as `1` aphid), so the pyramid is **inverted or spindle-shaped** whenever a single large producer supports thousands of small herbivores (`Tree → Caterpillars → Songbirds`), or when hundreds of fleas or protozoan parasites feed on a single bird or mammal (`L4 > L3`).
2. **==Pyramid of biomass==:** Bar width shows the dry mass of living material (`g m⁻²` or `kg ha⁻¹`) present at each trophic level at a single moment in time (**standing crop**). Organisms are dried in an oven at `~80 °C` until constant mass so that variable water content (which adds mass without supplying respiratory fuel) does not distort the measurement. *Advantage:* Overcomes the size distortion of pyramids of numbers; on land, pyramids of biomass are **almost always upright** because producer dry mass exceeds consumer dry mass. *Disadvantage:* Destructive (requires killing and oven-drying samples) and measures only a **single snapshot in time**, not the rate of production.
3. **==Pyramid of energy==:** Bar width shows the **rate of energy flow** (`kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`) passing through each trophic level over a full year. *Advantage:* **Upright when the same area, period and complete food-source boundary are used**: respiration dissipates some energy as heat and not all production becomes new biomass at the next level. Imported food must also be counted; comparing a local producer patch with consumers feeding outside it would mix different boundaries. *Disadvantage:* Difficult and time-consuming to measure calorific values and respiration rates of every species across twelve months.

### Following the same quantity: production, feeding losses and food supply

Follow an energy budget carefully. **Trophic transfer efficiency** here means the energy incorporated into new biomass at one feeding level divided by that incorporated into new biomass at the level below, over the same area and period. If we assume `10%`, the other `90%` is unavailable to the next consumer level through the following routes. Energy is conserved: some is dissipated as heat, while some remains in material that can feed decomposers. This is different from the fraction of swallowed food an animal absorbs.

1. **Respiration and heat transfer:** Organisms break down organic molecules, often by oxidising glucose (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`) to generate ATP (identified through work including that of **Yellapragada SubbaRow**, Chapter 1) for muscle movement, foraging, heartbeats, active transport across membranes, and maintaining body temperature (especially warm-blooded endotherms such as mammals and birds, Chapter 10 `10.3`). All of this energy eventually escapes to the environment as thermal energy (heat).
2. **Uneaten parts:** Consumers rarely eat the entire organism below them—woody tree trunks, deep roots, thorns, tough bark, hoofs, bones, scales, and feathers are left behind, and some individuals die of disease or old age without being eaten. Their chemical energy passes to **decomposers**, not to the next consumer level.
3. **Indigestible egested waste (faeces):** Much of what an animal swallows cannot be hydrolysed by its digestive enzymes—especially plant **cellulose** cell walls, lignin, insect chitin exoskeletons, hair, and bone. This undigested material is **egested** as **faeces** (Chapter 7 `7.1`), carrying chemical energy to soil decomposers.
4. **Excreted metabolic waste (urea and urine):** Excess amino acids are deaminated in the liver and **excreted** by the kidneys as **urea** in urine (Chapter 8 `8.5`), carrying some chemical energy away. Urine is a material output; heat is an energy transfer, not a substance in the urine.

These losses help explain two broad patterns:

- **Why long food chains are uncommon:** Assume producers incorporate `10,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` into new biomass. At `10%` production transfer, successive levels incorporate `1,000`, `100`, `10` and `1 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`. Energy supply becomes progressively smaller. This helps limit chain length, but there is no universal fifth-level ceiling: the area available, consumer requirements, transfer efficiencies and web structure also matter.
- **Why feeding edible crops directly to people can use energy more efficiently:** Livestock use much of their food energy for maintenance, movement and temperature regulation before producing meat, milk or eggs. Eating suitable crops directly avoids this intermediate conversion. The advantage varies with the crop, livestock and farming system; animals can also eat grass or by-products that people cannot eat.

  For a deliberately simplified food-supply calculation, begin with `10,000 kJ` in harvested edible grain. Allocate it either directly to people, or to livestock that return an assumed `10%` as edible animal food: `10,000 → 1,000 kJ`. These are **food energies supplied**, before human digestion, not energy retained as new human tissue. This comparison illustrates an additional conversion loss; it does not establish a universal tenfold land requirement. Our separate trophic-pyramid examples consistently track **new biomass production**.

### Populations and the four-phase batch-growth curve

How fast can any population grow inside its ecosystem? The rate of growth of a population depends on four main environmental factors (`0610 19.4`): **food supply**, **competition** (for food, space, water, light, or mates), **predation**, and **disease**.

When a small number of microorganisms (such as yeast cells in a flask of sugar broth or bacteria in a batch culture) enter a closed culture with finite resources, the population size plotted against time traces a microbial batch-growth curve with four commonly described phases. Its rising part is ==sigmoid== (`S`-shaped); a later death phase can follow if the closed culture deteriorates (Figure 15.2):

1. **==Lag phase==:** Population size changes slowly while cells adjust to the new environment (synthesising enzymes to digest the available substrate, absorbing water, and preparing to divide).
2. **==Exponential phase== (or **log phase**, `birth rate ≫ death rate`):** Food/nutrients and space are initially abundant, toxic waste concentration is negligible, and environmental limitations are not yet strongly slowing growth. In a microbial culture, cells can divide close to their maximum rate (`1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32...`), so the curve rises steeply.
3. **==Stationary phase== (approximately balanced division and death):** One or more **limiting factors** take effect—food/substrate begins to run low, space becomes crowded, intraspecific competition intensifies, toxic metabolic waste products accumulate, or other environmental conditions deteriorate. Division can slow greatly; in a simple stationary model, **division rate approximately equals death rate**, and the population size plateaus. For wild populations, the sustainable population size under the prevailing conditions is called **carrying capacity (`K`)**; it changes as resources and conditions change, and wild populations need not follow a neat four-phase culture curve.
4. **==Death phase== (`death rate > birth rate`):** In a closed container where no fresh nutrients are added and wastes are not removed, the food supply is exhausted and toxic metabolic wastes (such as ethanol in yeast or organic acids in bacteria) reach lethal levels, so the death rate exceeds the birth rate and the population crashes.

- **Never draw food chain arrows pointing from predator to prey:** Writing `Leopard → Deer → Grass` to mean "the leopard eats the deer" is the single commonest ecological mistake. Arrows (`→`) **always** point in the direction of **energy flow** (`Grass → Deer → Leopard`).

## 15.2 Atoms on loan: the water, carbon and nitrogen cycles

:::scene Marine Drive Monsoon Promenade & Dadar Kitchen Sink, Mumbai

Stand on the promenade at Marine Drive as the June southwest monsoon rolls in off the Arabian Sea. Warm ocean water that evaporated under the May sun has cooled high in the sky into towering cumulonimbus clouds. Within minutes, sheets of rain drum onto the basalt ridges of Malabar Hill and Borivali; some flows down storm drains and the Mithi River back to the sea, while some soaks into the soil where banyan and mango roots drink it up by osmosis and send it skyward through xylem vessels to evaporate out of leaf stomata as water vapour. Now step into a kitchen in Dadar where Kym is helping wash a fresh bundle of *methi* (fenugreek, *Trigonella foenum-graecum*) pulled soil-and-all from a suburban farm. Clinging to the pale branching roots are dozens of tiny, rounded **root nodules** the size of mustard seeds. Slice one open with a fingernail and its inside gleams **pinkish-red**—coloured by an oxygen-binding protein called **leghaemoglobin**, cousin to the haemoglobin in Kym's own red blood cells, shielding millions of ***Rhizobium* bacteria** as they crack atmospheric nitrogen gas (`N₂`) into the amino acids that will build Kym's enzymes and DNA.

Because Earth is virtually a closed system for matter, every carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen atom inside your body has been borrowed from the atmosphere, oceans, and soil—and will be returned to them. Three interlocking biogeochemical loops keep the biosphere stocked with raw materials (`0610 19.3`): the **water cycle**, the **carbon cycle**, and the **nitrogen cycle**.

### The water cycle and the carbon cycle

The water cycle is powered by solar thermal energy and gravity (Figure 15.3A):

- **==Evaporation==:** Heat from the Sun warms liquid water in oceans, lakes, rivers, and damp soil, giving water molecules enough kinetic energy to escape into the air as invisible **water vapour**.
- **==Transpiration==:** Plant roots absorb liquid water from the soil by osmosis (Chapter 3 `3.2`) and pull it up xylem vessels; inside leaves, water evaporates from the wet cell walls of spongy mesophyll cells and diffuses out through open **stomata** as water vapour (Chapter 5 `5.5`). Over a tropical rainforest like the Western Ghats, transpiration returns substantial water to the atmosphere; its share varies with vegetation, season and the area being measured.
- **==Condensation==:** Warm moist air rises, expands, and cools high in the troposphere; water vapour condenses around microscopic dust and salt nuclei into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals, forming **clouds**.
- **==Precipitation==:** When cloud droplets coalesce and grow too heavy to remain suspended, water falls back to Earth as **rain, snow, sleet, or hail**, returning to rivers and seas via **surface runoff** and **groundwater percolation**.

The carbon cycle revolves around atmospheric **carbon dioxide (`CO₂`, roughly `0.04%` of air; the measured value depends on year, season and site)** and five core processes (Figure 15.3B):

1. **==Photosynthesis== (`the main entry pathway in this sunlit ecosystem`):** Green plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria absorb `CO₂` from the air (or dissolved `HCO₃⁻`/`CO₂` in water) and use light energy trapped by chlorophyll to fix carbon atoms into **glucose (`C₆H₁₂O₆`)**, starch, cellulose, lipids, and proteins (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`).
2. **==Feeding== (and assimilation):** Animals eat plants (or other animals), digest their polymers with carbohydrases, proteases, and lipases (Chapter 7 `7.3`), and assimilate the carbon compounds into animal tissues.
3. **==Respiration==:** Producers, consumers, and decomposers carry out respiration; where oxygen is available, aerobic respiration (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`) twenty-four hours a day, oxidising organic carbon compounds to release ATP energy and returning `CO₂` gas to the atmosphere.
4. **==Decomposition==:** When plants and animals die, or shed leaves, faeces, and urea, decomposers (saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) secrete extracellular enzymes onto the dead organic matter, absorb the soluble products, and respire them, releasing `CO₂` back to the air.
5. **==Fossilisation== and ==combustion==:** When dead plants, peat bog mosses, or marine plankton are buried under mud and sediment in **anaerobic, acidic, or high-pressure conditions** where decomposers cannot break them down completely, geological heat and pressure over millions of years transform their carbon into **fossil fuels** (**coal**, **crude oil**, and **natural gas**). Carbonate rocks such as **limestone** form by a different route, often from accumulated calcium-carbonate shells and skeletons or carbonate precipitation. When humans burn fossil fuels or wood in power stations, vehicles, factories, and forest fires (**combustion**), stored carbon reacts with oxygen to release `CO₂` rapidly back into the atmosphere:

**Fossil fuel / Hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O**

### The nitrogen cycle: four bacterial guilds and the `N≡N` triple bond

Why do plants need nitrogen at all? As we saw in Chapter 5 (`5.3`) and Chapter 11 (`11.2–11.3`), carbohydrate molecules made in photosynthesis (`C₆H₁₂O₆`) contain only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. To build **amino acids**, **proteins** (enzymes, cell membrane carriers, rubisco, antibodies), **DNA**, **RNA**, and **ATP**, plants and animals must add **nitrogen (`N`)**.

At first glance, nitrogen looks abundant: **`78%` of Earth's atmosphere is nitrogen gas (`N₂`)**. Yet a plant starving in nitrogen-poor soil will turn yellow and stunted even though its leaves are bathed in `78% N₂` air. Why? Because the two nitrogen atoms in an `N₂` molecule are locked together by an extraordinarily strong **triple covalent bond (`N≡N`)** that neither plant chloroplasts nor animal enzymes can split. **No plant and no animal can fix `N₂` gas from the air.** Instead, non-leguminous plants must absorb soluble **nitrate ions (`NO₃⁻`)** (and some ammonium ions, `NH₄⁺`) dissolved in soil water through their **root hair cells by active transport** (Chapter 3 `3.4`).

Microbes, including bacteria, archaea and fungi, carry out different nitrogen transformations. Our model groups the main roles below—alongside lightning and industrial chemistry—to show how nitrogen moves through the nitrogen cycle (`0610 19.3`, Figure 15.4):

| **1. ==Nitrogen fixation==** | `N₂ (gas) → NH₃ / NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻` | **(a) ==Nitrogen-fixing bacteria==:** Mutualistic ***Rhizobium*** inside root nodules of leguminous plants (*peas, beans, clover, chickpeas/chana, lentils/masoor and pigeon peas/toor dal, fenugreek/methi*) and free-living soil ***Azotobacter*** reduce `N₂ → NH₄⁺` using the enzyme nitrogenase.<br>**(b) ==Lightning==:** High-voltage electrical discharge provides activation energy to react `N₂ + O₂ → nitrogen oxides`, which dissolve in rainwater to enter soil directly as nitrate ions (`NO₃⁻`).<br>**(c) Industrial Haber process:** Reacts `N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃` at `450 °C` and `200 atm` over an iron catalyst to make ammonium nitrate fertilisers. |
| **2. Absorption, Assimilation, Feeding & ==Deamination==** | `NO₃⁻ → Amino acids → Proteins → Urea` | Plant **root hair cells** absorb `NO₃⁻` from soil water by **active transport** (using ATP from aerobic root respiration) and combine it with carbohydrates to synthesize **amino acids and plant proteins**. Animals eat plants, digest proteins with proteases (`pepsin`, `trypsin`), and assimilate amino acids into animal proteins. Excess amino acids undergo **deamination in the liver** (Chapter 8 `8.5`), converting the amino group into urea, which is excreted in urine. |
| **3. Decomposition (==Ammonification==)** | `Dead proteins, faeces & urea → NH₄⁺` | **Decomposers** (putrefying bacteria and saprotrophic fungi) secrete extracellular proteases and urease onto dead plants, carcasses, egested faeces, and excreted urea, releasing **ammonium ions (`NH₄⁺`)** into the soil. |
| **4. ==Nitrification==** | `NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻` | Chemoautotrophic aerobic ==nitrifying bacteria== in **well-aerated soil** oxidise ammonium ions in two steps: first **ammonium ions to nitrite ions** (`NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻`, e.g. *Nitrosomonas*), and second **nitrite ions to nitrate ions** (`NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻`, e.g. *Nitrobacter*). Requires **oxygen (`O₂`)** supplied through aerated soil and dissolved in water films around microbes. |
| **5. ==Denitrification==** | `NO₃⁻ → N₂ (gas)` | Anaerobic ==denitrifying bacteria== (e.g. *Pseudomonas*) thriving in **oxygen-poor zones in soil, often promoted by waterlogging or compaction** use nitrate ions (`NO₃⁻`) in place of oxygen for respiration, reducing `NO₃⁻` back into inert **`N₂` gas** that escapes into the air and can remove nitrogen that crops could otherwise use; it also removes excess nitrate from some polluted waters. |

:::evidence Delft, 1888, Zurich, 1890 & Mauna Loa, 1958: Beijerinck, Winogradsky, and Keeling's biogeochemical proofs | Microbiological & atmospheric proof
In **1888**, the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck isolated the rod-shaped bacterium ***Rhizobium leguminosarum*** from the root nodules of pea and bean plants and proved that sterile soil lacking these bacteria could not trigger nodule formation or nitrogen fixation. Seven decades later, in **1958**, **Charles David Keeling** began continuous infrared measurements of atmospheric `CO₂` at Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii, providing direct chemical proof that human fossil-fuel combustion and deforestation were disrupting the global carbon cycle.

:::careful
- **Plants do NOT absorb `N₂` through their stomata:** Never write that "plants take in nitrogen from the air through their leaves." In the biological examples here, nitrogen-fixing microbes convert `N₂` to ammonia/ammonium. Lightning makes nitrogen oxides that can become nitrate, and the industrial Haber process makes ammonia. These are different fixation routes. Plants absorb nitrate ions (`NO₃⁻`) (or `NH₄⁺`) from soil water through root hair cells.
- **Do not confuse the three `-fication` terms:**
  - **Nitrogen fixation:** `N₂ (gas) → NH₄⁺ / amino acids` (*Rhizobium*, *Azotobacter*). **Lightning** instead forms nitrogen oxides that can become `NO₃⁻`; the **Haber process** makes `NH₃`.
  - **Nitrification:** `NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ (nitrite) → NO₃⁻ (nitrate)` (aerobic nitrifying bacteria in aerated soil).
  - **Denitrification:** `NO₃⁻ → N₂ (gas)` (anaerobic denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soil; *harmful* to crop yield).
- **Plants respire 24 hours a day:** In carbon cycle questions, remember that plants carry out **aerobic respiration continuously day and night** (`releasing CO₂`), while carrying out **photosynthesis only in the light**.

## 15.3 Footprints on the web: farming, deforestation, eutrophication and conservation

:::scene Powai Lake Outfall, Carter Road Mangroves & Thane Creek Flamingo Sanctuary, Mumbai

Take a morning walk along the eastern shore of **Powai Lake** in central Mumbai where an urban storm-water drain empties nutrient-rich runoff into a sheltered cove. The water surface is choked with a thick emerald-green mat of floating algae and water hyacinth. Beneath that green blanket, the water is dark, murky, and foul-smelling; no submerged pondweed survives on the lake bed, and several silver tilapia fish float belly-up near the reeds, their gill covers flared in water that has been stripped of dissolved oxygen. An hour later, ride the train west to **Carter Road in Bandra** at low tide, where tangled shopping bags and polystyrene cups snag on the pencil-like breathing roots (`pneumatophores`) of *Avicennia marina* mangroves. Yet just across the harbour in the **Thane Creek Flamingo Sanctuary**, protected mudflats and treated wetland buffers host tens of thousands of lesser and greater flamingos—an important habitat where protection and monitoring can help conserve wildlife. This scene illustrates a conservation aim; flamingo presence alone does not prove that water quality has recovered.

How did a single species of bipedal primate come to reshape every nutrient cycle on Earth? Modern agriculture and industry produce food for over eight billion humans, though access and food insecurity remain major problems, but those same methods place intense pressure on habitats, water bodies, the atmosphere, and wild species (`0610 Topic 20`).

### Modern food production, monocultures, and intensive livestock

During the twentieth century—and especially across India during the Green Revolution—food production per hectare multiplied through five technologies (`0610 20.1`):

1. **Agricultural machinery** (tractors, seed drills, combine harvesters), allowing farmers to cultivate and harvest much larger land areas rapidly and efficiently.
2. **Chemical fertilisers** (`N-P-K` compounds supplying nitrate `NO₃⁻`, phosphate `PO₄³⁻`, and potassium `K⁺` ions), replenishing limiting soil minerals so crops grow faster and synthesize more protein and chlorophyll.
3. **Insecticides**, killing insect pests that eat crop leaves/fruits or act as vectors of plant viruses, thereby increasing crop yield and quality.
4. **Herbicides**, killing competing **weeds** so the crop receives a larger share of sunlight, soil water, and mineral ions.
5. **Selective breeding** (Chapter 14 `14.4`), breeding high-yielding, disease-resistant crop varieties and high-producing livestock.

However, two modern farming systems carry severe ecological trade-offs:

| Farming System | Agricultural Advantages | Ecological Disadvantages & Risks (`0610 20.1`) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Large-scale ==monoculture==** *(growing one crop species over a field or large area; repeated cropping and genetic uniformity can add risks)* | Uniform plant height and ripening time allow efficient mechanised sowing, spraying, and harvesting; tailored fertiliser and herbicide schedules maximize single-crop yield. | **1. Loss of ==biodiversity==:** Replaces diverse wild habitats and food webs with one plant species.<br>**2. Pest and pathogen explosions:** A large area of similar, susceptible hosts allows specialist insect pests or fungal diseases to multiply exponentially, requiring heavy **insecticide** spraying (which also kills beneficial pollinators like bees, kills natural pest predators, and selects for insecticide-resistant pests).<br>**3. Soil mineral depletion:** Repeated crops can remove similar nutrients; depletion depends on yield, soil and nutrient management, and can require replenishment. |
| **Intensive livestock production** *(keeping large numbers of cattle, pigs, or poultry indoors in high-density temperature-controlled pens)* | Maximises energy efficiency and growth rate by restricting movement and maintaining a suitable temperature (so less food energy is spent on extra movement or maintaining body temperature in cold conditions; excessive heat instead causes heat stress, `15.1`), producing cheaper meat, milk, and eggs on less land. | **1. Rapid disease spread:** Crowded conditions allow viral and bacterial infections to sweep rapidly from animal to animal.<br>**2. Overuse of antibiotics:** Routine prophylactic feeding of antibiotics selects strongly for **antibiotic-resistant bacteria** (`MRSA`, *Salmonella*, Chapter 14 `14.3`) that can infect humans.<br>**3. Pollution & ethical welfare:** Concentrated urine and faeces (**slurry**) pollute waterways (**eutrophication**), ruminant cattle release **methane (`CH₄`)**, and crowding causes animal stress and welfare concerns. |

### Habitat destruction and the four effects of deforestation

Around the world, natural habitats are destroyed when humans clear land for **crops and livestock grazing**, **housing and road infrastructure**, **extraction of timber and paper pulp**, **open-cast mining and quarrying**, and **polluting marine and freshwater environments** (`0610 20.2`). Because species in a food web are interdependent, removing even a single producer, pollinator, or predator species can trigger a trophic cascade across the entire community.

The most widespread form of habitat destruction is ==deforestation==—the clearing of forests on a massive scale. In the Cambridge syllabus (`0610 20.2`), you must be able to explain the undesirable effects of deforestation across **four interconnected domains** (Figure 15.6A):

1. **Reduction of biodiversity and ==extinction==:** Tropical rainforests (such as the Western Ghats and Silent Valley studied by **E. K. Janaki Ammal**, **Salim Ali**, and **Madhav Gadgil**) contain over half of Earth's terrestrial species in layered canopy, epiphyte, understorey, and forest-floor microhabitats. Felling trees destroys food sources, nesting sites, and shelter, fragmenting populations until species become endangered or **extinct** (no living individuals remain).
2. **Severe soil erosion and leaching:** In an intact forest, the leafy canopy breaks the mechanical force of heavy monsoon raindrops, while a dense network of tree roots binds soil particles together. Once trees are felled, heavy rain hammers bare ground and washes away the nutrient-rich topsoil (**soil erosion**) while dissolved nitrate and magnesium ions wash deep out of reach (**leaching**), leaving barren rock or laterite hardpan where forests cannot easily regrow.
3. **Flooding and disruption of the water cycle:** Without tree canopies intercepting rain, spongy humus absorbing water, and transpiration returning water vapour to the sky (`15.2`), rainwater rushes straight down bare hillsides as surface runoff. Simultaneously, eroded topsoil washes into rivers and **silts up riverbeds**, reducing channel capacity so rivers burst their banks and cause destructive **floods** downstream (while reduced transpiration makes regional climates drier).
4. **Increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide (`CO₂`):** Living forests are major carbon sinks that remove `CO₂` from the air via photosynthesis. When forests are cut down, less `CO₂` is removed by photosynthesis, and when felled timber and forest undergrowth are **burned (`combustion`)** or left to rot via **microbial decomposition (`respiration`)**, vast stores of carbon are oxidised back into atmospheric **`CO₂`**, intensifying global warming.

### Pollution: a illustrative eutrophication pathway, plastics, and the enhanced greenhouse effect

What killed the fish in that stagnant cove of Powai Lake? When farmers apply excess **nitrate (`NO₃⁻`) and phosphate (`PO₄³⁻`) chemical fertilisers** to fields before heavy rain, the soluble ions **leach** through soil into rivers and lakes. Exactly the same thing happens when **untreated sewage** (rich in organic matter, ammonium, and phosphates) or livestock slurry drains into water. This nutrient enrichment is called ==eutrophication==. Here is **one common seven-step pathway** (`0610 20.3`, Figure 15.5A):

1. **Increased availability of nitrate (`NO₃⁻`) and other mineral ions:** Leached chemical fertilisers or untreated sewage enter the lake, river, or estuary, steeply raising mineral ion concentrations.
2. **Rapid growth of algae and surface water plants (**==algal bloom==**):** Because mineral ions were previously the limiting factor for producer growth, unicellular green algae and floating plants multiply rapidly across the water surface.
3. **Algal bloom blocks sunlight from reaching submerged aquatic plants:** The thick green carpet at the surface absorbs and reflects light, so rooted aquatic plants deeper in the water column cannot photosynthesise.
4. **Death of submerged aquatic plants (and short-lived surface algae):** Starved of light, the submerged plants die and sink to the bottom as dead organic detritus (joined by dying surface algae once nutrients are exhausted).
5. **Rapid multiplication of aerobic decomposer bacteria:** Saprotrophic **bacteria** in the water and sediment feed on the huge pile of dead organic matter (and any raw sewage solids) and reproduce exponentially.
6. **Depletion of dissolved oxygen (`O₂`) via bacterial aerobic respiration:** As the vast population of decomposer bacteria carries out aerobic respiration (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`), it consumes dissolved **`O₂`** from the water far faster than diffusion from the air can replace it (creating a spike in **biochemical oxygen demand, BOD**, and a sharp **oxygen sag**).
7. **Death of aerobic aquatic organisms (fish, crabs, insect larvae):** Without sufficient dissolved oxygen for their own aerobic respiration, fish and aquatic invertebrates **suffocate and die**, collapsing the aquatic food web and favouring organisms that tolerate low oxygen; anaerobic processes become more important where oxygen is absent.

### Plastics and gases follow different routes

Alongside eutrophication, two global pollution streams threaten terrestrial, marine, and atmospheric systems (`0610 20.3` and `0893 8Be.02`):

- **Persistent plastics and food-web pollutants:** Many common synthetic plastics resist biological breakdown under ordinary environmental conditions. Their chemistry differs: polyethylene has a carbon–carbon backbone, while nylon is a polyamide. Plastic bags and fragments can be swallowed and obstruct feeding or digestion; nets and rings can entangle animals. **Microplastics** are particles smaller than `5 mm`. They can enter organisms and move through feeding interactions, but ingestion, retention and transfer do not by themselves prove increasing concentrations at every trophic level. Scientists are investigating the effects of particles and their associated chemicals. **==Bioaccumulation==** means a substance builds up within an organism; biomagnification means its concentration increases up a food chain. Teach the latter with well-established persistent pollutants such as DDT/DDE, rather than assuming all microplastics inevitably become lethal to apex predators.
- **Greenhouse gases (`CO₂` and `CH₄`) and the ==enhanced greenhouse effect==:** Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface, which re-emits longer-wave **infrared (thermal) radiation**. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb part of this infrared radiation and re-radiate it back toward the surface. Human activities are rapidly raising the concentrations of two primary greenhouse gases (`0610 20.3`, Figure 15.5B): **carbon dioxide (`CO₂`)**, released by **combustion of fossil fuels** (coal, oil, natural gas) and **deforestation**; and methane (`CH₄`), released by anaerobic methanogenic microorganisms in the digestive rumen of **cattle and other livestock**, in waterlogged anaerobic **rice paddy fields**, and in decomposing organic waste buried in anaerobic **landfill sites**. The resulting **enhanced greenhouse effect** drives **climate change**: global warming, melting polar ice caps and glaciers, thermal expansion of seawater and **sea-level rise**, extreme weather, and habitat shifts.

### Conservation: sustainable resources, forestry, fisheries, seed banks, and assisted reproduction

A ==sustainable resource== is one that is **produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment so that it does not run out** (`0610 20.4`). Whereas fossil fuels (`coal`, `oil`, `natural gas`) and metallic ores are non-renewable, biological resources such as **forests (timber)** and **fish stocks** can be maintained sustainably if managed with scientific rules (Figure 15.6B):

| Resource or Target | Management & Conservation Methods (`0610 20.4`) | Why Each Method Works Biologically |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sustainable forestry** *(conserving forests while harvesting timber)* | **1. Selective logging:** Felling only mature trees of selected species rather than clear-felling.<br>**2. Reforestation (replanting):** Replanting suitable species and allowing enough time and survival for replacement growth.<br>**4. Education, legal quotas & protected reserves.** | Preserves canopy structure, soil root binding, and wildlife habitats; can help replace harvested biomass, provided harvest limits, regrowth and seedling survival are monitored; one planted seedling does not immediately replace a mature tree’s habitat. |

Minimum net mesh size:** Using larger diamond or square mesh holes (and escape panels).<br>**3. | **Conserving endangered species** *(preventing extinction)* | **1. Why do species become endangered or extinct in the first place? Six major pressures drive species toward extinction (`0610 20.4`): climate change, **habitat destruction**, **hunting and poaching**, **overharvesting**, **pollution**, and **introduced (invasive) species** (which outcompete native species for food/space, predate them, or introduce novel diseases).

Crucially, once a population shrinks to a very small size (`a genetic bottleneck`), it faces a severe genetic danger even if hunting stops: **loss of genetic variation** (`0610 20.4`, linking Chapters 13 and 14). In a tiny population, closely related individuals are forced to mate with one another (**inbreeding**). Inbreeding reduces heterozygosity and steeply increases the proportion of offspring that are **homozygous for harmful recessive alleles** (**inbreeding depression**, lowering fertility and survival). Furthermore, without a wide pool of different alleles, the population lacks the **genetic variation** needed for natural selection to adapt if a new pathogen or climate shift arrives. This is why **seed banks** collect seeds from thousands of wild plants across different habitats (preserving allele diversity in a tiny space at `-20 °C`) and why zoo **captive breeding programmes** use DNA profiles, **artificial insemination (AI)**, and **in vitro fertilisation (IVF)** to pair suitable donors across populations. AI introduces collected sperm into a female so fertilisation occurs inside her body. IVF brings sperm and eggs together outside the body; an embryo can then be transferred to a suitable female. Neither technique creates lost alleles: genetically different donors and suitable reintroduction habitats are still needed.

Conservation protects **biodiversity**, the number of different species living in an area, and vulnerable ecosystems. It also maintains processes such as decomposition and nutrient cycling and preserves sources of food, medicines, fuel and useful genes. Conventional seed banking works for seeds that tolerate drying and freezing; some tropical species require living collections or other specialised storage methods.

## 15.4 Living factories & rewritable code: fermenters, plasmids, recombinant insulin and CRISPR

:::scene Irani Bakery at Dhobi Talao, Mumbai & Hindustan Antibiotics Fermenter Hall, Pimpri

It is five o'clock in the morning at a century-old Irani bakery near Dhobi Talao in south Mumbai. Inside the warm proofing trough, a baker punches down a pillowy mound of *brun-pav* bread dough that has doubled in volume overnight. Millions of microscopic **yeast** cells (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) have been respiring anaerobically through the flour sugars, blowing tiny bubbles of carbon dioxide (`CO₂`) into the stretchy gluten network; when the trays slide into the wood-fired brick oven at `220 °C`, gases expand and the loaf structure sets while much of the **ethanol** evaporates into the air; small amounts can remain in the bread. Two hours away along the Mumbai–Pune expressway at **Pimpri**, three-storey stainless-steel **industrial fermenters** gleam inside the halls of Hindustan Antibiotics—descendants of a plant incorporated in `1954` and commissioned in `1955–56` with international assistance—where sterile air bubbles through spargers, motorised paddles churn nutrient broth, and cold-water jackets carry away kilowatts of microbial heat. And inside a cool refrigerator at a clinic in Parel sits a glass vial of **recombinant human insulin**—synthesised not by a pig's pancreas, but by *Escherichia coli* bacteria carrying a human gene stitched into their circular **plasmids**.

Long before humans knew what DNA or enzymes looked like—and long before **Louis Pasteur** proved in the `1850s` that living microorganisms drive fermentation—bakers and brewers were using microbes to transform food. Today, ==biotechnology== uses microorganisms (bacteria, yeast, and filamentous fungi) and their purified enzymes on an industrial scale, while ==genetic modification== (`genetic engineering`) rewrites the DNA of cells directly (`0610 Topic 21`).

### Why bacteria and fungi are ideal for biotechnology and genetic modification

Why do biotechnologists choose bacteria (and unicellular fungi like yeast) over multicellular plants or animals as living factories? Four biological features make bacteria uniquely suited (`0610 21.1`):

1. **Rapid reproduction rate:** Under optimum conditions (`37 °C`, abundant nutrients), some laboratory strains of *E. coli* can divide about every **`20 minutes`**. At that ideal rate, one cell needs about `30` doublings—`10 hours`—to produce roughly a billion descendants. Other microbes and production conditions have different growth rates, and resources eventually limit growth.
2. **Ability to make complex molecules:** Despite their simple prokaryotic structure (Chapter 2 `2.2`), bacteria possess `70S` ribosomes and metabolic pathways capable of synthesising complex polypeptides, enzymes, vitamins, and antibiotics—and because the **genetic code is nearly universal** (deciphered by **Har Gobind Khorana** and colleagues after **Rosalind Franklin**, Watson, and Crick revealed the DNA helix, Chapter 11 `11.3`), a bacterium reads a human gene using the exact same triplet codons as a human cell.
3. **Few ethical concerns:** Manipulating, culturing, and harvesting bacteria or fungi in vats raises no animal-welfare or pain concerns compared with experimenting on vertebrates.
4. **Presence of ==plasmids==:** Alongside their main circular chromosome, bacteria carry plasmids—small, circular, double-stranded loops of DNA (Chapter 2 `2.2`) that are easy to extract from cells, cut open, splice foreign genes into, and transfer from one bacterium to another as genetic **vectors**.

### Inside an industrial fermenter: fermenter controls

When **Ernst Chain**, **Howard Florey** and their Oxford colleagues developed penicillin as a treatment in the `1940s`, Norman Heatley helped solve practical culture and extraction problems. Larger-scale production then drew on USDA fermentation research at Peoria and industrial engineering, including aerated deep tanks. Hindustan Antibiotics’ Pimpri plant, commissioned in `1955–56`, brought antibiotic manufacture to India. Industrial ==fermenters== now produce **penicillin** (from *Penicillium* fungi), **mycoprotein** (from *Fusarium venenatum*), enzymes and recombinant proteins such as insulin.

To keep billions of microorganisms growing at their maximum rate, an industrial fermenter monitors and controls its conditions, using the following linked systems (`0610 21.2`, Figure 15.7B):

1. **==Aseptic precautions== (high-pressure steam sterilization):** Before inoculation, the entire stainless-steel vessel and all pipes are sterilized with high-pressure steam, and incoming air is passed through fine sterile filters. *Reason:* Kills foreign **contaminant bacteria and fungi** so they cannot compete with the culture microorganism for nutrients/oxygen or contaminate the medical/food product with toxic waste.
2. **Nutrients (carbon and nitrogen inlets):** Measured supplies of **glucose, starch, or molasses** (providing a carbon source and energy for respiration) plus **ammonia (`NH₃`), ammonium ions (`NH₄⁺`), or nitrate ions** (providing a nitrogen source for **amino acid and protein synthesis**) and mineral salts are fed in through sterile pipes.
3. **Optimum temperature (temperature probe + cold-water cooling jacket):** As trillions of bacteria or fungal hyphae respire rapidly inside a giant tank, they release huge amounts of thermal energy (heat). Cold water circulating through an outer **water-cooled jacket** absorbs and carries away this excess heat to maintain the exact **optimum temperature** (`~26–28 °C` for *Penicillium*, `37 °C` for *E. coli*). *Reason:* If temperature rises sufficiently above the range tolerated by that organism and process, proteins can denature and the culture can die; if temperature falls too low, kinetic energy drops and enzyme-catalysed reactions become too slow.
4. **Optimum pH (pH probe + acid/alkali inlets):** Dissolving `CO₂` and metabolic acids shift the broth pH. Electronic pH probes trigger automatic addition of dilute **acid or alkali** to hold pH steady at the optimum (`e.g. pH 6.5–7.0`). *Reason:* Extreme pH breaks ionic bonds in enzyme active sites, causing **denaturation** (Chapter 4 `4.3`).
5. **Oxygenation (sterile air sparger):** For aerobic microorganisms (*Penicillium*, *Fusarium*, aerobic *E. coli*), sterile compressed air or oxygen is bubbled through a perforated ring (**sparger**) at the bottom of the tank. *Reason:* Supplies dissolved oxygen (`O₂`) required for aerobic respiration to generate ATP for rapid growth and protein synthesis.
6. **Agitation (motor-driven stirring paddles / impellers):** Rotating paddles mix the liquid culture continuously. *Reason:* Prevents microorganisms from settling to the bottom, keeps every cell in uniform contact with **nutrients and dissolved oxygen**, and ensures an **even temperature and pH** throughout the entire tank.

7. **Waste products and exhaust:** Carbon dioxide and other gaseous outputs leave through a suitable filtered exhaust. Depending on the process, broth may be harvested or replaced to control accumulated wastes. Monitoring waste matters because toxic products can slow growth even while food remains. Sterility, agitation and waste handling support control of temperature, pH, oxygen and nutrient supply.

### Genetic modification: the five-step recombinant human insulin pipeline

In Chapter 10 (`10.4` and the companion story of **Frederick Banting**, Charles Best, and **Dilip Mahalanabis**), we saw how diabetic patients whose pancreatic β-cells cannot secrete **insulin** were saved by insulin extracted from the pancreases of slaughtered pigs and cattle. Yet animal insulin had serious drawbacks: it differed by one to three amino acids from human insulin (causing allergic immune reactions in some patients), supply was tied to slaughterhouse numbers, and religious or ethical diets objected to porcine or bovine products.

==Genetic modification== (or **genetic engineering**) is **changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing, or inserting individual genes** (`0610 21.3`). Following the `1973` discovery of recombinant plasmids by **Herbert Boyer** and **Stanley Cohen**, scientists engineered *E. coli* bacteria to manufacture pure **human insulin** via a precise **five-step molecular pipeline** (`0610 21.3`, Figure 15.8A):

1. **Prepare suitable insulin-coding DNA and cut it with a ==restriction enzyme==:** A raw human chromosomal gene contains introns that ordinary *E. coli* cannot remove. Scientists instead use an **intron-free** sequence, made synthetically or copied from processed human mRNA into complementary DNA (`cDNA`). It is placed with control sequences that bacteria can read. In this cloning example, `EcoRI` recognises a particular DNA sequence and makes staggered cuts, leaving short single-stranded **==sticky ends==**.
2. **Cut a bacterial plasmid with the same enzyme in this example:** A **plasmid vector** is opened with `EcoRI`, giving compatible ends that can pair with the prepared insert. Using the same enzyme is one convenient way to obtain compatible ends; different enzymes can sometimes produce compatible ends too. Not all restriction enzymes make sticky ends.
3. **Join insert and plasmid using ==DNA ligase==:** Complementary sticky ends base-pair, and ligase seals covalent bonds in the sugar-phosphate backbones, forming a **==recombinant plasmid==**. When drawing the paired ends, show the two DNA strands running in opposite directions rather than comparing isolated letter strings without orientation.
4. **Introduce the recombinant plasmid into bacteria and select suitable cells:** Some host *E. coli* take up the plasmid (**transformation**). Scientists identify cells with the intended construct and check that they express the desired product.
5. **Grow, recover, process and purify:** Selected bacteria are grown under controlled conditions. They use the insulin-coding instructions to make a product that is recovered and processed as required, folded correctly and purified before medical use. **Mature human insulin has two chains—21 and 30 amino acids—joined by disulfide bonds**, not one continuous 51-amino-acid chain. Some methods make an insulin precursor that must be processed; the early successful method made the two chains separately and joined them afterwards.

**Matthew Meselson** and **Franklin Stahl** showed how DNA strands are copied (Chapter 12). Here, faithful copying lets bacterial descendants inherit the inserted instructions. **Johann Gregor Mendel** studied inheritance in peas (Chapter 13), but his sexual-cross Punnett squares do not describe this asexual multiplication.

The nearly universal genetic code explains why a bacterial ribosome can assign the same amino acids to the same codons. It does **not** by itself supply expression controls, remove human introns or turn a precursor into a purified medicine.

How does **genetic modification** (`Chapter 15`) compare with **selective breeding** (`artificial selection`, studied in Chapter 14 `14.4` with E. K. Janaki Ammal's sugarcane and Gir cattle)?

1. **Source of genes:** Selective breeding can only select alleles **already present within the same species** (or closely related cross-fertile species); genetic modification can transfer a gene from **any species across kingdoms** (such as a human gene into *E. coli* or a bacterial gene into cotton).
2. **Precision:** Selective breeding mates whole organisms, shuffling thousands of alleles together during meiosis (Chapter 12 `12.3` and Chapter 13 `13.2`) and sometimes retaining nearby unwanted alleles (**linkage drag**). This differs from inbreeding depression, which can expose harmful recessive alleles through increased homozygosity. Genetic engineering can introduce one or several genes, remove DNA or change particular bases; the resulting line must be tested for intended and unintended effects.
3. **Timescale:** Selective breeding requires **many generations** (years or decades) of selecting and breeding offspring; an initial DNA change can be made more directly, but developing a stable, useful crop still requires selection, testing and often further breeding generations.

Finally, in 2012, **Jennifer Doudna**, **Emmanuelle Charpentier** and colleagues demonstrated programmable DNA cutting using **==CRISPR-Cas9==**, adapted from a bacterial antiviral defence. A guide RNA contains a short region matching a target near a suitable **PAM** sequence; it directs the **Cas9** enzyme to cut DNA. **Cutting is not the same as correcting:** the cell’s repair machinery changes the sequence afterwards. A chosen correction may need an added repair template or other editing machinery, and both intended and unintended changes must be checked. An approved sickle-cell treatment edits blood-forming stem cells to increase fetal haemoglobin; it does not simply replace the HbS letter. This connects our molecular story to real treatment while preserving the difference between targeting, repair and clinical testing.

    - **Calculation:** `Level 1 (Producers) = 30,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` → `Level 2 (Primary consumers) = 3,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` → `Level 3 (Secondary consumers) = 300 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` → **`Level 4 (Tertiary consumers) = 30 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`**.

That is an energy density, not a complete population budget (`0.3 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` equals `300,000 kJ km⁻² yr⁻¹`). Habitat area, feeding relationships and consumer needs are also required; these figures do not establish that SGNP has no sixth-level consumer.

3. **Recombinant human insulin & the universal code (`15.4` & Chapters 10–11):**
    - **Five-step pipeline:** Prepare intron-free insulin-coding DNA with bacterial expression controls; cut insert and plasmid to obtain compatible ends (the same restriction enzyme in this example); ligate and form recombinant plasmids; transform and select suitable bacteria; culture, recover, process/fold and purify the product.
