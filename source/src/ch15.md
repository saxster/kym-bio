## 15.1 Sunlit chains & the ten-percent tax: food webs, ecological pyramids and population growth

:::scene Sanjay Gandhi National Park (SGNP), Borivali & Oval Maidan, Mumbai

It is early October in **Sanjay Gandhi National Park (SGNP)** in Borivali, at the northern edge of Mumbai, just after the monsoon rains have triggered the once-in-eight-years mass flowering of the *Karvi* shrub (*Strobilanthes callosa*). Purple-blue blossoms carpet the basalt hillsides below the Kanheri Caves. Honeybees and carpenter bees hum from corolla to corolla; caterpillars chew the tender green leaves; a praying mantis waits motionless on a stem; a rufous treepie and a spotted owlet watch from a teak branch; and in the dappled shade below, a herd of spotted deer (*Axis axis*) grazes while an Indian leopard (*Panthera pardus fusca*) rests on a warm boulder overhead. Beneath their hooves, white threads of bracket fungi and billions of soil bacteria quietly digest fallen petals, shed antlers, and deer droppings back into dark forest humus. Across town at Oval Maidan, a single centuries-old **banyan tree** (*Ficus benghalensis*) spreads its aerial prop roots like a miniature forest of its own, feeding thousands of fig wasps, caterpillars, and fruit bats from one massive woody trunk.

Every living interaction on that Borivali hillside belongs to an ==ecosystem==—a distinct unit containing all the interacting organisms in a given area (the ==community==) together with their non-living physical and chemical environment (sunlight, basalt rock, rainwater, soil mineral ions, and air). Within an ecosystem, the specific place where an organism lives is its ==habitat== (such as the damp leaf-litter floor for a bracket fungus or the canopy twigs for a fig wasp), a group of organisms of the **same species** living in the same area at the same time is a ==population== (such as all the spotted deer in SGNP), and all the populations of **different species** living and interacting in that ecosystem form the ==community==.

To trace how an ecosystem stays alive, we must distinguish two quantities that never behave the same way: **energy** and **matter**. The **Sun** is the principal source of energy input to biological systems. In Chapter 5 (`5.1–5.3`), after **Jan Baptista van Helmont** weighed his willow tree and **Jan Ingenhousz** watched sunlit leaves bubble oxygen, we saw how chlorophyll inside green plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria traps **light energy** from the Sun and transfers it into **chemical energy** stored in the covalent bonds of glucose (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`). When animals eat plants, or predators eat prey, that stored chemical energy is passed from one organism to the next. At every step, organisms oxidise glucose in **aerobic respiration** (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`, Chapter 6 `6.2`) to power muscle contraction, active transport, nerve impulses, and protein synthesis, transferring energy to the surroundings as **thermal energy (heat)**. Once energy has radiated into the air as low-grade heat, **no plant or chloroplast can ever trap it again**. Energy therefore flows through ecosystems **in one direction only** (`non-cyclical: Sun → producers → consumers and decomposers → dissipated heat`). By contrast, the chemical **elements and mineral ions** (`carbon`, `hydrogen`, `oxygen`, `nitrogen`, `phosphorus`, `magnesium`) are **recycled** over and over within the ecosystem (`cyclical`).

### Food chains, food webs, and the exact meaning of the arrow

A ==food chain== is a diagram showing the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer:

**Karvi shrub (Producer) → Spotted deer (Primary consumer) → Indian leopard (Secondary consumer)**

Look closely at the arrows (`→`). In ecology and in every Cambridge examination, **an arrow in a food chain or food web points in the direction of energy transfer**—from the organism being eaten into the body of the organism that eats it (`Grass → Deer`, never `Deer → Grass`). Because most animals eat more than one kind of food and are eaten by more than one predator, real ecosystems are woven into a ==food web==—a network of interconnected food chains. Every organism in a food chain or food web occupies a specific feeding position called a ==trophic level== (`from the Greek trophē, meaning nourishment`):

| Trophic Level & Role | Syllabus Definition (`0610 19.2`) | SGNP & Mumbai Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1: ==Producer==** | An organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis. | *Karvi* shrubs, grasses, *Ficus benghalensis* (banyan tree), creek phytoplankton. |
| **Level 2: ==Primary consumer==** | An organism that gets its energy by feeding on producers; classified by diet as a ==herbivore== (an animal that gets its energy by eating plants). | Spotted deer, caterpillars, fig wasps, fruit bats, zooplankton. |
| **Level 3: ==Secondary consumer==** | An organism that gets its energy by feeding on primary consumers; may be a ==carnivore== (an animal that gets its energy by eating other animals) or an ==omnivore== (eating both plants and animals). | Praying mantids, tree frogs, rufous treepies, barn owls, Indian leopards (when eating deer). |
| **Level 4: ==Tertiary consumer==** / **==Quaternary consumer==** | Predators that feed on secondary consumers (`Level 4`) or tertiary consumers (`Level 5`, apex predators). | Crested serpent eagles eating tree frogs or rat snakes; Indian pythons. |
| **==Decomposer==** | An organism (principally saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material (`faeces`, `urea`, `dead leaves`, `carcasses`). | Bracket fungi, soil mould, putrefying bacteria in forest humus and creek mud. |

:::evidence Oxford, 1927 & Cedar Creek Bog, Minnesota, 1942: Elton's pyramids and Lindeman's ten-percent energy budget | Thermodynamic ecological proof
In **1927**, the English ecologist **Charles Elton** introduced the "pyramid of numbers" after observing Arctic and woodland food webs where abundant small herbivores supported progressively fewer larger predators. Fifteen years later, in **1942**, the twenty-six-year-old American ecologist **Raymond Lindeman** (working with Eleanor Hall at Cedar Creek Bog in Minnesota) published the first complete thermodynamic energy budget of an ecosystem. By measuring the chemical energy stored and respired across producers, herbivores, and carnivores over an entire year, Lindeman proved that only **about `10%` of the energy** entering one trophic level is transferred into new biomass at the next trophic level. The remaining **`~90%` is lost** between levels—establishing why ecological pyramids of energy can never invert and why food chains are strictly limited in length.

### Comparing pyramids of numbers, biomass, and energy

Ecologists use three different ecological pyramids to quantify trophic levels (`Level 1` always at the bottom bar, stepping upward to `Level 2`, `Level 3`, and `Level 4`):

1. **==Pyramid of numbers==:** Bar width shows the **number of individual organisms** at each trophic level in a fixed area. *Advantage:* Fast and non-destructive to count in the field. *Disadvantage:* Ignores organism size (`1` banyan tree counts the same as `1` aphid), so the pyramid is **inverted or spindle-shaped** whenever a single large producer supports thousands of small herbivores (`Tree → Caterpillars → Songbirds`), or when hundreds of fleas or protozoan parasites feed on a single bird or mammal (`L4 > L3`).
2. **==Pyramid of biomass==:** Bar width shows the **dry mass of living material** (`g m⁻²` or `kg ha⁻¹`) present at each trophic level at a single moment in time (**standing crop**). Organisms are dried in an oven at `~80 °C` until constant mass so that variable water content (which contains zero chemical energy) does not distort the measurement. *Advantage:* Overcomes the size distortion of pyramids of numbers; on land, pyramids of biomass are **almost always upright** because producer dry mass exceeds consumer dry mass. *Disadvantage:* Destructive (requires killing and oven-drying samples) and measures only a **single snapshot in time**, not the rate of production.
3. **==Pyramid of energy==:** Bar width shows the **rate of energy flow** (`kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`) passing through each trophic level over a full year. *Advantage:* **Always upright (pyramid-shaped) without exception** in every terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem, because the Second Law of Thermodynamics guarantees that less chemical energy is available to each successive trophic level. *Disadvantage:* Difficult and time-consuming to measure calorific values and respiration rates of every species across twelve months.

### The ten-percent rule, short food chains, and human diet efficiency

Why does roughly **`90%` of the energy vanish** between one trophic level and the next, leaving only **`~10%`** to become new biomass available to the next consumer? Four biological processes account for the loss (`0610 19.2`):

1. **Aerobic respiration and heat loss (`~50–60%` of ingested energy):** Every living organism oxidises glucose (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`) to generate ATP (discovered by **Yellapragada SubbaRow**) for muscle movement, foraging, heartbeats, active transport across membranes, and maintaining body temperature (especially warm-blooded endotherms such as mammals and birds, Chapter 10 `10.3`). All of this energy eventually escapes to the environment as **thermal energy (heat)**.
2. **Uneaten parts:** Consumers rarely eat the entire organism below them—woody tree trunks, deep roots, thorns, tough bark, hoofs, bones, scales, and feathers are left behind, and some individuals die of disease or old age without being eaten. Their chemical energy passes to **decomposers**, not to the next consumer level.
3. **Indigestible egested waste (faeces):** Much of what an animal swallows cannot be hydrolysed by its digestive enzymes—especially plant **cellulose** cell walls, lignin, insect chitin exoskeletons, hair, and bone. This undigested material is **egested** as **faeces** (Chapter 7 `7.1`), carrying chemical energy to soil decomposers.
4. **Excreted metabolic waste (urea and urine):** Excess amino acids are deaminated in the liver and **excreted** by the kidneys as **urea** in urine (Chapter 8 `8.5`), along with lost heat and sweat.

This `~90%` tax at every step explains two major patterns in biology:

- **Why food chains rarely have more than 4 or 5 trophic levels:** Suppose `10,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` of chemical energy is fixed by *Karvi* shrubs (`Level 1`). Primary consumers (`Level 2`, deer) build `1,000 kJ` of biomass; secondary consumers (`Level 3`, leopards) build `100 kJ`; tertiary consumers (`Level 4`) build `10 kJ`; and a hypothetical `5th` level receives only `1 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`. By the `4th` or `5th` level, so little usable energy remains per square kilometre that a higher predator would spend more energy hunting over a vast territory than it could ever recover from its prey, making a breeding population impossible to sustain.
- **Why supplying humans with crop plants is far more energy-efficient than feeding crops to livestock:** When humans eat **crop plants directly** (`rice, wheat, chickpeas, vegetables`), the food chain has only **two trophic levels (`1 transfer step`)**:

  **Crop plants (`10,000 kJ`) → Humans (`1,000 kJ assimilated, 10%`)**

  When humans grow arable crops (`maize, soya, grain`), feed those crops to **livestock** (`cattle, pigs, poultry`), and then eat the meat, milk, or eggs, the food chain has **three trophic levels (`2 transfer steps`)**:

  **Crop plants (`10,000 kJ`) → Livestock (`1,000 kJ`) → Humans (`100 kJ assimilated, 1%`)**

  Because the livestock lose `~90%` of the crop's chemical energy in their own **respiration, body heat, egested faeces, and excreted urine**, eating crop plants directly delivers roughly **ten times more food energy per hectare of farmland** (and supports far more people on the same land area) than routing crops through livestock.

### Populations and the four-phase sigmoid growth curve

How fast can any population grow inside its ecosystem? The rate of growth of a population depends on four main environmental factors (`0610 19.4`): **food supply**, **competition** (for food, space, water, light, or mates), **predation**, and **disease**.

When a small number of microorganisms (such as yeast cells in a flask of sugar broth or bacteria in a batch culture) or colonising organisms enter a closed environment with finite resources, the population size plotted against time traces a characteristic `S`-shaped curve called a ==sigmoid growth curve== with **four distinct phases** (Figure 15.2):

1. **==Lag phase== (`birth rate low, N very small`):** Population size increases very slowly at first because there are very few reproducing individuals present and cells are adjusting to the new environment (synthesising enzymes to digest the available substrate, absorbing water, and preparing to divide).
2. **==Exponential phase== (or **log phase**, `birth rate ≫ death rate`):** Food/nutrients and space are abundant, toxic waste concentration is negligible, and **no environmental limiting factors** restrict growth. Every cell or organism divides at its maximum physiological rate (`1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32...`), so the curve rises steeply.
3. **==Stationary phase== (`birth rate = death rate` around ==carrying capacity== `K`):** One or more **limiting factors** take effect—food/substrate begins to run low, space becomes crowded, intraspecific competition intensifies, predators or infectious diseases spread rapidly through the dense population, or toxic metabolic waste products accumulate. The birth rate falls and the death rate rises until **birth rate equals death rate**, and the population size plateaus around the maximum number of individuals the environment can support, called its **carrying capacity (`K`)**.
4. **==Death phase== (`death rate > birth rate`):** In a closed container where no fresh nutrients are added and wastes are not removed, the food supply is exhausted and toxic metabolic wastes (such as ethanol in yeast or organic acids in bacteria) reach lethal levels, so the death rate exceeds the birth rate and the population crashes.

**Why human population growth looks like an extended exponential curve.** Over the past `250` years, the global human population has surged from under `1 billion` to over `8 billion`, tracing a steep **exponential (J-shaped) curve** without yet entering a global stationary phase. - **Never draw food chain arrows pointing from predator to prey:** Writing `Leopard → Deer → Grass` to mean "the leopard eats the deer" is the single commonest ecological mistake. Arrows (`→`) **always** point in the direction of **energy flow** (`Grass → Deer → Leopard`).

## 15.2 Atoms on loan: the water, carbon and nitrogen cycles

:::scene Marine Drive Monsoon Promenade & Dadar Kitchen Sink, Mumbai

Stand on the promenade at Marine Drive as the June southwest monsoon rolls in off the Arabian Sea. Warm ocean water that evaporated under the May sun has cooled high in the sky into towering cumulonimbus clouds. Within minutes, sheets of rain drum onto the basalt ridges of Malabar Hill and Borivali; some flows down storm drains and the Mithi River back to the sea, while some soaks into the soil where banyan and mango roots drink it up by osmosis and send it skyward through xylem vessels to evaporate out of leaf stomata as water vapour. Now step into a kitchen in Dadar where Kym is helping wash a fresh bundle of *methi* (fenugreek, *Trigonella foenum-graecum*) pulled soil-and-all from a suburban farm. Clinging to the pale branching roots are dozens of tiny, rounded **root nodules** the size of mustard seeds. Slice one open with a fingernail and its inside gleams **pinkish-red**—coloured by an oxygen-binding protein called **leghaemoglobin**, cousin to the haemoglobin in Kym's own red blood cells, shielding millions of ***Rhizobium* bacteria** as they crack atmospheric nitrogen gas (`N₂`) into the amino acids that will build Kym's enzymes and DNA.

Because Earth is virtually a closed system for matter, every carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen atom inside your body has been borrowed from the atmosphere, oceans, and soil—and will be returned to them. Three interlocking biogeochemical loops keep the biosphere stocked with raw materials (`0610 19.3`): the **water cycle**, the **carbon cycle**, and the **nitrogen cycle**.

### The water cycle and the carbon cycle

The **water cycle** is powered by solar thermal energy and gravity (Figure 15.3A):

- **==Evaporation==:** Heat from the Sun warms liquid water in oceans, lakes, rivers, and damp soil, giving water molecules enough kinetic energy to escape into the air as invisible **water vapour**.
- **==Transpiration==:** Plant roots absorb liquid water from the soil by osmosis (Chapter 3 `3.2`) and pull it up xylem vessels; inside leaves, water evaporates from the wet cell walls of spongy mesophyll cells and diffuses out through open **stomata** as water vapour (Chapter 5 `5.5`). Over a tropical rainforest like the Western Ghats, transpiration returns more than half of all rainfall directly back to the atmosphere.
- **==Condensation==:** Warm moist air rises, expands, and cools high in the troposphere; water vapour condenses around microscopic dust and salt nuclei into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals, forming **clouds**.
- **==Precipitation==:** When cloud droplets coalesce and grow too heavy to remain suspended, water falls back to Earth as **rain, snow, sleet, or hail**, returning to rivers and seas via **surface runoff** and **groundwater percolation**.

The **carbon cycle** revolves around atmospheric **carbon dioxide (`CO₂`, `~0.04%` of air, or `~426 ppm`)** and five core processes (Figure 15.3B):

1. **==Photosynthesis== (`the single biological entry doorway`):** Green plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria absorb `CO₂` from the air (or dissolved `HCO₃⁻`/`CO₂` in water) and use light energy trapped by chlorophyll to fix carbon atoms into **glucose (`C₆H₁₂O₆`)**, starch, cellulose, lipids, and proteins (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`).
2. **==Feeding== (and assimilation):** Animals eat plants (or other animals), digest their polymers with carbohydrases, proteases, and lipases (Chapter 7 `7.3`), and assimilate the carbon compounds into animal tissues.
3. **==Respiration==:** Producers, consumers, and decomposers all carry out **aerobic respiration** (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`) twenty-four hours a day, oxidising organic carbon compounds to release ATP energy and returning `CO₂` gas to the atmosphere.
4. **==Decomposition==:** When plants and animals die, or shed leaves, faeces, and urea, **decomposers** (saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) secrete extracellular enzymes onto the dead organic matter, absorb the soluble products, and respire them, releasing `CO₂` back to the air.
5. **==Fossilisation== and ==combustion==:** When dead plants, peat bog mosses, or marine plankton are buried under mud and sediment in **anaerobic, acidic, or high-pressure conditions** where decomposers cannot break them down completely, geological heat and pressure over millions of years transform their carbon into **fossil fuels** (**coal**, **crude oil**, and **natural gas**) or carbonate rocks (**limestone**). When humans burn fossil fuels or wood in power stations, vehicles, factories, and forest fires (**combustion**), stored carbon reacts with oxygen to release `CO₂` rapidly back into the atmosphere:

**Fossil fuel / Hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O**

### The nitrogen cycle: four bacterial guilds and the `N≡N` triple bond

Why do plants need nitrogen at all? As we saw in Chapter 5 (`5.3`) and Chapter 11 (`11.2–11.3`), carbohydrate molecules made in photosynthesis (`C₆H₁₂O₆`) contain only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. To build **amino acids**, **proteins** (enzymes, cell membrane carriers, rubisco, antibodies), **DNA**, **RNA**, and **ATP**, plants and animals must add **nitrogen (`N`)**.

At first glance, nitrogen looks abundant: **`78%` of Earth's atmosphere is nitrogen gas (`N₂`)**. Yet a plant starving in nitrogen-poor soil will turn yellow and stunted even though its leaves are bathed in `78% N₂` air. Why? Because the two nitrogen atoms in an `N₂` molecule are locked together by an extraordinarily strong **triple covalent bond (`N≡N`)** that neither plant chloroplasts nor animal enzymes can split. **No plant and no animal can fix `N₂` gas from the air.** Instead, non-leguminous plants must absorb soluble **nitrate ions (`NO₃⁻`)** (and some ammonium ions, `NH₄⁺`) dissolved in soil water through their **root hair cells by active transport** (Chapter 3 `3.4`).

The entire biosphere depends on four specialized guilds of soil bacteria—plus lightning and industrial chemistry—to keep nitrogen moving through the **nitrogen cycle** (`0610 19.3`, Figure 15.4):

| Nitrogen Cycle Stage | Chemical Transformation | Organisms / Agents & Environmental Conditions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **2. Excess amino acids undergo **deamination in the liver** (Chapter 8 `8.5`), converting the amino group into **urea**, which is excreted in urine. |

## 15.3 Footprints on the web: farming, deforestation, eutrophication and conservation

:::scene Powai Lake Outfall, Carter Road Mangroves & Thane Creek Flamingo Sanctuary, Mumbai

The water surface is choked with a thick emerald-green mat of floating algae and water hyacinth. Beneath that green blanket, the water is dark, murky, and foul-smelling; no submerged pondweed survives on the lake bed, and several silver tilapia fish float belly-up near the reeds, their gill covers flared in water that has been stripped of dissolved oxygen. An hour later, ride the train west to **Carter Road in Bandra** at low tide, where tangled shopping bags and polystyrene cups snag on the pencil-like breathing roots (`pneumatophores`) of *Avicennia marina* mangroves. Yet just across the harbour in the **Thane Creek Flamingo Sanctuary**, protected mudflats and treated wetland buffers host tens of thousands of lesser and greater flamingos—living proof of what happens when an ecosystem is monitored, legally protected, and managed sustainably.

How did a single species of bipedal primate come to reshape every nutrient cycle on Earth? Modern agriculture and industry have solved the problem of feeding over eight billion humans, but those same methods place intense pressure on habitats, water bodies, the atmosphere, and wild species (`0610 Topic 20`).

### Modern food production, monocultures, and intensive livestock

During the twentieth century—and especially across India during the Green Revolution—food production per hectare multiplied through five technologies (`0610 20.1`):

1. **Agricultural machinery** (tractors, seed drills, combine harvesters), allowing farmers to cultivate and harvest much larger land areas rapidly and efficiently.
2. **Chemical fertilisers** (`N-P-K` compounds supplying nitrate `NO₃⁻`, phosphate `PO₄³⁻`, and potassium `K⁺` ions), replenishing limiting soil minerals so crops grow faster and synthesize more protein and chlorophyll.
3. **Insecticides**, killing insect pests that eat crop leaves/fruits or act as vectors of plant viruses, thereby increasing crop yield and quality.
4. **Herbicides**, killing competing **weeds** so the crop receives a larger share of sunlight, soil water, and mineral ions.
5. **Selective breeding** (Chapter 14 `14.4`), breeding high-yielding, disease-resistant crop varieties and high-producing livestock.

However, two modern farming systems carry severe ecological trade-offs:

| Farming System | Agricultural Advantages | Ecological Disadvantages & Risks (`0610 20.1`) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Large-scale ==monoculture==** *(growing a single genetically uniform crop species across vast fields year after year)* | Uniform plant height and ripening time allow efficient mechanised sowing, spraying, and harvesting; tailored fertiliser and herbicide schedules maximize single-crop yield. | **1. Loss of ==biodiversity==:** Replaces diverse wild habitats and food webs with one plant species.<br>**2. Pest and pathogen explosions:** An unbroken sea of genetically identical hosts allows specialist insect pests or fungal diseases to multiply exponentially, requiring heavy **insecticide** spraying (which also kills beneficial pollinators like bees, kills natural pest predators, and selects for insecticide-resistant pests).<br>**3. Soil mineral depletion:** Every plant withdraws the exact same mineral ions from the same root depth, forcing heavy fertiliser use. |
| **Intensive livestock production** *(keeping large numbers of cattle, pigs, or poultry indoors in high-density temperature-controlled pens)* | Maximises energy efficiency and growth rate by restricting movement and keeping surroundings warm (so animals lose less energy in **aerobic respiration** and heat, `15.1`), producing cheaper meat, milk, and eggs on less land. | **1. Rapid disease spread:** Crowded conditions allow viral and bacterial infections to sweep rapidly from animal to animal.<br>**2. Overuse of antibiotics:** Routine prophylactic feeding of antibiotics selects strongly for **antibiotic-resistant bacteria** (`MRSA`, *Salmonella*, Chapter 14 `14.3`) that can infect humans.<br>**3. Pollution & ethical welfare:** Concentrated urine and faeces (**slurry**) pollute waterways (**eutrophication**), ruminant cattle release **methane (`CH₄`)**, and crowding causes animal stress and welfare concerns. |

### Habitat destruction and the four effects of deforestation

Around the world, natural habitats are destroyed when humans clear land for **crops and livestock grazing**, **housing and road infrastructure**, **extraction of timber and paper pulp**, **open-cast mining and quarrying**, and **polluting marine and freshwater environments** (`0610 20.2`). Because species in a food web are interdependent, removing even a single producer, pollinator, or predator species can trigger a trophic cascade across the entire community.

The most widespread form of habitat destruction is ==deforestation==—the clearing of forests on a massive scale. In the Cambridge syllabus (`0610 20.2`), you must be able to explain the undesirable effects of deforestation across **four interconnected domains** (Figure 15.6A):

1. **Reduction of biodiversity and ==extinction==:** Tropical rainforests (such as the Western Ghats and Silent Valley studied by **E. K. Janaki Ammal**, **Salim Ali**, and **Madhav Gadgil**) contain over half of Earth's terrestrial species in layered canopy, epiphyte, understorey, and forest-floor microhabitats. Felling trees destroys food sources, nesting sites, and shelter, fragmenting populations until species become endangered or **extinct** (no living individuals remain).
2. **Severe soil erosion and leaching:** In an intact forest, the leafy canopy breaks the mechanical force of heavy monsoon raindrops, while a dense network of tree roots binds soil particles together. Once trees are felled, heavy rain hammers bare ground and washes away the nutrient-rich topsoil (**soil erosion**) while dissolved nitrate and magnesium ions wash deep out of reach (**leaching**), leaving barren rock or laterite hardpan where forests cannot easily regrow.
3. **Flooding and disruption of the water cycle:** Without tree canopies intercepting rain, spongy humus absorbing water, and **transpiration** returning water vapour to the sky (`15.2`), rainwater rushes straight down bare hillsides as **surface runoff**. Simultaneously, eroded topsoil washes into rivers and **silts up riverbeds**, reducing channel capacity so rivers burst their banks and cause destructive **floods** downstream (while reduced transpiration makes regional climates drier).
4. **Increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide (`CO₂`):** Living forests are major carbon sinks that remove `CO₂` from the air via **photosynthesis**. When forests are cut down, less `CO₂` is removed by photosynthesis, and when felled timber and forest undergrowth are **burned (`combustion`)** or left to rot via **microbial decomposition (`respiration`)**, vast stores of carbon are oxidised back into atmospheric **`CO₂`**, intensifying global warming.

### Pollution: the seven-step eutrophication cascade, plastics, and the enhanced greenhouse effect

What killed the fish in that stagnant cove of Powai Lake? When farmers apply excess **nitrate (`NO₃⁻`) and phosphate (`PO₄³⁻`) chemical fertilisers** to fields before heavy rain, the soluble ions **leach** through soil into rivers and lakes. Exactly the same thing happens when **untreated sewage** (rich in organic matter, ammonium, and phosphates) or livestock slurry drains into water. The resulting ecological collapse is called ==eutrophication==, and it unfolds in a strict **seven-step causal sequence** (`0610 20.3`, Figure 15.5A):

1. **Increased availability of nitrate (`NO₃⁻`) and other mineral ions:** Leached chemical fertilisers or untreated sewage enter the lake, river, or estuary, steeply raising mineral ion concentrations.
2. **Rapid growth of algae and surface water plants (**==algal bloom==**):** Because mineral ions were previously the limiting factor for producer growth, unicellular green algae and floating plants multiply rapidly across the water surface.
3. **Algal bloom blocks sunlight from reaching submerged aquatic plants:** The thick green carpet at the surface absorbs and reflects light, so rooted aquatic plants deeper in the water column cannot photosynthesise.
4. **Death of submerged aquatic plants (and short-lived surface algae):** Starved of light, the submerged plants die and sink to the bottom as dead organic detritus (joined by dying surface algae once nutrients are exhausted).
5. **Rapid multiplication of aerobic decomposer bacteria:** Saprotrophic **bacteria** in the water and sediment feed on the huge pile of dead organic matter (and any raw sewage solids) and reproduce exponentially.
6. **Depletion of dissolved oxygen (`O₂`) via bacterial aerobic respiration:** As the vast population of decomposer bacteria carries out **aerobic respiration** (`C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O`), it consumes dissolved **`O₂`** from the water far faster than diffusion from the air can replace it (creating a spike in **biochemical oxygen demand, BOD**, and a sharp **oxygen sag**).
7. **Death of aerobic aquatic organisms (fish, crabs, insect larvae):** Without sufficient dissolved oxygen for their own aerobic respiration, fish and aquatic invertebrates **suffocate and die**, collapsing the aquatic food web and leaving only anaerobic bacteria thriving in the foul mud.

The second choice. Let us correct both errors:

1. **Error 1:** Nitrate ions (`NO₃⁻`) at agricultural runoff concentrations do **not** directly poison fish; they act as plant mineral nutrients that trigger a surface **algal bloom**.
2. **Error 2:** Living surface algae actually **produce** oxygen by photosynthesis during daylight (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`); they do not strip the water of oxygen during the day.
3. **True mechanism:** The surface algal bloom **blocks sunlight** to submerged aquatic plants below, causing those plants (and later the algae) to **die**. **Aerobic decomposer bacteria** then multiply rapidly on the dead organic matter and carry out **aerobic respiration**, which consumes the dissolved **`O₂`** in the water and causes fish to suffocate.
:::

:::evidence USA, 1962 & Silent Valley, Kerala, 1973–1983: Rachel Carson's Silent Spring and the Silent Valley rainforest campaign | Ecotoxicological & conservation proof
In **1962**, the marine biologist **Rachel Carson** published *Silent Spring*, compiling chemical and field evidence that persistent synthetic insecticides such as **DDT** are not broken down by soil or water decomposers; instead, they undergo **bioaccumulation** within tissues and **biomagnification** up food chains (`water → plankton → small fish → large fish → ospreys and bald eagles`), interfering with calcium deposition in bird oviducts so apex predators laid thin-shelled eggs that crushed during incubation. Alongside eutrophication, two global pollution streams threaten terrestrial, marine, and atmospheric systems (`0610 20.3` and `0893 8Be.02`):

- **==Non-biodegradable== plastics and ==bioaccumulation==:** Natural polymers like starch, cellulose, and proteins are hydrolysed by bacterial and fungal enzymes. Most synthetic plastics (polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, nylon) have carbon-carbon backbones that decomposer enzymes cannot recognise or cleave, making them **non-biodegradable**. In terrestrial and marine ecosystems (such as Mumbai's creeks and beaches), discarded plastics cause three major harms: (1) **Ingestion and starvation** (sea turtles mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish, and seabirds, whales, and fish swallow indigestible plastic that fills their stomachs); (2) **Entanglement and suffocation** (discarded ghost fishing nets and plastic rings trap marine mammals, turtles, and birds); and (3) **Microplastics, bioaccumulation, and biomagnification** (plastic fragments `< 5 mm` absorb persistent fat-soluble toxins that build up inside organisms via **bioaccumulation** and magnify up food chains via **biomagnification** to reach lethal concentrations in apex predators).
- **Greenhouse gases (`CO₂` and `CH₄`) and the ==enhanced greenhouse effect==:** Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface, which re-emits longer-wave **infrared (thermal) radiation**. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb part of this infrared radiation and re-radiate it back toward the surface. Human activities are rapidly raising the concentrations of two primary greenhouse gases (`0610 20.3`, Figure 15.5B): **carbon dioxide (`CO₂`)**, released by **combustion of fossil fuels** (coal, oil, natural gas) and **deforestation**; and **methane (`CH₄`)**, released by anaerobic methanogenic microorganisms in the digestive rumen of **cattle and other livestock**, in waterlogged anaerobic **rice paddy fields**, and in decomposing organic waste buried in anaerobic **landfill sites**. The resulting **enhanced greenhouse effect** drives **climate change**: global warming, melting polar ice caps and glaciers, thermal expansion of seawater and **sea-level rise**, extreme weather, and habitat shifts.

### Conservation: sustainable resources, forestry, fisheries, seed banks, and assisted reproduction

A ==sustainable resource== is one that is **produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment so that it does not run out** (`0610 20.4`). Whereas fossil fuels (`coal`, `oil`, `natural gas`) and metallic ores are non-renewable, biological resources such as **forests (timber)** and **fish stocks** can be maintained sustainably if managed with scientific rules (Figure 15.6B):

Why do species become endangered or extinct in the first place? Six major pressures drive species toward extinction (`0610 20.4`): **climate change**, **habitat destruction**, **hunting and poaching**, **overharvesting**, **pollution**, and **introduced (invasive) species** (which outcompete native species for food/space, predate them, or introduce novel diseases).

Crucially, once a population shrinks to a very small size (`a genetic bottleneck`), it faces a severe genetic danger even if hunting stops: **loss of genetic variation** (`0610 20.4`, linking Chapters 13 and 14). In a tiny population, closely related individuals are forced to mate with one another (**inbreeding**). Inbreeding reduces heterozygosity and steeply increases the proportion of offspring that are **homozygous for harmful recessive alleles** (**inbreeding depression**, lowering fertility and survival). Furthermore, without a wide pool of different alleles, the population lacks the **genetic variation** needed for natural selection to adapt if a new pathogen or climate shift arrives. This is why **seed banks** collect seeds from thousands of wild plants across different habitats (preserving allele diversity in a tiny space at `-20 °C`) and why zoo **captive breeding programmes** use DNA profiles, **artificial insemination (AI)**, and **in vitro fertilisation (IVF)** to pair unrelated mates across continents before reintroducing offspring into restored wild habitats.

## 15.4 Living factories & rewritable code: fermenters, plasmids, recombinant insulin and CRISPR

:::scene Irani Bakery at Dhobi Talao, Mumbai & Hindustan Antibiotics Fermenter Hall, Pimpri

It is five o'clock in the morning at a century-old Irani bakery near Dhobi Talao in south Mumbai. Inside the warm proofing trough, a baker punches down a pillowy mound of *brun-pav* bread dough that has doubled in volume overnight. Millions of microscopic **yeast** cells (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) have been respiring anaerobically through the flour sugars, blowing tiny bubbles of **carbon dioxide (`CO₂`)** into the stretchy gluten network; when the trays slide into the wood-fired brick oven at `220 °C`, the trapped gas expands into a crisp, airy crumb while every trace of **ethanol** evaporates up the chimney. Two hours away along the Mumbai–Pune expressway at **Pimpri**, three-storey stainless-steel **industrial fermenters** gleam inside the halls of Hindustan Antibiotics—descendants of the vessels launched in `1954` with the help of biochemist **Yellapragada SubbaRow**—where sterile air bubbles through spargers, motorised paddles churn nutrient broth, and cold-water jackets carry away kilowatts of microbial heat. And inside a cool refrigerator at a clinic in Parel sits a glass vial of **recombinant human insulin**—synthesised not by a pig's pancreas, but by *Escherichia coli* bacteria carrying a human gene stitched into their circular **plasmids**.

Long before humans knew what DNA or enzymes looked like—and long before **Louis Pasteur** proved in the `1850s` that living microorganisms drive fermentation—bakers and brewers were using microbes to transform food. Today, ==biotechnology== uses microorganisms (bacteria, yeast, and filamentous fungi) and their purified enzymes on an industrial scale, while ==genetic modification== (`genetic engineering`) rewrites the DNA of cells directly (`0610 Topic 21`).

### Why bacteria and fungi are ideal for biotechnology and genetic modification

Why do biotechnologists choose **bacteria** (and unicellular fungi like yeast) over multicellular plants or animals as living factories? Four biological features make bacteria uniquely suited (`0610 21.1`):

1. **Rapid reproduction rate:** Under optimum conditions (`37 °C`, abundant nutrients), bacteria divide by binary fission every **`20 minutes`**, growing from a single cell into billions of protein-synthesising cells within a few hours.
2. **Ability to make complex molecules:** Despite their simple prokaryotic structure (Chapter 2 `2.2`), bacteria possess `70S` ribosomes and metabolic pathways capable of synthesising complex polypeptides, enzymes, vitamins, and antibiotics—and because the **genetic code is universal** (deciphered by **Har Gobind Khorana** and colleagues after **Rosalind Franklin**, Watson, and Crick revealed the DNA helix, Chapter 11 `11.3`), a bacterium reads a human gene using the exact same triplet codons as a human cell.
3. **Few ethical concerns:** Manipulating, culturing, and harvesting bacteria or fungi in vats raises no animal-welfare or pain concerns compared with experimenting on vertebrates.
4. **Presence of ==plasmids==:** Alongside their main circular chromosome, bacteria carry **plasmids**—small, circular, double-stranded loops of DNA (Chapter 2 `2.2`) that are easy to extract from cells, cut open, splice foreign genes into, and transfer from one bacterium to another as genetic **vectors**.

### Inside an industrial fermenter: six controlled variables

When **Ernst Chain** and **Howard Florey** scaled up penicillin production from Alexander Fleming's mould (*Penicillium chrysogenum*) in the `1940s`—and when Hindustan Antibiotics built its deep-tank plant at Pimpri in `1954`—they used giant stainless-steel vessels called ==fermenters==. Today, industrial fermenters produce **penicillin** (from the fungus *Penicillium*), **mycoprotein** (high-protein vegetarian food from the fungus *Fusarium venenatum*), industrial enzymes, and **human insulin** (from genetically modified *E. coli*).

To keep billions of microorganisms growing at their maximum rate, an industrial fermenter continuously monitors and controls **six conditions** (`0610 21.2`, Figure 15.7B):

1. **==Aseptic precautions== (high-pressure steam sterilization):** Before inoculation, the entire stainless-steel vessel and all pipes are sterilized with high-pressure steam, and incoming air is passed through fine sterile filters. *Reason:* Kills foreign **contaminant bacteria and fungi** so they cannot compete with the culture microorganism for nutrients/oxygen or contaminate the medical/food product with toxic waste.
2. **Nutrients (carbon and nitrogen inlets):** Measured supplies of **glucose, starch, or molasses** (providing a carbon source and energy for respiration) plus **ammonia (`NH₃`), ammonium ions (`NH₄⁺`), or nitrate ions** (providing a nitrogen source for **amino acid and protein synthesis**) and mineral salts are fed in through sterile pipes.
3. **Optimum temperature (temperature probe + cold-water cooling jacket):** As trillions of bacteria or fungal hyphae respire rapidly inside a giant tank, they release huge amounts of **thermal energy (heat)**. Cold water circulating through an outer **water-cooled jacket** absorbs and carries away this excess heat to maintain the exact **optimum temperature** (`~26–28 °C` for *Penicillium*, `37 °C` for *E. coli*). *Reason:* If temperature rises too high (`>45 °C`), the microorganisms' **enzymes denature** and the culture dies; if temperature falls too low, kinetic energy drops and enzyme-catalysed reactions become too slow.
4. **Optimum pH (pH probe + acid/alkali inlets):** Dissolving `CO₂` and metabolic acids shift the broth pH. Electronic pH probes trigger automatic addition of dilute **acid or alkali (buffers)** to hold pH steady at the optimum (`e.g. pH 6.5–7.0`). *Reason:* Extreme pH breaks ionic bonds in enzyme active sites, causing **denaturation** (Chapter 4 `4.3`).
5. **Oxygenation (sterile air sparger):** For aerobic microorganisms (*Penicillium*, *Fusarium*, aerobic *E. coli*), sterile compressed air or oxygen is bubbled through a perforated ring (**sparger**) at the bottom of the tank. *Reason:* Supplies dissolved **oxygen (`O₂`)** required for **aerobic respiration** to generate ATP for rapid growth and protein synthesis.
6. **Agitation (motor-driven stirring paddles / impellers):** Rotating paddles mix the liquid culture continuously. *Reason:* Prevents microorganisms from settling to the bottom, keeps every cell in uniform contact with **nutrients and dissolved oxygen**, and ensures an **even temperature and pH** throughout the entire tank.

### Genetic modification: the five-step recombinant human insulin pipeline

In Chapter 10 (`10.4` and the companion story of **Frederick Banting**, Charles Best, and **Dilip Mahalanabis**), we saw how diabetic patients whose pancreatic β-cells cannot secrete **insulin** were saved by insulin extracted from the pancreases of slaughtered pigs and cattle. Yet animal insulin had serious drawbacks: it differed by one to three amino acids from human insulin (causing allergic immune reactions in some patients), supply was tied to slaughterhouse numbers, and religious or ethical diets objected to porcine or bovine products.

==Genetic modification== (or **genetic engineering**) is **changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing, or inserting individual genes** (`0610 21.3`). Following the `1973` discovery of recombinant plasmids by **Herbert Boyer** and **Stanley Cohen**, scientists engineered *E. coli* bacteria to manufacture pure **human insulin** via a precise **five-step molecular pipeline** (`0610 21.3`, Figure 15.8A):

1. **Isolate and cut the human insulin gene using a ==restriction enzyme==:** The human insulin gene is isolated from a human cell and cut out of human chromosomal DNA using a specific **restriction enzyme** (`restriction endonuclease`, molecular scissors such as `EcoRI`). The restriction enzyme recognises a specific base sequence and makes a staggered cut across the double-stranded DNA, leaving short, exposed single-stranded overhangs of unpaired bases called ==sticky ends== (`e.g. -AATT-`).
2. **Cut a bacterial plasmid vector with the EXACT SAME restriction enzyme:** Circular **plasmids** are extracted from bacteria and cut open using the **same restriction enzyme**. Because the exact same restriction enzyme is used, the cut ends of the open plasmid carry **complementary sticky ends** (`-TTAA-`) that match the sticky ends on the human insulin gene.
3. **Join the human gene and plasmid using ==DNA ligase== to form a ==recombinant plasmid==:** The cut human insulin gene fragments and the cut open plasmids are mixed together. Their complementary sticky ends pair up by **complementary base pairing** (`A` hydrogen-bonds with `T`, `C` with `G`), and the enzyme **DNA ligase** ("molecular glue") forms covalent bonds along the sugar-phosphate backbone to seal them into a single circular molecule of **recombinant DNA** (**recombinant plasmid**).
4. **Insert the recombinant plasmid into host bacteria (`transformation`):** The recombinant plasmids are mixed with host *E. coli* bacteria (treated with calcium ions and a brief heat or electric shock) so that the bacteria take up the recombinant plasmids into their cytoplasm.
5. **Clone the transformed bacteria in an industrial fermenter and express the gene:** The genetically modified bacteria are cultured inside an **industrial fermenter** under optimum aseptic conditions (`37 °C`, `pH 7.0`, glucose/amino acid nutrients, sterile `O₂`, stirring). Every `20 minutes`, the bacteria divide by binary fission, copying (`cloning`) the recombinant plasmid into every daughter cell and transcribing and translating the human gene into pure **human insulin protein**, which is extracted, purified, and bottled for patients.

???
The second choice. Because two different restriction enzymes were used, the sticky ends on the human insulin gene (`-AATT-`) and the sticky ends on the bacterial plasmid (`-GATC-`) are **not complementary** (`A` only pairs with `T`, and `C` only pairs with `G`). Without complementary base pairing between sticky ends, the human gene cannot anneal into the open plasmid, so **DNA ligase** cannot seal them together into a recombinant plasmid. Using the **same restriction enzyme** on both the human DNA and the plasmid is essential.

### Genetically modified (GM) crops, selective breeding vs GM, and CRISPR-Cas9

Genetic modification is also used to engineer crop plants with novel traits (`0610 21.3`, Figure 15.8B):

| GM Crop Modification | How It Works & Agricultural Advantages | Disadvantages, Risks & Concerns (`0610 21.3`) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Insect-resistant crops** *(e.g. **Bt cotton** in Maharashtra & **Bt maize**)* | Inserted `cry` gene from the soil bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* causes the plant to make a crystal protein toxic specifically to bollworm and stem-borer caterpillars that chew the crop. **Advantages:** Higher crop yield, less crop damage, and far fewer broad-spectrum chemical insecticide sprays (protecting bees and farmworkers). | **Selection for resistant pests:** Constant exposure to Bt toxin selects for rare mutant caterpillars resistant to Bt (`natural selection`, Chapter 14 `14.3`), requiring farmers to plant non-Bt "refuge" rows. Potential effects on non-target insects. |
| **2. Herbicide-resistant crops** *(e.g. glyphosate-resistant **soya beans** & maize)* | Inserted bacterial gene makes the crop resistant to a specific broad-spectrum herbicide. **Advantages:** Farmers can spray the field after crop emergence to kill competing **weeds** with a single application without harming the crop, raising yield and reducing mechanical ploughing. | **Gene transfer to wild relatives ("superweeds"):** Wind or insect pollen from the GM crop could cross-pollinate closely related wild weed species, transferring the herbicide-resistance gene into wild weeds. Loss of field-margin weeds reduces food for wild insects and birds. |
| **3. Nutritionally enhanced crops** *(e.g. **==Golden Rice==**)* | Inserted genes from maize/daffodil and a soil bacterium enable rice endosperm to synthesize **beta-carotene**, which the human body converts into **Vitamin A** (Chapter 7 `7.1`). **Advantages:** Prevents childhood Vitamin A deficiency and night blindness in regions where polished white rice is the staple diet. | **Economic and seed concerns:** Patented GM seeds can be expensive for smallholder farmers if new seed must be purchased each season; consumer scepticism about GM labelling and long-term ecological monitoring. |

How does **genetic modification** (`Chapter 15`) compare with **selective breeding** (`artificial selection`, studied in Chapter 14 `14.4` with **E. K. Janaki Ammal**'s sugarcane and Gir cattle)?

1. **Source of genes:** Selective breeding can only select alleles **already present within the same species** (or closely related cross-fertile species); genetic modification can transfer a gene from **any species across kingdoms** (such as a human gene into *E. coli* or a bacterial gene into cotton).
2. **Precision:** Selective breeding mates whole organisms, shuffling thousands of alleles together during meiosis (Chapter 12 `12.3` and Chapter 13 `13.2`) and often dragging along unwanted linked alleles (`inbreeding depression`); genetic modification isolates and inserts **one single target gene** without altering the rest of the genome.
3. **Timescale:** Selective breeding requires **many generations** (years or decades) of selecting and breeding offspring; genetic modification produces a transformed cell line in **a single laboratory generation**.

Finally, in **2012**, **Jennifer Doudna** and **Emmanuelle Charpentier** transformed genetic engineering by adapting a natural bacterial antiviral defence system called **==CRISPR-Cas9==** (`Figure 15.8B`). Instead of inserting a foreign plasmid vector at random, scientists synthesize a `20`-nucleotide **guide RNA (`gRNA`)** whose base sequence matches a chosen address in a cell's own DNA next to a short marker (`PAM`). The guide RNA leads the **`Cas9` endonuclease** enzyme directly to that chromosomal locus to make a precise cut—allowing doctors and plant breeders to correct a single mutated letter (such as repairing the sickle-cell `HbS` allele we traced from **Gregor Mendel** and **L. D. Sanghvi** in Chapter 13 to balancing selection in Chapter 14, or editing stem cells first shown to carry the full genome by **Matthew Meselson** and **John Gurdon** in Chapter 12).

:::note
**Why a bacterial ribosome can translate a human gene.** Remember from Chapter 2 (`2.2`) and Chapter 11 (`11.3`) that although *E. coli* has `70S` ribosomes and no nucleus, the **triplet genetic code is universal** across almost all living organisms on Earth: the mRNA codon `AUG` codes for methionine, `GAG` codes for glutamic acid, and `UGU` codes for cysteine whether the ribosome sits inside a Borivali bacterium, a banyan leaf, or a human pancreatic β-cell.
:::

:::five
? State four reasons why **bacteria** are useful in biotechnology and genetic modification.
(1) **Rapid reproduction rate** (dividing every `~20 minutes` by binary fission); (2) **ability to synthesize complex molecules** using the universal genetic code; (3) **few ethical concerns** over their manipulation and culture; and (4) **presence of circular plasmids** that are easy to extract, modify, and transfer as vectors.
? Write the word and balanced chemical equations for **anaerobic respiration in yeast**, and explain why baked bread contains zero alcohol even though yeast produces ethanol in dough.
**Word equation:** `glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide`. **Balanced symbol equation:** `C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂`. During bread-making, **`CO₂` gas bubbles** get trapped in elastic gluten and make the dough rise, while the high temperature of baking in the oven (`>200 °C`, well above ethanol's boiling point of `78 °C`) **evaporates all the ethanol** (and kills the yeast).
? State the role of (a) **pectinase** in fruit juice production, (b) **proteases and lipases** in biological washing powders, and (c) **immobilised lactase** in producing lactose-free milk.
(a) **Pectinase** hydrolyses insoluble pectin gluing plant cell walls together, **increasing juice volume (yield)** and **clarifying cloudy juice**. (b) **Proteases and lipases** hydrolyse insoluble protein stains (blood, egg) into soluble amino acids and lipid stains (grease, butter) into soluble fatty acids and glycerol at low washing temperatures (`30–40 °C`). (c) **Immobilised lactase** (in alginate beads) hydrolyses milk **lactose into soluble glucose and galactose**, producing enzyme-free milk safe for lactose-intolerant people while allowing the beads to be reused continuously.
? Name six conditions that are monitored and controlled inside an **industrial fermenter**, and explain why the vessel is surrounded by a **water-cooled jacket**.
(1) **Aseptic steam sterilization**, (2) **nutrients** (glucose carbon source + ammonia/nitrate nitrogen source), (3) **optimum temperature**, (4) **optimum pH** (via pH probes and acid/alkali addition), (5) **oxygenation** (via a sterile air sparger), and (6) **agitation** (via motorised stirring paddles). The **water-cooled jacket** removes the intense **heat released by microbial respiration** so the temperature stays at the optimum (`e.g. 28 °C or 37 °C`) and does not **denature the microorganisms' enzymes**.
? List the five steps used to engineer bacteria to produce **human insulin**, naming the specific enzyme used to cut DNA (`leaving sticky ends`) and the specific enzyme used to join DNA molecules together.
(1) Cut the **human insulin gene** from human DNA using a **restriction enzyme**, leaving **sticky ends**; (2) cut a **bacterial plasmid** vector open using the **same restriction enzyme**, leaving **complementary sticky ends**; (3) mix the gene and plasmid with **DNA ligase** to join their sticky ends into a **recombinant plasmid**; (4) insert the **recombinant plasmid** into host **bacteria**; (5) culture the transformed bacteria in an **industrial fermenter** so they divide rapidly and express the gene to synthesize **human insulin**.
?? Explain why both the human DNA containing the insulin gene and the bacterial plasmid vector must be cut with the **exact same restriction enzyme**.
Cutting both DNA molecules with the **same restriction enzyme** produces **complementary sticky ends** (exposed unpaired bases that match by `A–T` and `C–G` complementary base pairing), allowing the human gene to anneal into the open plasmid before **DNA ligase** seals the sugar-phosphate backbone.
?? State two advantages and two potential disadvantages of growing **genetically modified (GM) crops** such as Bt cotton or herbicide-resistant soya beans.
**Advantages:** (1) **Higher crop yields** and reduced crop loss to pests or weeds; (2) **reduced spraying of broad-spectrum chemical insecticides** (`Bt crops`, protecting non-target pollinators) or prevention of nutritional deficiency (`Golden Rice` supplying provitamin A). **Disadvantages:** (1) Risk of **gene transfer via pollen to wild relatives** (creating herbicide-resistant "superweeds") or **natural selection for Bt-resistant insect pests**; (2) **higher cost of patented GM seeds** for smallholder farmers and reduced weed biodiversity in field margins.
?? State three differences between **selective breeding** (`Chapter 14`) and **genetic modification** (`Chapter 15`).
(1) **Gene source:** Selective breeding uses only alleles **within the same (or closely related) species**, whereas GM can transfer genes **between completely different species/kingdoms**; (2) **Precision:** Selective breeding shuffles thousands of alleles across whole genomes (risking linked undesirable alleles), whereas GM transfers **a single isolated target gene**; (3) **Timescale:** Selective breeding takes **many generations**, whereas GM produces a modified line in **one generation**.
:::

:::careful
- **Restriction enzymes vs DNA ligase (`0610 21.3`):** Never swap their jobs. **Restriction enzymes** *cut* DNA at specific base sequences to produce **sticky ends**. **DNA ligase** *joins* two DNA fragments together by sealing bonds along the sugar-phosphate backbone after complementary sticky ends have base-paired, forming **recombinant DNA**.
- **Plasmids vs bacterial chromosomes (`0610 21.1 & 21.3`):** The vector used to carry the human insulin gene into bacteria is a circular **plasmid**, not the main bacterial chromosome and not a ribosome.
- **Bread-making vs Bioethanol (`0610 21.2`):** In **bread-making**, it is **`CO₂` gas** that makes the dough rise (while ethanol evaporates during baking). In **biofuel production**, it is the **ethanol** that is collected and distilled as fuel.
:::

1. **Trophic energy & population limits (`15.1`):** At Station 1, forest producers fix `30,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` of solar energy into net plant biomass. Calculate how much energy (`kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`) reaches tertiary consumers (`Level 4`) at `10%` transfer efficiency per step, explain why no `6th`-level predator exists in the park, and state why energy cannot be recycled by decomposers.
2. **Nitrogen cycle & eutrophication (`15.2–15.3`):** Explain how wild legumes and soil bacteria keep the aerated forest soil at Station 1 fertile without chemical fertiliser, and trace the exact seven-step mechanism that caused dissolved oxygen at Station 2 to crash from `8.2 mg L⁻¹` to `1.4 mg L⁻¹`.
3. **Recombinant biotechnology (`15.4` & Chapters 10–11):** Describe the five-step process used to engineer *E. coli* bacteria to manufacture the human insulin inside the clinic vial, and explain why a prokaryotic bacterium can translate a human gene into the exact same amino acid sequence as a human pancreatic β-cell.
???

1. **Trophic energy & one-way flow (`15.1`):**
    - **Calculation:** `Level 1 (Producers) = 30,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` → `Level 2 (Primary consumers) = 3,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` → `Level 3 (Secondary consumers) = 300 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹` → **`Level 4 (Tertiary consumers) = 30 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`**.
    - **Why no 6th level exists:** Because `~90%` of energy is lost at each step (via aerobic respiration/heat, indigestible egested faeces, excreted urea, and uneaten parts), a `5th` level would receive only `3 kJ` and a `6th` level only `0.3 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹`—far below the minimum energy required to sustain a viable breeding predator population.
    - **Why energy is never recycled:** Decomposers oxidise dead organic matter by respiration and release its remaining chemical energy as **thermal energy (heat)** to the atmosphere, which chloroplasts cannot trap; decomposers recycle **mineral ions and carbon/nitrogen atoms**, never energy.
2. **Nitrogen cycle at Station 1 & Eutrophication at Station 2 (`15.2–15.3`):**
    - **Station 1 fertility:** Mutualistic **nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium* bacteria** inside the pink root nodules of wild legumes (together with free-living soil *Azotobacter* and monsoon lightning) convert atmospheric **`N₂ → NH₄⁺` / amino acids**. When leaves and roots fall, **decomposers** break proteins down into **`NH₄⁺`** (**ammonification**), and because the hillside soil is well-drained and aerated with `O₂`, **aerobic nitrifying bacteria** oxidise **`NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻`** (**nitrification**) for root hair active transport.
    - **Station 2 eutrophication (`7 steps`):** (1) Leached **`NO₃⁻` fertiliser and sewage** (`24.0 mg L⁻¹`) enter the water; (2) they stimulate a dense surface **algal bloom** (`95%` cover); (3) the surface bloom **blocks sunlight** (`8%` transmission) to submerged aquatic plants below; (4) **submerged plants die** and sink as organic detritus; (5) **aerobic decomposer bacteria** multiply rapidly on the dead organic matter; (6) the bacteria carry out **aerobic respiration** (`BOD = 16.4 mg L⁻¹`), stripping **dissolved `O₂`** down to `1.4 mg L⁻¹`; (7) **tilapia fish suffocate and die** from lack of `O₂`.
3. **Recombinant human insulin & the universal code (`15.4` & Chapters 10–11):**
    - **Five-step pipeline:** (1) Isolate and cut the **human insulin gene** from human DNA using a **restriction enzyme**, leaving **sticky ends**; (2) cut a circular **bacterial plasmid** vector using the **exact same restriction enzyme**, creating **complementary sticky ends**; (3) mix the gene and plasmid with **DNA ligase** to seal the sugar-phosphate backbone and form a **recombinant plasmid**; (4) insert the **recombinant plasmid** into host ***E. coli* bacteria**; (5) culture the transformed bacteria in a steam-sterilized, water-jacket-cooled **industrial fermenter** (`37 °C`, `pH 7.0`, nutrients, sterile `O₂`, stirring paddles) so they divide every `20 minutes` by binary fission and express pure **human insulin**.
    - **Why bacteria translate human mRNA accurately:** Because all living organisms share the **universal triplet genetic code** (`Chapter 11`): the bacterial `70S` ribosomes and tRNAs read the exact same mRNA codons (`AUG`, `GAG`, `UGU`, etc.) as human `80S` ribosomes, assembling the identical `51`-amino-acid human insulin polypeptide that regulates blood glucose homeostasis (`Chapter 10`).
