Chapter 2 gave our microscopic city a boundary. Now there is a problem at every doorway. Oxygen must enter, waste must leave, and water must stay within useful limits. A membrane that blocked everything would make a very secure, very short-lived cell.

Walk along Carter Road and look towards the mangroves from the public path. Their roots occupy mud reached by salty tides. Put an ordinary garden plant in similarly salty conditions and it may lose water, even with wet soil around it. The mangrove has no exemption from physics. Its survival depends on the same rules that can make a potato strip go limp in a kitchen cup. To understand those roots, begin with a crowded train.

## 3.1 Blind bounces, not wishes

:::scene Mumbai, a crowded local train doorway

At a station, people pass through a train doorway in both directions. Counting only the people getting off gives you a poor account of how the carriage population changes. You must subtract those getting on. That difference is the **net** change.

Keep the two-way counting. Leave people's decisions behind. A molecule has no destination in mind, and it cannot inspect the other side of a membrane for an empty seat.

:::predict
Two equal-sized chambers contain the same gas at the same temperature. One begins with more molecules than the other. An opening connects them. Which account best predicts the change after the opening is made?
- Crossings occur only from the more crowded chamber until concentrations match
- Crossings occur both ways, with more expected from the more concentrated side
- Equal temperature means the expected numbers crossing each way must already be equal
???
The second account follows from random motion. More molecules near one side of the opening give more opportunities to cross from that side. Individual molecules need no instruction about the other chamber.
:::

:::evidence A spreading colour, then a stricter test | Repeatable observation
Place a drop of food colouring in water and its colour spreads. This establishes something observable: the coloured material changes its distribution. It does not, by itself, establish how. A falling drop, small temperature differences and accidental stirring can move whole parcels of water too.

Our chamber model asks a more limited question. If all particles follow the same blind movement rule, with no preference for the emptier side, can their distribution spread out? Count crossings in both directions. Starting with unequal concentrations usually gives a net transfer towards the less concentrated side; starting equally still gives crossings. This is a test of what the rule predicts, not a substitute for observing real molecules. The distinction matters: a convincing animation is evidence about its own instructions before it is evidence about nature.
:::

Gas molecules continually move and collide. A small dissolved molecule also changes direction through encounters with the surrounding liquid. Thermal motion does not stop when the distribution looks even. In [*The Invisible World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-chem/), you met this restless molecular motion; here we count one of its consequences.

Take an invented model with 80 particles on the left and 20 on the right, in equal volumes. Suppose each has the same one-in-ten chance of crossing during an interval. We expect about eight crossings rightward and two leftward. That gives a net six rightward. These are averages for our chosen rule, not a prediction that exactly six real molecules cross a cell each second.

The amount of a substance in a given volume is its **concentration**. Its net spreading from higher to lower concentration, through random motion, is ==diffusion==. The change in concentration across a distance is a **concentration gradient**. More particles in a larger volume need not mean a higher concentration; that is why the two chamber volumes matter.

:::note Diffusion
The net movement of particles down their concentration gradient as a result of random movement. Individual particles can move in either direction.
:::

At 50 particles on each side, our rule predicts about five crossings each way. The expected net change is zero. A small sample can still fluctuate: 49 and 51 is no surprise. This moving balance is **dynamic equilibrium**. “No net movement” describes the difference between two flows, not the behaviour of each molecule.

:::fig c3_diffusion() | Count both directions. The crossing chances and particle counts are chosen model numbers; dots stand for groups. Near equilibrium, individual trials fluctuate around equal concentrations.
:::live diffusion_membrane {"lesson":true}

A respiring cell can keep using oxygen, maintaining a lower oxygen concentration inside than outside. Oxygen then has a net inward diffusion. Carbon dioxide made inside can have its own outward gradient at the same time. Each substance has its own account; the membrane does not send “everything” one way.

A steeper gradient and a larger exchange area increase net transfer, other conditions being equal. A shorter path helps too: a particle's wandering includes many steps that undo earlier progress. Higher temperature generally speeds diffusion because molecular motion is faster. The cell spends no ATP to instruct these crossings. Their energy comes from molecular motion.

:::aside
For a quantitative connection to physics: At about 27 °C, the characteristic thermal energy scale called **kᵦT** is about **4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J**. Here T is temperature in kelvin, and kᵦ is the constant that converts temperature into this energy scale. It is a scale for comparison, not a fixed energy carried by every molecule.
:::

:::five Diffusion
? What is it?
Net spreading down a concentration gradient, produced by particles moving randomly in both directions.
? Why is it called this?
The word comes from a Latin root meaning to spread or pour out. The name describes the result; random movement explains it.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains how a substance can spread without a cell directing each particle or paying ATP for each crossing.
? Why does it work?
With more particles in an equal volume on one side, more have opportunities to cross from that side. Opposing flows usually differ until concentrations become similar.
? How does it help?
It explains oxygen uptake, carbon dioxide loss and why a short exchange distance matters.
?? How do we know?
Spreading observations and molecular evidence support the account. A random-walk model lets us test whether blind motion alone can produce net spreading.
?? What it isn't
A decision to find space, or a rule that every particle travels downhill at every instant.
?? Where does it stop working?
Bulk flow can also transport material. A membrane that excludes a substance prevents that crossing even if a concentration gradient exists.
:::

:::careful
> “Once it is evenly spread, the molecules have finished moving.”

They keep moving. Near equilibrium, crossings in opposite directions balance on average. Closing the opening stops transfer between chambers; it does not switch off motion within each chamber.
:::

:::home A drop with no spoon
Use food colouring and a clear glass of cool tap water on a stable tray. Let the water settle, add one small drop gently, and watch without stirring. Sketch the coloured region at the start and after a few minutes. Keep the glass away from food and drinking cups afterwards.

If a coloured plume falls or swirls, record it. That is a clue that whole water parcels are moving, so you cannot use this glass to measure a pure diffusion rate. Compare your observation with the model's isolated random movement rule. Empty the glass, rinse it and wipe spills; do not taste the coloured water.
:::

:::school
For 0610 Topic 3.1, include **net movement**, **higher to lower concentration**, and **random movement** in your explanation; the energy comes from the particles' kinetic energy. The four named rate factors are surface area, temperature, concentration gradient and distance.
:::

:::check
An equal-volume model starts with 80 particles left and 20 right. You count 17 rightward crossings and 11 leftward crossings. How many particles are now on each side, and has movement been one-way?
???
The signed net transfer is 17 − 11 = 6 rightward. Left now has 80 − 6 = 74 and right has 20 + 6 = 26; the total remains 100. Eleven crossings were leftward, so movement was two-way even while the net change was rightward.
:::

## 3.2 Water potential and the selective gate

:::scene Baltimore, a water-channel experiment published in 1992

Two frog egg cells sit in very dilute water. They look much alike at first. One soon swells much faster than the other. The important difference is a protein placed in its membrane.

The surprise concerns the boundary. A cell is surrounded by water, and filled with water. Why should changing a protein alter how fast water crosses?

:::predict
Researchers arrange for some frog egg cells to make a suspected water-channel protein. Control eggs do not receive the instructions for that protein. What result would support the channel idea?
- The two groups always swell at exactly the same rate
- Eggs making the protein take up water much faster in dilute surroundings
- Only salt can enter eggs making the protein
???
The second result supports a water pathway. The control comparison matters: swelling by itself would not identify which protein changed water permeability.
:::

:::evidence The protein that changed the boundary | 1992 publication
Gregory Preston, Peter Agre and their colleagues tested a protein originally found in red blood cells. They arranged for frog egg cells to make it, then compared those eggs with controls in dilute water. The eggs making the protein took up water and swelled much more rapidly. Controls changed little over the comparison period.

The experiment connected a particular protein with increased water permeability. It did not mean control membranes were absolutely waterproof. Nor did it show that the protein spent ATP pumping water. Water already had a direction of net transfer under those conditions; the protein provided a faster route. The team called this kind of protein an aquaporin, a water pore. Agre's account dates the first test to 1991; the research paper appeared in 1992.
:::

:::story p-agre

A membrane's oily middle makes passage much easier for some substances than others. Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross readily. Charged salt ions interact strongly with surrounding water; entering the oily layer is energetically unfavourable. Sucrose also crosses that layer poorly. This is more than a kitchen sieve sorting by size. Electrical charge and chemical interactions matter.

Water can cross the lipid layer, and many cells have faster water-selective protein channels: **aquaporins**. A typical water-specific aquaporin provides a route for water while excluding sucrose and salt ions. Other proteins can provide routes for particular ions or sugars. “Blocked here” never means “blocked by every possible cell membrane”.

The boundary is ==partially permeable==: it permits some substances to cross more readily than others.

:::note Partially permeable
Allowing some substances to pass through while restricting others. A membrane's permeability depends on both its lipid layer and the proteins present.
:::

**First, keep the pressures equal.** Picture a small compartment holding sugar solution. Its membrane floor passes water but retains sugar; outside is more dilute water. Water molecules cross both ways. Dissolved substances change water's chemical environment, so at the same pressure the opposing transfers do not balance: there is net entry from the more dilute side. Sugar need not cross at all. The net passage of water across a partially permeable membrane is ==osmosis==.

**Next, let pressure build.** Give the compartment a movable lid. Entry raises the lid. If the lid meets resistance, pressure rises inside and opposes further net entry. With enough opposing pressure, the two transfers balance. Sugar is still more concentrated inside, and individual water molecules still cross. A plant's supporting wall will let a cell build pressure in a related way.

**Now keep both effects in one account.** We use a quantity called ==water potential== to predict net water movement. Dissolved substances lower it; pushing on water raises it. Net water transfer goes from higher total water potential to lower total water potential. Pure water at the reference pressure is assigned zero. Below zero, −0.30 is higher than −0.80, just as −3 is higher than −8 on a number line.

Start with a chosen outside value of −0.30 MPa and an inside solute contribution of −0.80 MPa, with no added pressure inside. Which side is higher? The outside, so net entry is favoured. MPa means megapascals, a pressure unit used for water potential. Add +0.50 MPa of pressure inside and its total becomes −0.30 MPa: the two sides now match. We have changed the pressure account without making the solute concentrations equal.

At equal pressure, dilute-to-concentrated is a useful shortcut when the solute cannot cross. With unequal pressures, compare the total water potentials. “Concentrated solution” means more dissolved substance per volume, not higher water potential.

:::note Water potential
A measure used to predict net water movement, affected by dissolved substances and pressure. Water moves overall from higher to lower water potential.
:::

:::note Osmosis
Net movement of water molecules from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane.
:::

:::fig c3_water_gate() | The water pore and the pressure account are different views of the same problem. The enlarged channel is schematic; the numerical potentials are chosen examples. A positive pressure can balance a solute difference.
:::live water_potential

Use the live model in that same order: compare the solute contributions first, then add inside pressure. Predict the direction before moving a control. At equal total potentials, look for two-way exchange rather than a frozen boundary.

:::five Osmosis and water potential
? What is it?
Osmosis is net water passage through a partially permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential. Water potential includes the effects of solute and pressure.
? Why is it called this?
Osmosis comes from a Greek word connected with pushing or impulse. It is a historical name, not an explanation that salt pulls deliberately on water.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains water movement across boundaries that water can cross more readily than particular dissolved substances.
? Why does it work?
Dissolved substances change water's chemical environment. Pressure also affects transfer, so unequal opposing flows can balance even with unequal solutes.
? How does it help?
It predicts whether a cell gains water, loses it or has no sustained net water change.
?? How do we know?
Membrane experiments measure water transfer. Adding a water-channel protein increases permeability; opposing pressure can balance net entry.
?? What it isn't
Sugar or salt moving across the membrane. An aquaporin is a pathway, not an ATP-driven water pump.
?? Where does it stop working?
The dilute-to-concentrated shortcut assumes comparable pressures and a solute that cannot readily cross. Different pressures or permeant solutes require a fuller account.
:::

:::careful
> “Salt sucks water through any barrier.”

A gradient cannot make an impermeable barrier pass water. Where passage is possible, net movement depends on water potential, including pressure. Salt ions may remain on their own side throughout.
:::

:::school
In 0610 Extended Topic 3.2, connect **water molecules**, **higher to lower water potential**, and **a partially permeable membrane**. Use dilute-to-concentrated as the equal-pressure example; avoid the ambiguous phrase “concentration of solution”.
:::

:::check
A supported cell has a solute contribution of −0.80 MPa. Outside water potential stays at −0.30 MPa. Predict net movement first with no added pressure inside, then with +0.50 MPa inside. In the second case, must every water molecule stop crossing?
???
Without added pressure, outside is higher (−0.30 > −0.80 MPa), so net entry is favoured. With the pressure contribution, inside total is −0.80 + 0.50 = −0.30 MPa, matching outside. There is no sustained net transfer under these conditions, but water still crosses in both directions. Equal water potential does not require equal solute concentration.
:::

## 3.3 Balloons inside boxes

:::scene Mumbai, two cups and a raw potato

A raw potato strip can become firmer in plain water and limper in salty water. The water outside is visible; the changing water inside its cells is hidden. A potato gives you a way to feel a consequence of a microscopic boundary.

Before cutting anything, compare two kinds of boundary. An animal cell has a cell membrane. A plant cell also has a supporting wall outside that membrane. The difference changes what happens after water enters.

:::predict
An animal cell and a plant cell are placed in very dilute surroundings. Water can enter both. Which outcome is most plausible?
- Both must burst because water enters both
- The plant wall can support rising pressure, while an animal cell may swell and burst
- The plant wall completely blocks water, leaving the cell dry
???
The second outcome. The wall allows pressure to build as water enters. That pressure raises internal water potential and can balance further net entry. A red blood cell lacks that supporting wall.
:::

:::evidence The outline that stays, and the contents that retreat | Repeatable microscopy
At school, compare a suitable plant epidermis under a microscope before and after adding a sufficiently concentrated salt solution. Watch the outer cell outlines and the membrane-bound contents separately. With enough water loss, the contents contract away from the wall; the outer wall remains largely in place. Replacing the salt solution with water can reverse the change if the cells have not been badly damaged.

That comparison identifies which structure changed. A vague instruction to “look for shrunken cells” would miss the important distinction between wall and contents. A potato becoming limp supports a water-loss interpretation at tissue scale, but a ruler cannot show membrane separation. The microscope and the kitchen strip answer related questions at different scales.
:::

In dilute surroundings, a human red blood cell gains water. Its membrane accommodates some swelling, but excessive swelling can rupture it. In a concentrated solution of solute that does not readily enter, the cell loses water and shrivels. Neither event requires the solute to rush into the cell first.

A plant cell's vacuole can take up water and enlarge. The contents press outwards against the wall; the wall resists further expansion. Internal pressure rises and opposes further net entry. A cell held firm by this pressure is ==turgid==, and the pressure is ==turgor pressure==. Soft stems and leaves gain support from many such pressurised cells together. Think of a filled balloon inside a supporting box, while remembering that a real wall is a mesh and a real membrane is a molecular layer.

:::note Turgid
Firm with water and turgor pressure. Turgor is the pressure of the cell contents against the supporting wall.
:::

As water leaves, the vacuole becomes smaller and turgor falls. A cell with little turgor is ==flaccid==. After sufficient loss, the membrane-bound contents pull away from the wall: ==plasmolysis==. This names a visible separation, not every instance of wilting. A limp strip alone does not prove that every cell inside it has plasmolysed.

:::note Flaccid
Having little turgor pressure, so the cell is less firm.
:::

:::note Plasmolysis
Separation of the membrane-bound contents from the plant cell wall after sufficient water loss by osmosis.
:::

:::fig c3_cells() | The wall changes the outcome. These are schematic endpoints at different enlargements, not a measured time series. The middle plant has little turgor; matching solute concentrations alone do not describe every pressurised plant cell.
:::live osmosis_cells

The intact wall is not the selective gate responsible for this water exchange. The cell membrane is. Water entering a plant cell also crosses the vacuole's membrane on its way into the vacuole. Our simple envelope drawing omits that extra molecular boundary so the pressure comparison remains readable.

:::five Turgor and plasmolysis
? What is it?
Turgor is pressure supported by the wall. Plasmolysis is the separation of membrane-bound contents from that wall after substantial water loss.
? Why is it called this?
Turgid means swollen. Plasmolysis combines word-parts for formed substance and loosening; here the visible clue is the contents drawing away from the wall.
? What problem did it solve?
It connects water movement inside individual cells with the firmness or wilting of a whole leaf or potato strip.
? Why does it work?
Water entry expands the contents against a resisting wall and raises pressure. Water loss reduces pressure and can shrink the contents away from the wall.
? How does it help?
It explains why plants can stand firm without a skeleton and why the same dilute water has different effects on plant cells and red blood cells.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy distinguishes the outer wall from the retreating contents; tissue experiments compare firmness and dimensions before and after immersion.
?? What it isn't
A wall sucking up water, or the rigid wall shrinking by the same amount as the vacuole.
?? Where does it stop working?
Severe damage may prevent recovery. Whole tissues include cut cells and several cell types, so their measured changes are not an exact picture of one cell.
:::

:::careful
> “The salty cup makes the potato limp because salt enters and bursts its cells.”

The initial explanation is net water loss from cells to lower-water-potential surroundings. Loss of turgor makes tissue less firm. Salt permeability and damage can matter during longer exposure, but they do not turn this into the dilute-water bursting of an animal cell.
:::

:::home Two cups, six strips, one fair comparison
An adult should cut the potato and handle the knife. Use one raw potato to make six similar strips, about 60 mm long and 5 mm across. Prepare one cup with 100 mL of tap water. For the other, dissolve 5 g of table salt and add water to a **final volume of 100 mL**. This chosen 5% mass/volume solution is not a recipe for seawater. Without a suitable scale, a level teaspoon is an approximate alternative; label the concentration “approximate”.

Measure and label each strip. Put three in each cup, fully immersed, at the same room temperature and for the same time. Keep cups covered loosely against spills and dust. Predict changes in length and firmness. After 30 minutes, remove the strips, blot each gently in the same way, and measure again without squeezing. If the difference is unclear, note that and compare after a longer immersion, up to an hour. Never replace an awkward result with the expected one.

Record initial length, final length and firmness for each strip; compare the three-strip averages and their spread. Calculate percentage length change as **100 × (final − initial) ÷ initial**. A ruler with millimetre marks may miss a small change. A reliable mass measurement, if a sufficiently precise scale is available, gives another comparison; consistent blotting matters. A kitchen scale that reads only whole grams is too coarse for small changes in these strips. You can transfer the salty strips to fresh water to test possible recovery, without promising it.

Different potatoes, damaged cut surfaces and measurement uncertainty affect results. Do not eat the used potato or taste the liquids. Dispose of them the same day, wash hands and clean the cups and board.
:::

:::school
For 0610 Topic 3.2, explain the water-potential direction and use **turgid**, **turgor pressure**, **flaccid**, or **plasmolysis** for the observed state. Reserve plasmolysis for contents separating from the wall; your kitchen strip tests tissue changes rather than showing that separation directly.
:::

:::recall 2
Does the wall replace the cell membrane in a plant cell?
???
No. The wall supports the cell; the membrane controls passage. The wall lies outside the membrane, and both remain important when water enters or leaves.
:::

:::check
A plant cell's membrane-bound contents have visibly pulled away from an intact wall. Which way has net water movement occurred, and what would you need to know before predicting recovery?
???
The cell has lost water overall and is plasmolysed. Fresh surroundings with higher water potential could favour re-entry, but recovery also depends on the cell remaining sufficiently undamaged. An intact-looking wall alone does not establish a living, functional membrane.
:::

## 3.4 Pushing uphill: roots and the salty sea

:::scene Aarhus, crab nerve membranes, 1957

A membrane can provide a route for an ion. That leaves another puzzle. How can cells maintain more of an ion on the side where it is already concentrated? If electrical effects do not oppose it, passive spreading through an open route would tend to undo the concentration difference.

Jens Christian Skou investigated an enzyme in crab nerve membranes that broke down ATP. Its activity depended on the ions present. The link between chemical reactions and an uneven distribution of ions was becoming something experimenters could test.

:::predict
A root hair cell contains more nitrate ions per volume than the surrounding soil solution. Which explanation could sustain further nitrate uptake against that concentration gradient?
- A nitrate pathway alone guarantees further accumulation, regardless of electrical conditions
- Increasing membrane area alone can sustain uptake against an unfavourable combined gradient
- A transporter couples nitrate entry to an energy source, such as another ion gradient
???
The third explanation supplies the missing account. A root can use ATP to establish another gradient, then couple nitrate entry to movement down that gradient. Concentration is part of the story for ions; electrical forces matter too.
:::

:::evidence ATP chemistry in the membrane | Skou's 1957 paper
Skou studied preparations of crab nerve membranes. He measured ATP breakdown while changing the ions in the mixture. Sodium and potassium affected the enzyme's activity. These chemical responses helped identify machinery involved in maintaining unequal ion distributions across a living membrane.

This was a clue linking ATP-consuming chemistry with ion transport, rather than a film of every individual pumping step. Later work established the sodium–potassium pump's transport mechanism. The experiment did not identify a nitrate pump in a root, and those two machines should not be confused. What carries across to the root problem is the principle: a membrane protein can couple a chemical energy transfer to transport that passive spreading alone would not maintain.
:::

:::story p-skou

When ATP breakdown is coupled to changes in a carrier protein's shape, the protein can move ions against an unfavourable gradient. Such energy-coupled movement is ==active transport==. A protein path alone is not enough to identify it: passive channels and carriers exist too. For a charged ion, the distinguishing question is whether energy is used to maintain transport against its combined concentration and electrical gradient.

:::note Active transport
Energy-coupled movement across a membrane against a concentration gradient, or, for ions, against the combined concentration and electrical gradient. ATP may power transport directly or establish a gradient that powers another transport step.
:::

Follow a root hair's relay in three frames.

1. **Build the gradient.** One membrane protein uses ATP to export hydrogen ions, H⁺. The resulting difference in proton concentration and electrical charge stores energy.
2. **Use the gradient.** Protons return down that combined gradient through a second protein. Their return is coupled to nitrate entry, even when nitrate is already more concentrated inside. This second protein is a **cotransporter**. Respiration supplies ATP to maintain the first step.
3. **Account for water separately.** Changing dissolved-substance distributions can change water potential. Water can then enter passively through available routes when its total water potential is lower inside. The nitrate transporter is not an ATP-powered water pump.

The electricity qualification matters. An ion carries charge, so electrical attraction or repulsion contributes alongside concentration. We call their combined effect an **electrochemical gradient**. The school concentration-gradient description gives a useful starting account; the proton relay shows how a real root can achieve it without inventing a motor that burns exactly one ATP for every nitrate.

Then read the separate mangrove water account on the right: its numbered clues concern salt handling and water potential. Water follows its own gradient, without an ATP-driven water pump. Protein shapes and arrows are schematic.
:::live root_transport

Now return to the mangroves. Grey mangroves, *Avicennia marina*, occur along Maharashtra's coast. Their roots exclude much of the salt from entering the water-conducting tissues. “Much” matters: the exclusion is not perfect. Nor is it enough on its own. Water still has to enter from salty soil with a low water potential.

Mangroves maintain a still lower water potential in the absorbing tissues and along the uptake pathway. Dissolved substances contribute: ions can be kept in vacuoles, while other dissolved compounds help the cytoplasm function under these conditions. Evaporation from leaves also contributes to tension in the water-conducting system; we will follow that pull in Chapter 5. Grey mangroves can secrete some salt through glands on their leaves. Other mangroves use different combinations of salt-handling features.

The answer to Carter Road's puzzle is therefore a coordinated account. Restrict salt entry, manage the salt that enters, and maintain conditions in which water moves down its water-potential gradient into the plant. Energy helps establish and maintain the living machinery and dissolved-substance distributions. Water itself still enters by passive movement along its gradient. Its adaptations maintain the conditions for ordinary osmosis.

:::five Active transport
? What is it?
Energy-coupled membrane transport against a concentration gradient, or for ions against the combined concentration and electrical gradient.
? Why is it called this?
Active distinguishes transport coupled to the cell's energy supply from passive movement down a gradient. It does not imply conscious effort.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains how a root can keep taking up a scarce mineral ion when the cell already contains more of it per volume than the soil solution.
? Why does it work?
ATP breakdown can be coupled to a protein's transport cycle. A gradient built by one pump can also supply energy to a second transporter.
? How does it help?
It connects mineral uptake, ion balance and the continuous ATP supply needed by living cells.
?? How do we know?
Membrane experiments link ATP-consuming proteins and particular ions. Transport studies distinguish energy-coupled accumulation from passive equilibration.
?? What it isn't
Every passage through a protein, or a water pump that forces osmosis uphill. Aquaporins can carry water passively.
?? Where does it stop working?
A sustained energy shortage prevents gradients being maintained indefinitely. Stored gradients can persist for a while, so loss of ATP need not stop every crossing instantly.
:::

:::careful
> “Roots actively suck in water because the plant needs a drink.”

Name the transported substance and its route. Mineral-ion uptake can depend on energy-coupled transport; net water uptake follows water potential. A mangrove's salt barriers alone cannot explain intake unless its water-potential account also works.
:::

:::school
For 0610 Topic 3.3, connect movement from **lower to higher concentration**, **against a concentration gradient**, **carrier proteins**, and **energy from respiration**. Root hair uptake of mineral ions is the key example; the proton relay explains how that energy can be supplied indirectly.
:::

:::recall 1
Does a root cell stop requiring respiration because the leaves can capture sunlight?
???
No. Root cells require a continuing ATP supply for work such as maintaining transport gradients. The plant's ability to photosynthesise does not replace cellular respiration.
:::

:::check
A mangrove excludes much salt at its roots. A second plant excludes the same amount, but its absorbing tissues have a higher water potential than the soil water. Will equal salt exclusion guarantee equal water uptake?
???
No. Salt exclusion restricts one transfer; water uptake requires a favourable water-potential difference. If tissue water potential is higher than the surrounding soil water, that comparison favours net water loss rather than intake. Check the total water potential, not only the salt barrier.
:::

## The thirsty sailor's case

:::case Surrounded by water, losing water
A sailor has run out of fresh water. Seawater surrounds the boat. The sailor argues that a drink must help: it contains far more water than salt.

Clue one: dissolved substances can change the distribution of water between body fluids and cells. Clue two: absorbed excess salt must be removed, and the kidneys use water to excrete it. Clue three: humans do not have a mangrove's salt-handling system.

Why can drinking seawater make the sailor more dehydrated, despite the water swallowed?
???
The salt load matters along with the water intake. Human kidneys require water to remove that excess salt; dealing with seawater can cause a net loss of body water. Raised salt concentration in body fluids also favours water leaving cells. Osmosis explains those cell-level shifts, while salt excretion explains why the whole-body water account can worsen. Seawater is not safe drinking water. This is a reasoning case, never a drinking experiment.
:::
