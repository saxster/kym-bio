At the end of Chapter 13, we stood in a genetics clinic at KEM Hospital in Parel, Mumbai, with two questions. Why can bacterial populations become resistant to an antibiotic, and why does the sickle-cell allele (`Hbˢ`) remain common in some historically malarial regions? A child who inherits two copies (`HbˢHbˢ`) can develop severe sickle-cell anaemia, yet one copy can reduce the risk of severe malaria. The prescription lesson remains practical: **take antibiotics exactly as your clinician directs, and ask before changing the treatment**. The evolutionary puzzle is about which inherited variants leave descendants.

Together, those two clinical puzzles force us to ask the deepest question in biology. In Chapters 11, 12, and 13, we saw how DNA stores triplet codons (**Rosalind Franklin** and **Har Gobind Khorana**), how **Matthew Meselson**, **Franklin Stahl**, and **Walther Flemming** traced semi-conservative DNA replication and chromosome division, and how **Johann Gregor Mendel**'s alleles segregate in `3 : 1` and `1 : 2 : 1` ratios. If copying and repair make DNA replication very accurate, where did every different allele on Earth come from in the first place? And once new alleles exist, what processes influence which ones vanish, which ones take over a hospital ward in seventy-two hours, and which ones build drought-tolerant grasses, floating water lilies, and the entire four-billion-year tree of life?

## 14.1 The glitch engine: continuous vs discontinuous variation, mutations and mutagens

:::scene Mumbai School Biology Laboratory & University of Wisconsin, Madison, 1952

Imagine measuring the heights of two hundred 12-year-old students in Mumbai, then grouping the measurements into `5 cm` intervals. You might find many students near the middle and fewer near either end, though a real class need not make a perfect bell curve. Now sort their recorded `ABO` blood groups into `O`, `B`, `A`, and `AB`. These make separate categories: there is no blood group "A-and-a-quarter". These are an imagined classroom comparison, not measurements from a survey.

Look closely at those two classroom charts before we name a single technical term. All two hundred students belong to the same species, *Homo sapiens*, yet no two classmates (except identical twins) are genetic copies. In biology, any difference between individuals of the same species is called ==variation==. Why does one human characteristic spread out into an unbroken rainbow of measurements while another snaps into four rigid, non-overlapping boxes?

The height chart illustrates ==continuous variation==: a characteristic can take intermediate numerical values rather than falling into a few separate categories. Height, mass and hand span are examples; two hundred students cannot fill every possible value, but another student could measure between two existing measurements. Height reflects the effects of many genes (**polygenic inheritance**) and environmental conditions such as nutrition and childhood health. A frequency histogram uses touching intervals. Many height datasets are approximately bell-shaped, but a bell curve is not the definition of continuous variation; body mass, for example, may be unevenly distributed.

The blood-group chart illustrates ==discontinuous variation==: the recorded phenotypes fall into distinct categories with no intermediates. Your `ABO` group is usually explained by one locus on Chromosome `9` with three common alleles (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`). Mendel's round and wrinkled **seeds**, and purple and white pea flowers, provide other useful single-gene examples. Changing diet or exercise does not change your inherited ABO alleles. These examples do not establish a universal rule: some categorical traits involve many genes or environmental influences. Incubation temperature, for instance, affects whether some turtle species develop as males or females. Plot separate categories as separated bars.

| Feature | Continuous Variation | Discontinuous Variation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Phenotypic pattern** | Numerical range in which intermediate values are possible | Distinct, separate categories; no intermediates |
| **Genetic control** | Often many genes; height is a polygenic example | A single locus in our ABO and Mendelian pea examples |
| **Environmental influence** | Depends on the trait; nutrition affects growth | Diet does not change inherited ABO alleles; other categorical traits can respond to environment |
| **Graph representation** | Histogram with touching intervals; a bell curve is common, not required | Categorical bar chart with spaces between bars |
| **Classic examples** | Human height, body mass, foot length, leaf surface area | `ABO` blood groups (`A, B, AB, O`), pea seed shape/colour |

:::predict
Suppose an Olympic sprinter in Mumbai trains for ten years, building large leg muscles and a deep sun tan, and also eats a calcium-rich childhood diet that helped her grow `4 cm` taller than her poorly nourished cousins. Which of these features—her muscle bulk, her sun tan, her extra `4 cm` of diet-boosted height, or her `Group AB` blood alleles (`IᴬIᴮ`) and polygenic height alleles—can be passed on to her baby at fertilisation?
- All four features are passed on equally because the baby develops inside the mother's body
- Her gametes pass inherited height alleles and **one** ABO allele (`Iᴬ` or `Iᴮ` in each egg); these acquired body changes are not transmitted as new DNA alleles
- Only continuous traits like height and muscle mass are inherited, whereas discontinuous traits like `ABO` blood group reset every generation
???
The second choice. Environmental variations—muscle hypertrophy in somatic muscle fibres, melanin pigment induced by ultraviolet light in skin cells, or extra bone growth from childhood nutrition—do not alter the base sequence of DNA inside her haploid egg cells (gametes). These acquired features are not inherited as changed DNA alleles. Parental environments can affect offspring in other ways, including maternal effects and, in some circumstances, inherited epigenetic marks; that is different from inheriting a trained muscle or a suntan.
:::

Where, then, does genetic variation in a population come from? Four distinct processes contribute to the variation we observe, but they do very different jobs:

1. **Mutation (the ONLY source of brand-new alleles):** A ==mutation== is a random change in the genetic material of a cell. More specifically, a ==gene mutation== is a random change in the base sequence of DNA—such as the single base substitution (`CCT GAG GAG → CCT GTG GAG`) at codon `6` of the beta-globin (`HBB`) gene on Chromosome `11` that replaces hydrophilic glutamic acid (`Glu⁶`) with hydrophobic valine (`Val⁶`), changing a surface contact rather than requiring a wholly different fold (the distinction between protein structure and interaction connects to **G. N. Ramachandran**), creating a hydrophobic patch on the haemoglobin surface that helps molecules join into fibres at low oxygen, and producing the sickle-cell allele `Hbˢ` from `Hbᴬ`. A ==chromosome mutation== alters the structure or number of whole chromosomes, such as Trisomy `21` (Down syndrome, `47` chromosomes) or the polyploid chromosome sets (`2n → 4n`) studied in Chapter 12. **Mutation is the sole biological process that invents brand-new alleles.**
2. **Meiosis (reshuffling existing alleles into new gamete combinations):** As we traced in Chapter 12, **crossing over** between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during Prophase I swaps maternal and paternal alleles along a chromosome, while **independent assortment** at Metaphase I lines up `23` homologous pairs randomly (`2²³ = 8,388,608` possible chromosome combinations per gamete).
3. **Random mating and random fertilisation:** In sexual reproduction, any of millions of genetically unique sperm can fuse with any genetically unique ovum (`2²³ × 2²³ ≈ 70.4 trillion` possible diploid zygote combinations even before crossing over).
4. **Environmental factors:** Nutrition, sunlight, temperature, and water availability can modify phenotypes. Such changes need not create new DNA alleles and can affect continuous or categorical traits.

What causes DNA mutations, and how fast do they happen? DNA polymerases and repair systems correct many copying errors, but some changes remain. Rates vary among organisms, cell types and DNA sites; our widget chooses `10⁻⁹` per base pair per division as an illustrative baseline, not a universal measured rate. However, external physical or chemical agents called ==mutagens== damage DNA and sharply increase the **rate** of mutation:

- **Ionising radiation** (such as X-rays, gamma rays, and alpha or beta particles from radioactive decay) strips electrons from atoms and snaps sugar-phosphate backbones or chemically alters bases, with the effect depending on dose, organism and repair.
- **Ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation** in sunlight cross-links adjacent thymine bases (`T=T` dimers), distorting the double helix.
- **Chemical mutagens**—such as benzopyrene in tobacco tar, nitrous acid (`HNO₂`), mustard gas, and ethidium bromide—chemically modify bases or wedge between base pairs, causing mispairing during the next replication round.

Crucially, **random with respect to need** means mutations are not produced because an organism knows which change would help. Some DNA sites mutate more often than others, and mutagens have characteristic patterns of damage. Some antibiotics also increase mutation rates through stress responses. These effects do not guarantee a useful mutation; selection acts on whichever variants occur.

:::evidence Bloomington, 1943 & Madison, 1952: Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test and Lederberg velveteen replica plating | Experimental microbiological proof
For decades, many scientists believed that pouring an antibiotic onto bacteria *caused* the bacteria to mutate in order to survive the drug. Following **Salvador Luria** and Max Delbrück's `1943` mathematical fluctuation test, **Joshua Lederberg** and **Esther Lederberg** designed a direct visual test at the University of Wisconsin in `1952`:

1. **Master plate (no antibiotic):** They spread *Escherichia coli* bacteria across a plain nutrient agar master plate that contained **zero antibiotic** and incubated it until distinct colonies grew (including positions labelled `C3`, `C7`, and `C11` in our illustrative diagram).
2. **Velveteen stamp:** They pressed a wooden cylinder covered in sterile velveteen cloth gently onto the master plate; the microscopic fabric fibres acted like thousands of tiny needles, picking up cells from every colony at its exact `(x, y)` coordinate.
3. **Replica plates (+ lethal antibiotic):** They pressed that velveteen stamp onto fresh agar plates containing streptomycin. Almost all colonies died, except `3` resistant colonies (`C3`, `C7`, `C11`) that grew at the **exact same `(x, y)` coordinates** on every replicate plate.
4. **Decisive deduction:** Going back to coordinates `C3`, `C7`, and `C11` on the original **antibiotic-free master plate**, the Lederbergs picked those colonies into broth and repeatedly isolated and purified resistant bacteria without exposing the source culture to antibiotic—even though neither they nor their ancestors had ever touched a single molecule of antibiotic. The resistance mutation had occurred **randomly before** drug exposure; the antibiotic merely killed the sensitive majority and revealed the pre-existing mutants.

:::careful
- **Misconception: "Mutations are always harmful and turn organisms into monsters."**
  *Correction:* Most DNA base substitutions are **neutral** (occurring in non-coding DNA or producing a synonymous codon that codes for the same amino acid). Some are harmful (such as `HbˢHbˢ` in non-malarial regions), and a small fraction are **beneficial** in a specific environment (such as a bacterial enzyme that breaks down penicillin or `HbᴬHbˢ` in a malaria belt).
- **Misconception: "Meiosis and fertilisation create new alleles."**
  *Correction:* Crossing over, independent assortment, and random fertilisation create **new combinations of existing alleles** (new genotypes), but **only mutation** creates brand-new alleles by altering the DNA base sequence.
- **Misconception: "Good nutrition or climate can shift discontinuous traits just a little bit."**
  *Correction:* Diet can affect height and mass but does not change inherited ABO alleles. Other categorical traits can respond to the environment, so category shape alone does not establish its cause.
:::

## 14.2 The six-step filter: natural selection, peppered moths and antibiotic resistance

:::scene Birmingham & Dorset Woodlands, 1953–1955, and KEM Hospital Ward, Mumbai

First, stand beside **Bernard Kettlewell** in a soot-darkened woodland near Birmingham in `1953`. A pale and a dark peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) face different risks because birds can more easily spot the one contrasting with its resting background. Now imagine a laboratory culture containing susceptible and resistant bacteria. An antibiotic may kill susceptible cells more readily, changing the composition of the survivors. This laboratory comparison explains selection without pretending to forecast a child's infection or prescribe a treatment duration.

In Section 14.1 we saw how random DNA mutations continuously inject new alleles into populations, and how meiosis and fertilisation shuffle them. Left to itself, mutation is blind: it produces alleles that darken moth wings, thin plant cuticles, or alter bacterial enzymes without anticipating which changes will help. How does a blind glitch engine produce organisms that become better suited to particular environments?

The answer is the mechanism independently discovered by **Charles Robert Darwin** and **Wallace in Ternate** (Alfred Russel Wallace) and presented jointly in London in `1858`: ==natural selection==. Natural selection is not a conscious designer. The following six linked steps explain selection in our examples; the essential result is a heritable difference in contribution to the next generation:

1. **Variation within populations:** Individuals of the same species vary in their phenotypes and underlying genotypes (alleles), including new alleles created by random mutation.
2. **Production of many offspring (overproduction):** Every species—whether bacteria dividing every `20 minutes` as **Louis Pasteur** cultured in broth flasks, oak trees dropping thousands of acorns, or moths laying hundreds of eggs—produces far more offspring than the environment can support.
3. **Struggle for existence (competition and selection pressure):** Because food, water, light, territory, and mates are limited, and because predators, pathogens, and climate extremes threaten survival, individuals face an intense ==struggle for existence==. Any environmental factor that affects an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing is called a ==selection pressure==.
4. **Differential reproductive success:** Some inherited features help their carriers leave more offspring. Better survival is one route (==differential survival==), but greater mating success or fertility can also matter.
5. **Reproduction and inheritance:** Individuals that reproduce pass alleles to offspring. A variant that leads its carriers to contribute more offspring can increase in frequency.
6. **Increase in advantageous allele frequency over many generations:** Over many generations, the frequency of the advantageous allele increases in the population while disadvantageous alleles become rarer. This change in the heritable characteristics (and allele frequencies) of a population over generations is ==evolution==.

:::predict
A hypothetical culture starts with `990` susceptible bacteria and `10` resistant bacteria. During one exposure, suppose `90%` of susceptible cells die but every resistant cell survives. Does resistance become more common even before any cell divides?
- No: a population changes only when new mutations appear
- Yes: `99` susceptible and `10` resistant cells remain, so the resistant fraction rises from `1%` to about `9.2%`
- The antibiotic must have changed all `99` survivors into resistant bacteria
???
The second choice. The resistant **number** stayed at `10`, but its **fraction** increased because susceptible cells were removed. If resistance is inherited, reproduction by survivors can carry that difference forward. The numbers are model assumptions, not measurements from a patient.
:::

This is one route to ==antibiotic resistance==: mutations and horizontal gene transfer supply variation, then drug exposure can favour resistant variants. Some mutations arise before exposure; some arise later, and some antibiotics can raise mutation rates. None of this means bacteria produce the particular mutation they need. Bacteria are **resistant**, rather than "immune" in the antibody-based sense, though bacteria have their own defence systems against threats such as phages.

**MRSA** means *methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus*. Its resistance involves altered antibiotic targets; not every resistance mechanism is an enzyme that destroys the drug. To reduce the development and spread of resistance:

1. **Use antibiotics only when clinically needed.** They do not treat viral infections such as the common cold.
2. **Follow the clinician's dose and duration, and ask before changing treatment.** The shortest effective duration depends on the infection and drug. Longer exposure is not automatically better: unnecessary exposure can also select resistance.
3. **Reduce routine agricultural misuse**, including growth-promotion use in healthy livestock.
4. **Prevent transmission** through hand hygiene, appropriate isolation and sterilisation.

:::evidence Birmingham & Dorset, 1953–1955 & Cambridge, 2001–2007: Kettlewell's peppered moths and Majerus's 4,864-moth replication | Field predation proof
The peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) rests motionless on birch and oak trees during the day, relying on camouflage to hide from insectivorous birds (robins, hedge sparrows, and great tits):

- **Pre-1848 unpolluted woodland:** Tree trunks and branches were covered in pale, crusty lichens. Pale speckled `typica` moths (`cc`, `~98%`) blended invisibly against the lichens, whereas rare black `carbonaria` mutants (`C_`, first recorded in Manchester in `1848` at `<2%`) stood out starkly and were eaten by birds.
- **1895–1955 industrial soot peak:** Coal-burning factories pumped out sulphur dioxide (`SO₂`), which killed the pale lichens, and black soot, which coated tree bark. Now the selection pressure inverted: pale `typica` moths stood out like white beacons on black bark, while dark `carbonaria` moths were camouflaged. By `1895`, `90–98%` of Manchester peppered moths were dark `carbonaria`. Between `1953` and `1955`, **Bernard Kettlewell** released marked moths in polluted Birmingham woods and unpolluted Dorset woods: in Birmingham he recaptured **`27.5%` of dark `carbonaria` versus `13.1%` of pale `typica` in his 1953 Birmingham release** (nearly a `2 : 1` survival advantage for dark moths), whereas in unpolluted Dorset he recaptured **`15.8%` of pale `typica` versus `7.4%` of dark `carbonaria`** (approximately a `2 : 1` recapture advantage for pale moths).
- **Methodological critique and later validation:** Researchers questioned whether early release methods matched natural resting behaviour. In six release seasons (`2002–2007`), **Michael Majerus** used `4,864` moths at low densities near Cambridge. Each night moths entered net sleeves around branches; sleeves were removed before dawn, leaving moths at chosen resting positions. Subsequent observations supported differential bird predation. His wild observations included moths on branches **and trunks**; the lesson is improved experimental realism, not that moths never rest on trunks.
- **Post-1956 Clean Air reversal:** After the UK Clean Air Acts cut `SO₂` and soot emissions, pale lichens recolonised tree bark across Britain. Without a single moth "choosing" to turn pale, bird predation reversed the allele frequencies: dark `carbonaria` plunged from `>90%` back below `12%`, and pale `typica` rebounded to `88–95%`.

**The forceps-and-newspaper camouflage hunt (Mumbai Desk Investigation)**

*Educational adaptation disclosure:* This safe household and classroom simulation is a non-trialled educational adaptation designed to demonstrate how visual predation on a patterned background produces differential survival and shifts morph frequencies across generations. It uses only dry paper dots and blunt tweezers (`0` chemicals, `0` heat, `0` live animals).

You can measure differential survival and changes in **morph frequency** on your desk. This is an asexual paper model: colour is copied exactly, unlike dominance and mating in real moths.

1. Prepare `30` white dots and `30` newspaper-print dots, plus at least `100` spare dots of each kind. Scatter the starting `60` across a newspaper sheet while your partner looks away. These are contrasting paper morphs, not literal pale and dark moths.
2. Give your partner blunt tweezers. They pick the first dot they notice, looking away between picks, until they have removed **exactly `30` dots**. Record the elapsed time and both colours caught.
3. Count each surviving colour. Add one matching daughter dot per survivor, then scatter all `60` again. For example, if `8` white and `22` printed dots survive, reproduction gives `16` white and `44` printed dots: `73.3%` printed. These are worked-example numbers, not a promised result.
4. Repeat for two more generations and calculate each percentage from your counts. Run at least two fresh trials: chance affects which dots are caught.
5. Repeat on plain white paper. If both colours remain, the favoured colour may reverse. If one has disappeared, changing the background cannot recreate it: restart with both colours to compare the environments.

:::note
**Why bacteria evolve resistance in days while elephants take millennia.** Two biological factors make bacterial evolution ultra-fast: (1) **Generation time:** Under warm, nutrient-rich conditions, *E. coli* or *S. aureus* divides every `20–30 minutes`, completing `72` generations in a single `24-hour` day—as many generations as human populations have experienced since the Roman Empire. (2) **Plasmids and horizontal transfer:** As we learned in Chapter 2, bacteria carry small circular **plasmids** alongside their main chromosomal DNA. Resistance genes often sit on plasmids that can be copied and passed directly between neighbouring bacteria (even across species), spreading a resistance allele through a hospital ward at high speed.
:::

- **Misconception: "Organisms mutate or adapt because they 'need to', 'try to', or 'want to' survive."**
  *Correction:* Need does not specify the helpful DNA change. Variants can arise before or during an environmental change; selection favours those that lead to more descendants under the new conditions.
- **Misconception: "A moth evolves darker wings during its own lifetime."**
  *Correction:* Cells can acquire mutations during an individual's life. In this example, **populations** evolve as inherited variants change in frequency across generations; an individual moth does not choose a new inherited wing colour.
- **Misconception: "Humans become resistant to antibiotics if they take them too often."**
  *Correction:* It is the **populations of bacteria** living inside or on humans that become resistant to antibiotics by natural selection, not the human patient's own body cells.

1. **Six-mark exam question — Rat resistance to warfarin:** In the `1950s`, the poison warfarin was introduced to control brown rat (*Rattus norvegicus*) populations on farms. Initially, warfarin killed over `98%` of rats by blocking vitamin K recycling in blood clotting. Within ten years, farm rat populations were thriving and unaffected by standard doses of warfarin. Using the six steps of natural selection, explain how warfarin resistance evolved in the rat population.
2. **Viral coughs and antibiotic stewardship:** A patient visits a Mumbai clinic with a `2-day` runny nose and cough caused by a rhinovirus (common cold) and asks the doctor for amoxicillin *"just in case"*. Give **two** biological reasons why the doctor must refuse to prescribe an antibiotic.
???

1. (1) **Genetic variation:** Due to pre-existing **random gene mutation**, a tiny fraction of rats in the original population already carried an allele conferring resistance to warfarin (an altered vitamin K epoxide reductase enzyme). (2) **Overproduction:** Rats produce large litters (many more offspring than farm food and shelter can support). (3) **Selection pressure / struggle for existence:** When farmers applied warfarin, the poison acted as an intense **environmental selection pressure**. (4) **Differential survival:** Sensitive wild-type rats died of internal bleeding, whereas rats carrying the warfarin-resistance allele survived (and experienced less competition for food). (5) **Reproduction and inheritance:** The surviving resistant rats bred and **passed the resistance allele** to their offspring. (6) **Allele frequency increase:** Repeated over many generations of warfarin application, the frequency of the resistance allele increased until almost the entire farm rat population was warfarin-resistant.
2. (1) **Zero effect on viruses:** Rhinoviruses are acellular particles (protein capsid + nucleic acid) with no peptidoglycan cell wall, no cell membrane, and no `70S` ribosomes; amoxicillin targets bacterial cell-wall synthesis and therefore cannot kill or inhibit viruses. (2) **Selection for antibiotic resistance:** Taking amoxicillin unnecessarily exposes the patient's normal commensal bacteria (in the gut and respiratory tract) to an antibiotic **selection pressure**, killing sensitive bacteria and selecting for pre-existing **antibiotic-resistant mutants**, which can multiply and transfer resistance plasmids to pathogenic bacteria.

## 14.3 Built for the extremes: adaptive features of xerophytes, hydrophytes and animals

:::scene Thar Desert Dunes, Rajasthan & Mumbai Botanical Pond

Hold two contrasting leaves in a Mumbai teaching garden. A marram grass (*Ammophila arenaria*) blade from dry coastal dunes rolls inward, enclosing grooves and hairs that shelter its stomata. A floating water-lily (*Nymphaea*) leaf spreads a broad pad across a pond; its upper surface meets air, and air chambers run through its tissues. A third plant, fully submerged *Elodea*, has thin leaves with no stomata. Floating and submerged leaves share an aquatic habitat but do not solve gas exchange in the same way.

Why do leaves in these environments have different structures? In Chapters 3 and 5, we discovered the central dilemma of every photosynthesising plant: to make glucose (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`), an air-exposed leaf generally opens stomatal pores so carbon dioxide (`CO₂`) can diffuse in—yet the moment stomata open, water vapour (`H₂O`) diffuses out down a steep water potential gradient (`ΔΨ`) from the moist spongy mesophyll air spaces into the drier outside air (transpiration).

Over millions of generations, natural selection has shaped inherited features that solve this dilemma in radically different habitats. In Cambridge biology, an ==adaptive feature== is defined precisely as **an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment**. On the Extended curriculum, we also define an organism's ==fitness== as **the probability of an organism surviving and reproducing in the environment in which it is found**. Notice how the two definitions lock together: an inherited adaptive feature functions to increase an organism's fitness in its specific habitat.

Let us examine how two classic groups of plants—**xerophytes** and **hydrophytes**—solve opposite water-balance problems:

### 1. Xerophytes: plants adapted to dry, arid environments
A ==xerophyte== (from the Greek *xeros*, dry, and *phyton*, plant) is a plant adapted to live in environments with very scarce liquid water and high evaporation rates—such as hot deserts (cacti like *Opuntia* in Rajasthan), wind-swept coastal sand dunes (marram grass, *Ammophila arenaria*), or frozen regions where soil water is locked as ice. If a standard garden bean plant (*Phaseolus vulgaris*, a mesophyte) is planted on a dry sand dune, it can lose water faster than its roots replace it, causing its cells to lose turgor and the shoot to wilt. Xerophytes survive because they inherit four linked structural and physiological adaptations:

- **Waxy cuticle:** A water-resistant surface barrier reduces water loss through the epidermis. Its thickness and composition vary among species; there is no single thickness or fixed percentage saving for all xerophytes.
- **Sunken stomata in rolled grooves lined with hairs (trichomes):** In marram grass, hinge cells lose turgor in dry wind, curling the leaf blade into a tight C-shaped tube around longitudinal grooves. Stomata sit at the bottom of **sunken pits** inside these grooves, guarded by dense interlocking **epidermal hairs (trichomes)**. Together, the rolled leaf, sunken pits, and hairs trap a pocket of still, moist water vapour right outside the stomatal pore. Retaining this humid boundary layer **reduces the water potential gradient (`ΔΨ`)** (and concentration gradient) between the internal mesophyll air spaces and the air immediately outside the stoma, drastically slowing the rate of water vapour diffusion (for example, a chosen drop from `9.8` to `1.4 mg cm⁻² h⁻¹` would mean `85.7%` less water loss, not a fixed species value).
- **Small, rolled, or spine-like leaves + succulent stems:** In cacti (*Opuntia*), leaves are reduced to sharp **spines** with almost zero surface area for transpiration (while also deterring thirsty desert herbivores). Photosynthesis and water storage (`mucilage-rich parenchyma`) are shifted into the thick, green, fleshy **succulent stem**.
- **Root systems and night-time gas exchange:** Different dry-habitat plants have different root strategies: deep roots can reach persistent water, while widespread shallow roots exploit brief rain. Many cacti use **CAM**: they take up `CO₂` mainly at night and store it for daytime photosynthesis, reducing water loss. Marram grass does not share every cactus feature.

### 2. Hydrophytes: plants adapted to wet, aquatic environments
A ==hydrophyte== (from *hydro*, water) is a plant adapted to live partially or wholly submerged in freshwater ponds, lakes, and slow rivers—such as floating water lilies (*Nymphaea*), sacred lotus (*Nelumbo nucifera*), or submerged pondweed (*Elodea* / *Hydrilla*). A hydrophyte usually has ready access to water; instead, the plant struggles to keep its photosynthetic leaves up in the sunlight and to supply oxygen (`O₂`) to roots buried in waterlogged, anaerobic mud (recall from Chapter 6 that dissolved `O₂` diffuses `~10,000×` slower in water than in air). Hydrophytes inherit four contrasting adaptive features:

- **Large air spaces (`aerenchyma`) in leaves, stems, and petioles :** Specialised spongy tissue called ==aerenchyma== forms continuous honeycomb air chambers (`lacunae`) that provide **buoyancy**—floating the broad leaf blades right at the sunlit water surface—and create a low-resistance internal **gas diffusion pathway** that channels `O₂` produced during photosynthesis down to the submerged respiring roots.
- **Stomata on the upper epidermis only:** In a floating water lily leaf, the lower epidermis rests flat against liquid water where air pores would flood and be useless (`0` stomata). Instead, stomata are concentrated exclusively on the **upper epidermis** exposed to the atmosphere, allowing rapid `CO₂` uptake and `O₂` release. (Fully submerged leaves like *Elodea* have **no stomata at all**, exchanging dissolved gases directly across thin epidermal walls.)
- **Different surface barriers in floating and submerged leaves:** A floating water-lily's air-facing surface can carry water-repellent wax, helping keep its stomata exposed to air. Fully submerged leaves generally have thin surface barriers that allow dissolved gases to pass through. "Water plant" does not mean "no wax".
- **Support and transport:** Water's buoyancy reduces the mechanical support some aquatic shoots need. Fully submerged shoots can absorb water and some dissolved substances across their surfaces and often have reduced xylem. Rooted floating-leaved plants still use roots for anchorage and nutrient uptake; roots are not universally tiny or merely decorative.

| Feature | Dry-habitat example | Floating water-lily leaf | Fully submerged *Elodea* leaf |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Surface barrier | Waxy cuticle reduces evaporation | Upper surface can carry water-repellent wax | Thin barrier permits dissolved-gas exchange |
| Stomata | Sheltered inside rolled marram leaf; on cactus stems | Upper epidermis faces air | Absent from submerged leaves |
| Air spaces | Species-dependent | Aerenchyma supports buoyancy and internal gas transport | Internal gas spaces can supply submerged tissues |
| Shape/support | Rolled leaf or succulent photosynthetic stem | Broad floating pad, with roots anchored in sediment | Thin simple leaves and flexible shoots |

## 14.4 Guided evolution and deep time: selective breeding, fossils and DNA clocks

:::scene Imperial Sugarcane Station, Coimbatore, 1930s & Institute of Palaeobotany, Lucknow, 1948

Visit two Indian laboratories working at different scales of evolutionary time. At Coimbatore in the `1930s`, cytogeneticist **E. K. Janaki Ammal** studied sugarcane chromosomes and hybrids between sugarcane and related grasses. Her studies of inheritance and chromosome behaviour helped strengthen the scientific basis for crop breeding. Now travel to Lucknow in `1948`, where **Birbal Sahni** described *Pentoxyleae*, an unusual extinct group of seed plants from the Rajmahal Hills. He called these fossils Jurassic; later work assigned the relevant Rajmahal material an **Early Cretaceous** age. Their anatomy helps researchers compare ancient seed plants, but does not by itself prove that they were direct ancestors of modern conifers.

In Section 14.2 we saw how the natural environment—predators, drought, cold, and disease—filters heritable variation across generations. Ten thousand years ago, human farmers realised they could replace the environment as the filter. Instead of waiting for nature to select which plants or animals bred, humans chose the parents themselves. This human-directed process is called ==selective breeding==, or ==artificial selection==.

Whether a farmer in Gujarat is breeding **Gir** or **Sahiwal** dairy cattle for higher milk yield, **E. K. Janaki Ammal** is studying sugarcane chromosomes and hybrids, or an agronomist is breeding dwarf, rust-resistant wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), a simple selective-breeding programme follows this **five-step loop**:

1. **Choose the desirable feature:** Humans decide which heritable characteristic they want (such as high milk yield in cattle, high sucrose or disease resistance in crops, docile temperament in dogs, or large flower petals).
2. **Select parents showing the feature:** From a genetically varied population, breeders select male and female individuals that display the highest level of that desired phenotype.
3. **Breed the selected parents together:** The chosen parents are crossed (by controlled pollination in plants or selective mating/artificial insemination in livestock) while preventing unselected individuals from breeding.
4. **Select the best offspring:** When the offspring mature, breeders measure their phenotypes and **select only the offspring that show the desired characteristic most strongly** to become the parents of the next generation.
5. **Repeat over many generations:** Steps `2–4` are repeated across **many generations** (`10`, `20`, `40`, or more) until the frequency of the desired alleles rises and the trait is strongly expressed across the whole bred variety.

How does selective breeding compare with natural selection? Both processes rely on heritable genetic variation, differential reproduction, and allele frequency changes over generations, yet they differ sharply in six ways:

| Dimension | Natural Selection | Selective Breeding (Artificial Selection) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Selective agent** | The **environment** (predators, pathogens, climate, food competition) | **Humans** (farmers, breeders, horticulturalists, scientists) |
| **2. Trait favoured** | Adaptive features that help the organism survive and reproduce in its wild habitat | Characteristics useful, profitable, or attractive to **humans** |
| **3. Effect on wild fitness** | Favours higher reproductive contribution under current conditions; no guarantee of perfection | May **reduce** wild survival fitness (e.g. seedless fruits, heavy udders, flat-faced Pugs) |
| **4. Rate of change** | Depends on selection strength, variation and generation time | Rapid across tens of human-controlled generations |
| **5. Genetic diversity** | Can decrease through selective sweeps or be maintained by balancing selection | **Reduced** (`gene pool narrows`), increasing risk of **inbreeding depression** |
| **6. Classic examples** | Peppered moths (*Biston betularia*), antibiotic resistance, `Hbˢ` balancing selection | Coimbatore sugarcane, Gir dairy cattle, rust-resistant wheat, *Brassica* vegetables |

### Reading the history that selection leaves behind

How do we investigate change over millions of years? Fossils preserve traces of ancient organisms; living sequences retain inherited similarities. These provide complementary evidence, but neither produces an exact tree without interpretation.

1. **Fossils and palaeobotany:** A ==fossil== is preserved evidence of an ancient organism, including remains and traces. In an undisturbed sedimentary sequence, younger layers normally lie above older ones. Cambrian seas already held diverse, complex animals; Permian Gondwana rocks preserve *Glossopteris*. Sahni's Rajmahal *Pentoxyleae* shows an unusual combination of seed-plant features. Identifying relationships requires comparing many features, rather than treating every mixed anatomy as a direct ancestor.
2. **Sequence evidence:** A shark and dolphin can look similar because similar conditions favour streamlined bodies (**convergent evolution**). Comparing the same inherited DNA regions or proteins supplies additional evidence. Closely related species generally have more similar homologous sequences, but rates vary among regions and lineages. Beta-globin is useful among relevant vertebrates; it is not a universal protein present in all life. Sequence evidence also supports the bacterial origins of mitochondria and chloroplasts, a history connected to **Lynn Margulis**: a powerful explanation built from many converging observations.
3. **Estimating dates:** A ==molecular clock== uses accumulated sequence changes to help estimate divergence times. It must be calibrated with independent age information and allow for rate differences and uncertainty. A short sequence is evidence, not proof of an exact date. Fossils often give minimum ages: the oldest fossil found need not be the first member of its lineage.

## Why the sickle-cell allele stays common in malaria belts

D. Sanghvi): opposing selection pressures against HbᴬHbᴬ (malaria mortality) and HbˢHbˢ (sickle-cell anaemia) illustrate heterozygote advantage using assumed relative fitness values.

:::case Why the sickle-cell allele stays common in malaria belts
We are now ready to solve the chapter's central mystery—and tie together the entire four-chapter arc of Volume IV (*Chapters 11, 12, 13, and 14*).

Consider two **hypothetical populations**, inspired by the malaria and sickle-cell studies of **Anthony Allison** in East Africa and **L. D. Sanghvi** and colleagues in India. Population H has no malaria exposure; Population M has persistent *P. falciparum* exposure. To isolate selection, assume random mating, a large population, no migration or new mutation, and constant relative reproductive contributions (`w`). The numbers below are **teaching assumptions**, not measured fitness values for named districts or modern patients. In M, let `wAA = 0.85`, `wAS = 1.00` and `wSS = 0.20`.

| Genotype (Chr `11`) | Anaemia & *P. falciparum* Malaria Effects | Relative Fitness (`w`) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`HbᴬHbᴬ`** | predominantly HbA; usually biconcave cells; no sickle-cell anaemia; lacks the HbAS protection against severe malaria | **`w ≈ 0.85`** (selected against by malaria) |
| **`HbᴬHbˢ` (Carrier)** | `~60% HbA + ~40% HbS`; usually no sickle-cell anaemia; reduced risk of severe malaria, not complete protection | **`w = 1.00` (HIGHEST fitness)** |
| **`HbˢHbˢ`** | predominantly HbS (with variable HbF and HbA₂); HbS polymerises at low `O₂` into rigid crescents; **severe sickle-cell anaemia** | **`w ≈ 0.20`** (selected against by anaemia) |

In one of Allison's historical samples, parasites were detected in `113/247` children without sickle-cell trait (`45.7%`) and `12/43` with the trait (`27.9%`). **Detected parasites, severe disease and reproductive contribution are different measurements.** Those parasite percentages do not determine the fitness assumptions above.

Use all four sections of Chapter 14 (together with Chapters 11–13) to answer three synthesis questions:

1. **Molecular origin and non-directed mutation (`14.1` & Ch 11):** Explain how the `Hbˢ` allele originally arose on Chromosome `11`, and evaluate the claim that *"living in a malarial forest caused people's beta-globin genes to mutate into `Hbˢ` for protection."*
2. **Punnett inheritance and opposing selection pressures (`14.2–14.3` & Ch 13):** Construct the `2 × 2` Punnett square outcome when two heterozygous carriers (`HbᴬHbˢ × HbᴬHbˢ`) have children. Then explain why selection reduces `Hbˢ` in malaria-free Population H, whereas heterozygote advantage can maintain it in Population M. The model equilibrium is `q ≈ 15.8%`, giving approximately `26.6%` carriers among newborns under random mating.
3. **Heterozygote advantage and eradication prediction (`14.3–14.4`):** Suppose a public-health campaign removes malaria exposure immediately from Population M, after which you follow the next `20` generations while everything else stays constant. Predict and explain what will happen to the frequency of the `Hbˢ` allele in Population M over those `20` generations.
???

1. **Molecular origin (`14.1` & Ch 11):** `Hbˢ` differs from `Hbᴬ` by a base substitution in the `HBB` gene on Chromosome `11`: coding-strand `GAG → GTG`, corresponding to mRNA `GAG → GUG` and the traditional beta-chain substitution `Glu⁶ → Val⁶`. The altered surface favours haemoglobin polymerisation at low oxygen. Malaria does not specify that helpful mutation because it is needed; selection acts on inherited variants that occur. Mutation rates depend on the site and biological process, so the generic copying-error rate is not a measured historical origin rate for HbS.
2. **Punnett inheritance & balancing selection (`14.2–14.3` & Ch 13):**
    - **Cross (`HbᴬHbˢ × HbᴬHbˢ`):** Gametes `(Hbᴬ)` and `(Hbˢ)` combine in a `2 × 2` grid to yield **`25% HbᴬHbᴬ` : `50% HbᴬHbˢ` : `25% HbˢHbˢ`** (`1 : 2 : 1` genotype ratio).
    - **In malaria-free Population H:** There is no malaria mortality, so `HbᴬHbᴬ` and `HbᴬHbˢ` individuals have equal high fitness (`w = 1.00`), while the model gives HbSS a lower reproductive contribution (`w = 0.20`) because of sickle-cell disease. This disadvantage reduces transmission of HbS, reducing the HbS frequency, increasingly slowly as more copies remain in unaffected heterozygotes.
    - **In malarial Population M:** Two opposing selection pressures operate simultaneously. The model assigns HbAA a lower reproductive contribution because of malaria (`w = 0.85`), and HbSS a still lower contribution because of sickle-cell disease (`w = 0.20`). These are assumptions about the combined effect on descendants, not death rates for children today. **Heterozygous carriers (`HbᴬHbˢ`) have the highest fitness (`w = 1.00`, heterozygote advantage)** because `~60% HbA` prevents severe anaemia while several mechanisms, including altered parasite growth and clearance of infected cells, contribute to protection against severe malaria. Because `HbᴬHbˢ` survivors reproduce most successfully and pass `Hbˢ` to `50%` of their gametes, the allele is maintained at a stable equilibrium (**balancing selection**, `q* = 0.15/(0.15 + 0.80) ≈ 15.8%` under these assumptions).
3. **Prediction after malaria removal:** Set `wAA = wAS = 1.00` and retain `wSS = 0.20`. Selection continues against HbSS, but HbS copies in heterozygotes are sheltered from that recessive disadvantage. In this model the next generation's frequency is `q′ = (pq + 0.20q²)/(p² + 2pq + 0.20q²)`, where `p = 1 − q`. Starting at `q = 0.158`, twenty generations give about **`q = 0.0454`**, or **`8.66%` carriers** among newborns under random mating. The allele declines but does not vanish quickly; this is a model calculation, not a forecast for a district.
