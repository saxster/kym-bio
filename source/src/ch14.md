The first was a bright red warning stamped across an antibiotic prescription card: *COMPLETE THE FULL 7-DAY COURSE — DO NOT STOP ON DAY 3 WHEN FEVER FADES*. The second was an epidemiological map compiled from the western and central Indian population surveys of **L. D. Sanghvi**, showing that the recessive sickle-cell allele (`Hbˢ`) on Chromosome `11`—an allele that causes severe, life-threatening sickle-cell anaemia in `HbˢHbˢ` homozygotes—persists at carrier frequencies of `10%` to `24%` in communities living across historically malarial forest belts.

Together, those two clinical puzzles force us to ask the deepest question in biology. In Chapters 11, 12, and 13, we saw how DNA stores triplet codons (**Rosalind Franklin** and **Har Gobind Khorana**), how **Matthew Meselson**, **Franklin Stahl**, and **Walther Flemming** traced semi-conservative DNA replication and chromosome division, and how **Johann Gregor Mendel**'s alleles segregate in `3 : 1` and `1 : 2 : 1` ratios. If DNA replication is proofread to one error in a billion base pairs (`~10⁻⁹`), where did every different allele on Earth come from in the first place? And once new alleles exist, what invisible filter decides which ones vanish, which ones take over a hospital ward in seventy-two hours, and which ones build drought-proof desert grasses, floating water lilies, and the entire four-billion-year tree of life?

## 14.1 The glitch engine: continuous vs discontinuous variation, mutations and mutagens

:::scene Mumbai School Biology Laboratory & University of Wisconsin, Madison, 1952

Picture two hundred twelfth-birthday students from across Mumbai lining up along the back wall of their school biology laboratory for two measurements. First, each student steps barefoot under a stadiometer bar and writes their height in centimetres on a sticky note. When the notes are stacked into `5 cm` columns from `140 cm` to `185 cm`, they form a smooth, bell-shaped skyline: a few short students at the left edge, a few tall students at the right edge, and a crowded middle cluster around `162–166 cm`, with every fraction of a centimetre filled in between. Next, each student reads their `ABO` blood group from their school health card and places a second note onto a chart marked `Group O`, `Group B`, `Group A`, and `Group AB`. This second skyline looks completely different: four tall, separated towers (`38%` O, `32%` B, `22%` A, and `8%` AB) with empty floor between them. No student has "Group A-and-a-quarter" blood.

Look closely at those two classroom charts before we name a single technical term. All two hundred students belong to the same species, *Homo sapiens*, yet no two classmates (except identical twins) are genetic copies. In biology, any difference between individuals of the same species is called ==variation==. Why does one human characteristic spread out into an unbroken rainbow of measurements while another snaps into four rigid, non-overlapping boxes?

The height chart illustrates ==continuous variation==. In continuous variation, individuals within a population show a complete range of phenotypes between two extremes, with every intermediate value possible. If you measure body height, body mass, hand span, resting heart rate, or the length of mango leaves on a single tree, you can always find a measurement halfway between any two others. When plotted on a graph, continuous variation forms a frequency histogram of touching bars that smooths into a normal distribution (bell-shaped curve). Why does it behave this way? Because continuous traits are **polygenic**—controlled by the additive small effects of many different genes acting together—and they are strongly modified by **environmental factors** such as childhood protein and calcium nutrition, sunlight, water supply, and physical training. Good nutrition shifts the mean of the height bell curve upward by `2.8` to `4.2 cm`, while smoothing the steps between genotypes into a continuous spectrum.

The blood-group chart illustrates ==discontinuous variation==. In discontinuous variation, individuals fall into a few distinct, clear-cut phenotypic categories with no intermediates between them. As we saw in Chapter 13, your `ABO` blood group is controlled by a single gene locus on Chromosome `9` with three common alleles (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`). **Johann Gregor Mendel**'s Brno pea traits—round (`RR`, `Rr`) versus wrinkled (`rr`) seed coats, or purple versus white flowers—and human red–green colour vision follow the same rule. Discontinuous variation is controlled by **one gene or a very small number of genes alone**, and the environment has **zero effect** (`0%` shift) on which category you belong to: changing your diet, altitude, or exercise routine will never turn `Group O` blood (`IᴼIᴼ`) into `Group A` (`IᴬIᴼ`). Therefore, discontinuous variation is always plotted as a bar chart with clear gaps between separated category bars.

| Feature | Continuous Variation | Discontinuous Variation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Phenotypic pattern** | Unbroken range between two extremes; all intermediates present | Distinct, separate categories; no intermediates |
| **Genetic control** | Polygenic (many genes adding small effects) | Single gene locus (or `1–2` genes with major effect) |
| **Environmental influence** | Strong (nutrition, light, moisture shift the mean) | None (`0%` shift; environment cannot alter category) |
| **Graph representation** | Histogram (touching bars) with smooth bell curve | Categorical bar chart with spaces between bars |
| **Classic examples** | Human height, body mass, foot length, leaf surface area | `ABO` blood groups (`A, B, AB, O`), pea seed shape/colour |

???
The second choice. Environmental variations—muscle hypertrophy in somatic muscle fibres, melanin pigment induced by ultraviolet light in skin cells, or extra bone growth from childhood nutrition—do not alter the base sequence of DNA inside her haploid egg cells (gametes). **Environmental variation is never inherited.** Evolutionary change across generations can act only on **genetic (heritable) variation**.
:::

Where, then, does genetic variation in a population come from? Four distinct processes contribute to the variation we observe, but they do very different jobs:

1. **Mutation (the ONLY source of brand-new alleles):** A ==mutation== is a random change in the genetic material of a cell. More specifically, a ==gene mutation== is a random change in the base sequence of DNA—such as the single base substitution (`CCT GAG GAG → CCT GTG GAG`) at codon `6` of the beta-globin (`HBB`) gene on Chromosome `11` that replaces hydrophilic glutamic acid (`Glu⁶`) with hydrophobic valine (`Val⁶`), changing the three-dimensional polypeptide fold mapped by **G. N. Ramachandran** and creating the sickle-cell allele `Hbˢ` from `Hbᴬ`. A ==chromosome mutation== alters the structure or number of whole chromosomes, such as Trisomy `21` (Down syndrome, `47` chromosomes) or the polyploid chromosome sets (`2n → 4n`) studied in Chapter 12. **Mutation is the sole biological process that invents brand-new alleles.**
2. **Meiosis (reshuffling existing alleles into new gamete combinations):** As we traced in Chapter 12, **crossing over** between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during Prophase I swaps maternal and paternal alleles along a chromosome, while **independent assortment** at Metaphase I lines up `23` homologous pairs randomly (`2²³ = 8,388,608` possible chromosome combinations per gamete).
3. **Random mating and random fertilisation:** In sexual reproduction, any of millions of genetically unique sperm can fuse with any genetically unique ovum (`2²³ × 2²³ ≈ 70.4 trillion` possible diploid zygote combinations even before crossing over).
4. **Environmental factors:** Nutrition, sunlight, temperature, and water availability modify the phenotypic expression of continuous traits, though these changes are non-heritable.

What causes DNA mutations, and how fast do they happen? During S-phase DNA replication, DNA polymerase proofreads new strands so accurately that spontaneous copying glitches occur at a baseline rate of only about `10⁻⁹` per base pair per cell division (`~1` mutation per `10⁸` bacterial divisions at a typical gene locus). However, external physical or chemical agents called ==mutagens== damage DNA and sharply increase the **rate** of mutation:

- **Ionising radiation** (such as X-rays, gamma rays, and alpha or beta particles from radioactive decay) strips electrons from atoms and snaps sugar-phosphate backbones or chemically alters bases, raising mutation rates up to `50×` or more above baseline.
- **Ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation** in sunlight cross-links adjacent thymine bases (`T=T` dimers), distorting the double helix (`~8×` rate increase).
- **Chemical mutagens**—such as benzopyrene in tobacco tar, nitrous acid (`HNO₂`), mustard gas, and ethidium bromide—chemically modify bases or wedge between base pairs (`~20×` rate increase), causing mispairing during the next replication round.

Crucially, mutagens increase the *frequency* of random glitches across the genome; they **never** direct *which* gene mutates or tailor a mutation to help the organism survive.

:::evidence Bloomington, 1943 & Madison, 1952: Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test and Lederberg velveteen replica plating | Experimental microbiological proof
For decades, many scientists believed that pouring an antibiotic onto bacteria *caused* the bacteria to mutate in order to survive the drug. Following **Salvador Luria** and Max Delbrück's `1943` mathematical fluctuation test, **Joshua Lederberg** and **Esther Lederberg** designed a direct visual test at the University of Wisconsin in `1952`:

1. **Master plate (no antibiotic):** They spread *Escherichia coli* bacteria across a plain nutrient agar master plate that contained **zero antibiotic** and incubated it until distinct colonies grew (including positions `C3`, `C7`, and `C11`).
2. **Velveteen stamp:** They pressed a wooden cylinder covered in sterile velveteen cloth gently onto the master plate; the microscopic fabric fibres acted like thousands of tiny needles, picking up cells from every colony at its exact `(x, y)` coordinate.
3. **Replica plates (+ lethal antibiotic):** They pressed that velveteen stamp onto fresh agar plates containing lethal penicillin or streptomycin. Almost all colonies died, except `3` resistant colonies (`C3`, `C7`, `C11`) that grew at the **exact same `(x, y)` coordinates** on every replicate plate.
4. **Decisive deduction:** Going back to coordinates `C3`, `C7`, and `C11` on the original **antibiotic-free master plate**, the Lederbergs picked those colonies into broth and proved they were already `100%` antibiotic-resistant—even though neither they nor their ancestors had ever touched a single molecule of antibiotic. The resistance mutation had occurred **randomly before** drug exposure; the antibiotic merely killed the sensitive majority and revealed the pre-existing mutants.

:::five
? What is the biological definition of variation, and how does continuous variation differ from discontinuous variation in its phenotypic pattern?
**Variation** is the differences between individuals of the same species. **Continuous variation** produces a complete range of phenotypes between two extremes with intermediates (e.g. height, body mass), whereas **discontinuous variation** divides individuals into a few distinct, separate categories with no intermediates (e.g. `ABO` blood groups, pea seed shape).
? Compare the genetic and environmental causes of continuous variation versus discontinuous variation, and state which graph type represents each.
**Continuous variation** is controlled by **many genes (polygenic)** acting together plus **environmental factors** (such as nutrition, sunlight, and water), and is plotted as a **histogram of touching bars with a smooth normal (bell-shaped) curve**. **Discontinuous variation** is controlled by **one gene or a small number of genes alone** with **zero environmental effect**, and is plotted as a **bar chart with gaps between separate category bars**.
? Define a gene mutation and explain why mutation holds a unique role compared with meiosis, random mating, and random fertilisation.
A **gene mutation** is a random change in the base sequence of DNA (such as `GAG → GTG` in the `HBB` gene). Mutation is the **only biological process that creates brand-new alleles**; meiosis (crossing over and independent assortment), random mating, and random fertilisation only reshuffle existing alleles into new combinations.
? Name two classes of mutagens with concrete examples, and state their exact effect on mutation.
(1) **Ionising radiation** (such as X-rays, gamma rays, and radioactive alpha/beta particles) and **ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation**; (2) **Chemical mutagens** (such as benzopyrene in tobacco tar, nitrous acid, and mustard gas). Mutagens **increase the rate (frequency) of random mutations**, but they never direct which gene mutates.
? Why is environmental variation (such as a sprinter's muscle bulk or childhood diet-boosted height) never passed on to offspring?
Because environmental factors modify somatic body tissues without changing the **DNA base sequence inside haploid gametes (sperm and egg cells)**; only alleles carried in gametes are transmitted at fertilisation.
?? How did Joshua and Esther Lederberg's 1952 replica-plating experiment prove that bacterial resistance mutations are not caused by antibiotics?
By stamping bacterial colonies with sterile velveteen cloth from an **antibiotic-free master plate** onto antibiotic agar plates, they found resistant colonies (`C3, C7, C11`) at identical `(x, y)` coordinates across replicates and isolated pure resistant cultures directly from the matching spots on the antibiotic-free master plate—proving the mutation arose randomly **before** the bacteria ever encountered the antibiotic.
?? Why do many single-base substitutions in DNA cause no change at all in the organism's phenotype (silent or neutral mutations)?
Because the genetic code is **degenerate** (multiple mRNA codons code for the same amino acid, such as `GAA` and `GAG` both coding for glutamic acid), and because large regions of eukaryotic DNA are non-coding introns or intergenic sequences; if the amino acid sequence and 3D protein fold do not change, the phenotype remains unchanged.
?? Why does an asexual bacterial clone still acquire genetic variation rapidly despite having no meiosis or fertilisation?
Although bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission (`10⁻⁹` spontaneous error rate per base pair), a single infection or flask contains billions (`10⁹–10¹⁰`) of cells dividing every `20–30 minutes`, producing dozens of new random mutants every hour, and bacteria can also share resistance genes horizontally via circular **plasmids**.
:::

:::careful
- **Misconception: "Mutations are always harmful and turn organisms into monsters."**
  *Correction:* Most DNA base substitutions are **neutral** (occurring in non-coding DNA or producing a synonymous codon that codes for the same amino acid). Some are harmful (such as `HbˢHbˢ` in non-malarial regions), and a small fraction are **beneficial** in a specific environment (such as a bacterial enzyme that breaks down penicillin or `HbᴬHbˢ` in a malaria belt).
- **Misconception: "Meiosis and fertilisation create new alleles."**
  *Correction:* Crossing over, independent assortment, and random fertilisation create **new combinations of existing alleles** (new genotypes), but **only mutation** creates brand-new alleles by altering the DNA base sequence.
- **Misconception: "Good nutrition or climate can shift discontinuous traits just a little bit."**
  *Correction:* Environment modifies continuous traits (like height and body mass), but has **zero effect** (`0%`) on discontinuous traits (like `ABO` blood group).
:::

## 14.2 The six-step filter: natural selection, peppered moths and antibiotic resistance

:::scene Birmingham & Dorset Woodlands, 1953–1955, and KEM Hospital Ward, Mumbai

Stand in two places where life-or-death filtering happens in real time. First, step into a coal-smoke-blackened woodland near Birmingham in `1953` beside the physician **Bernard Kettlewell**. Resting side by side on a soot-darkened birch trunk are two peppered moths (*Biston betularia*): one has pale, speckled wings (`typica`), and the other is coal-black (`carbonaria`). From a hidden canvas tent, you watch a robin hop onto a branch, cock its head, snap up the conspicuous pale moth in half a second, and fly off without noticing the black moth three centimetres away. Second, return to the paediatric ward at KEM Hospital in Mumbai, where a boy with a bacterial throat infection felt his fever drop on Day 3 of a `7-day` amoxicillin course and threw the remaining four days of capsules into a drawer. Inside his tonsils, `99%` of the sensitive bacteria are dead—leaving the `1%` of pre-existing resistant bacteria with an open feast of glucose and zero competition.

In Section 14.1 we saw how random DNA mutations continuously inject new alleles into populations, and how meiosis and fertilisation shuffle them. Left to itself, mutation is blind: it produces alleles that darken moth wings, thin plant cuticles, or alter bacterial enzymes completely at random. How does a blind glitch engine produce organisms that match their environments with breathtaking precision?

The answer is the mechanism independently discovered by **Charles Robert Darwin** and **Wallace in Ternate** (Alfred Russel Wallace) and presented jointly in London in `1858`: ==natural selection==. Natural selection is not a conscious designer; it is an automatic, six-step ecological filter that operates whenever heritable variation meets a limited environment:

1. **Variation within populations:** Individuals of the same species vary in their phenotypes and underlying genotypes (alleles), including new alleles created by random mutation.
2. **Production of many offspring (overproduction):** Every species—whether bacteria dividing every `20 minutes` as **Louis Pasteur** cultured in broth flasks, oak trees dropping thousands of acorns, or moths laying hundreds of eggs—produces far more offspring than the environment can support.
3. **Struggle for existence (competition and selection pressure):** Because food, water, light, territory, and mates are limited, and because predators, pathogens, and climate extremes threaten survival, individuals face an intense ==struggle for existence==. Any environmental factor that affects an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing is called a ==selection pressure==.
4. **Differential survival:** Individuals that happen to possess inherited adaptive features best suited to those selection pressures have a higher probability of surviving than less well-adapted individuals (==differential survival==).
5. **Reproduction and inheritance by survivors:** The better-adapted survivors reach reproductive age, breed, and **pass on their advantageous alleles** to their offspring.
6. **Increase in advantageous allele frequency over many generations:** Over many generations, the frequency of the advantageous allele increases in the population while disadvantageous alleles become rarer. This change in the heritable characteristics (and allele frequencies) of a population over generations is ==evolution==.

:::predict
In a bacterial throat infection of `10⁹` cells, `99%` of the bacteria are sensitive to amoxicillin and `1%` carry a pre-existing random mutation that produces a beta-lactamase enzyme capable of partially breaking down amoxicillin. - In (A), sustained drug pressure plus immune phagocytes eliminate both sensitive and remaining resistant bacteria (`0%` load); in (B), Days `1–3` kill sensitive competitors, leaving the `1%` resistant bacteria alive to divide every `20 minutes` into a `99.4%` drug-resistant relapse
- In (A), taking antibiotics for `7` days causes the patient's white blood cells to mutate and become resistant to amoxicillin
???
The second choice. Let us trace both clinical cases step by step:

- **Case A (Completed 7-day antibiotic course):** On Days `1–3`, amoxicillin inhibits peptidoglycan cell-wall cross-linking (recall Chapter 2 and Chapter 10), lysing almost all sensitive bacteria (`total bacterial load drops from 100% to ~26%`), which is why the patient's fever and pain fade by Day 3. Crucially, continuing the full high-dose regimen across Days `4–7` maintains lethal drug concentrations long enough to suppress the small remaining fraction of partially resistant bacteria while the patient's own immune system (phagocytes and antibodies from Chapter 10) engulfs and clears the last survivors (`0%` bacterial load by Step 10).
- **Case B (Stopped early on Day 3 — resistant relapse):** Stopping the antibiotic on Day 3 is an evolutionary disaster. Days `1–3` killed off the `99%` of sensitive bacteria that were competing for nutrients and attachment sites, acting as a strong selection pressure that left the pre-existing resistant cells (`~21%` of the surviving Day-3 load) alive. When the drug pressure stops at Day 3, those surviving resistant cells divide every `20 minutes` by binary fission without competition. By Day 7 (Step 10), the infection rebounds to `98%` of its original size—and now **`99.4%` of the bacterial population carries the resistance allele**.
:::

This process is the evolution of ==antibiotic resistance==—natural selection happening inside a single human body over a few days. Notice the exact causal language required in biology: the antibiotic **never causes** the bacteria to mutate, and bacteria **never become "immune"** (bacteria are single cells with no lymphocytes or antibodies). Instead, random mutation creates a resistance allele first; the antibiotic acts as the environmental **selection pressure** that kills sensitive cells; resistant survivors reproduce and pass the resistance allele (on their circular chromosome or a transferable **plasmid**) to their offspring. When strains of *Staphylococcus aureus* accumulate resistance to methicillin, penicillin, and related beta-lactam antibiotics in hospitals, they form **MRSA** (*methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus*). To slow down the evolution of antibiotic resistance, doctors and patients must follow four rules:

1. **Prescribe antibiotics only when essential**—and **never for viral infections** (such as influenza, the common cold, or measles), because viruses lack cell walls and `70S` ribosomes, so antibiotics have `0%` effect on viruses while needlessly selecting for resistant bacteria in the patient's gut and throat.
2. **Ensure patients complete the full prescribed course** so sensitive and partially resistant bacteria are completely eliminated rather than selected for relapse.
3. **Reduce agricultural overuse** of routine antibiotics as growth promoters in healthy livestock.
4. **Maintain strict hospital hygiene** (hand-washing, isolation wards, and sterilisation) to block transmission of resistant strains like MRSA.

:::evidence Birmingham & Dorset, 1953–1955 & Cambridge, 2001–2007: Kettlewell's peppered moths and Majerus's 4,864-moth replication | Field predation proof
The peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) rests motionless on birch and oak trees during the day, relying on camouflage to hide from insectivorous birds (robins, hedge sparrows, and great tits):

- **Pre-1848 unpolluted woodland:** Tree trunks and branches were covered in pale, crusty lichens. Pale speckled `typica` moths (`cc`, `~98%`) blended invisibly against the lichens, whereas rare black `carbonaria` mutants (`C_`, first recorded in Manchester in `1848` at `<2%`) stood out starkly and were eaten by birds.
- **1895–1955 industrial soot peak:** Coal-burning factories pumped out sulphur dioxide (`SO₂`), which killed the pale lichens, and black soot, which coated tree bark. Now the selection pressure inverted: pale `typica` moths stood out like white beacons on black bark, while dark `carbonaria` moths were camouflaged. By `1895`, `90–98%` of Manchester peppered moths were dark `carbonaria`. Between `1953` and `1955`, **Bernard Kettlewell** released marked moths in polluted Birmingham woods and unpolluted Dorset woods: in Birmingham he recaptured **`53.1%` of dark `carbonaria` versus only `27.5%` of pale `typica`** (nearly a `2 : 1` survival advantage for dark moths), whereas in unpolluted Dorset he recaptured **`13.7%` of pale `typica` versus `4.7%` of dark `carbonaria`** (nearly a `3 : 1` advantage for pale moths).
- **Methodological critique and 2001–2007 validation:** Later biologists pointed out that Kettlewell had released moths during the day onto lower tree trunks, whereas wild moths normally settle before dawn high in the canopy underneath horizontal twigs. Between `2001` and `2007`, Cambridge geneticist Michael Majerus conducted a six-year experiment releasing `4,864` moths at natural low densities onto high canopy branches at dusk, confirming directly that differential bird predation was indeed the decisive selective agent.
- **Post-1956 Clean Air reversal:** After the UK Clean Air Acts cut `SO₂` and soot emissions, pale lichens recolonised tree bark across Britain. Without a single moth "choosing" to turn pale, bird predation reversed the allele frequencies: dark `carbonaria` plunged from `>90%` back below `12%`, and pale `typica` rebounded to `88–95%`.

**The 60-second forceps-and-newspaper camouflage hunt (Mumbai Desk Investigation)**

*Educational adaptation disclosure:* This safe household and classroom simulation is a non-trialled educational adaptation designed to demonstrate how visual predation on a patterned background produces differential survival and shifts morph frequencies across generations. It uses only dry paper dots and blunt tweezers (`0` chemicals, `0` heat, `0` live animals).

You can measure differential survival and multi-generation allele shifts on your desk in ten minutes:

1. **Prepare the habitat and prey:** Lay one full sheet of dense black-and-white printed newspaper flat on your desk. Using a standard paper hole-punch, punch `30` circular dots from a sheet of bright white printer paper (`typica` morph) and `30` circular dots from a spare sheet of matching newspaper print (`carbonaria` morph). Scatter all `60` dots (`50%` white, `50%` newspaper) evenly across the newspaper sheet while your partner looks away.
2. **Generation 1 predation (15 seconds):** Give your partner a pair of blunt tweezers or forceps (the "robin's beak"). Looking away between each pick, they must turn, grab the **very first dot that catches their eye**, place it in a cup, and repeat as fast as possible for `15 seconds` (removing roughly `30` dots).
3. **Reproduction by survivors:** Count the surviving dots left on the newspaper. In a typical trial on newspaper print, predators snatch `~22` conspicuous white dots and only `~8` camouflaged newspaper dots, leaving `8` white survivors (`26.7%`) and `22` newspaper survivors (`73.3%`). Now let each surviving dot "reproduce" once by adding one matching daughter dot next to every survivor (bringing the population back to `60` dots: `16` white, `44` newspaper).
4. **Generations 2 and 3:** Repeat the `15-second` forceps hunt and doubling step for two more generations, recording the percentage of camouflaged newspaper dots at Generation `0` (`50%`), Generation `1` (`~73%`), Generation `2` (`~88%`), and Generation `3` (`~95%`). Finally, swap the background sheet to plain white paper and watch the selection pressure reverse direction.
:::

:::note
**Why bacteria evolve resistance in days while elephants take millennia.** Two biological factors make bacterial evolution ultra-fast: (1) **Generation time:** Under warm, nutrient-rich conditions, *E. coli* or *S. aureus* divides every `20–30 minutes`, completing `72` generations in a single `24-hour` day—as many generations as human populations have experienced since the Roman Empire. (2) **Plasmids and horizontal transfer:** As we learned in Chapter 2, bacteria carry small circular **plasmids** alongside their main chromosomal DNA. Resistance genes often sit on plasmids that can be copied and passed directly between neighbouring bacteria (even across species), spreading a resistance allele through a hospital ward at high speed.
:::

:::five
? State the six sequential steps of natural selection in exact biological order.
(1) **Genetic variation** exists within a population (including new alleles from random mutation); (2) **Production of many offspring** (overproduction); (3) **Struggle for existence** (competition for limited resources and exposure to selection pressures); (4) **Differential survival** of individuals better adapted to the environment; (5) **Reproduction by survivors**, passing on their advantageous alleles to offspring; (6) **Increase in frequency of advantageous alleles** over many generations.
? Explain why dark *carbonaria* peppered moths increased from `<2%` to `>90%` in Manchester during the nineteenth century and then fell back below `12%` after the 1956 Clean Air Act.
Industrial `SO₂` killed pale lichens and soot blackened tree bark; insectivorous birds (the selective agent) easily spotted and ate pale `typica` moths while camouflaged dark `carbonaria` moths survived, reproduced, and passed on the dark allele. After the Clean Air Act cut pollution, pale lichens recovered, reversing the selection pressure so pale `typica` moths survived better and rebounded in frequency.
? Why is it biologically wrong to say *"the antibiotic caused the bacteria to mutate"* or *"the bacteria became immune to the antibiotic"*?
Mutations are **random changes in DNA base sequence that exist before** the antibiotic is applied; the antibiotic acts only as an **environmental selection pressure** that kills sensitive bacteria and allows pre-existing resistant mutants to survive and reproduce. Furthermore, bacteria are single prokaryotic cells lacking lymphocytes or antibodies, so they become **resistant**, never "immune".
? Explain what happens inside a bacterial infection when a patient stops taking a `7-day` antibiotic course on Day 3 because their fever has gone.
By Day 3, the antibiotic has killed most of the `99%` sensitive bacteria (relieving symptoms), but pre-existing partially or fully resistant bacteria remain alive. Stopping the drug on Day 3 removes the selection pressure before the drug and immune phagocytes can eliminate those survivors; freed from competition with sensitive cells, the resistant bacteria divide rapidly by binary fission, causing a **drug-resistant relapse**.
? State three practical measures that reduce the rate at which antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains (such as MRSA) evolve and spread.
(1) Prescribe antibiotics **only when necessary and never for viral infections** (since viruses lack cell walls and ribosomes); (2) ensure patients **complete the full prescribed antibiotic course**; (3) enforce strict **hospital hygiene, hand-washing, and isolation** (and reduce routine antibiotic use in healthy livestock).
?? Why did Charles Robert Darwin have to write to Captain FitzRoy and Syms Covington in London in 1837 to reconstruct his Galápagos finch data?
Because on the Galápagos in `1835` **Charles Robert Darwin** mistook the birds for blackbirds, grosbeaks, and wrens and mixed specimens from different islands in the same bags; only when ornithologist **John Gould** showed in `1837` that all `13` species were closely allied endemic finches did Darwin use FitzRoy's and Covington's island-labelled skins to prove that populations had diverged on separate islands.
?? How did Alfred Russel Wallace's February 1858 insight on Ternate connect Thomas Malthus's population essay to natural selection?
Shivering with malarial fever on Ternate, **Wallace in Ternate** recalled Malthus's observation that populations overproduce offspring while disease, famine, and predators keep population sizes stable; Wallace deduced that any variant with a heritable survival advantage in that struggle must survive at a higher rate and replace less-adapted forms over generations.
?? Why did Michael Majerus repeat Bernard Kettlewell's peppered moth experiment between 2001 and 2007, and what did his 4,864-moth study show?
Critics noted that **Bernard Kettlewell** had released moths in daylight onto lower tree trunks rather than natural high canopy branches; Majerus's six-year study of `4,864` moths resting naturally on high branches confirmed that differential bird predation was indeed the decisive selective pressure driving both industrial melanism and its Clean Air reversal.

## 14.3 Built for the extremes: adaptive features of xerophytes, hydrophytes and animals

:::scene Thar Desert Dunes, Rajasthan & Mumbai Botanical Pond

Hold two living leaves up to the morning sun in a botany teaching garden in Mumbai. In your left hand is a tough, grey-green blade of marram grass (*Ammophila arenaria*), a coastal sand-dune plant that survives scorching wind and salt spray alongside desert prickly-pear cacti (*Opuntia*). Instead of lying flat, the marram grass blade curls tightly inward like a rolled-up scroll; under a hand lens (`×20`), its outer surface shines with a thick, candle-like wax, while its inner grooves are lined with tiny interlocking hairs. In your right hand is a broad, flat circular leaf of a white water lily (*Nymphaea*) lifted from a freshwater pond. Its upper surface feels soft and barely waxy, its underside is slick with pond water, and when you snip across its stalk with scissors, you see a honeycomb of open air tunnels so buoyant that the cut leaf floats right back to the surface like a life raft.

Why are those two leaves built as architectural opposites? In Chapters 3 and 5, we discovered the central dilemma of every photosynthesising plant: to make glucose (`6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`), a leaf must open its stomatal pores so carbon dioxide (`CO₂`) can diffuse in—yet the moment stomata open, water vapour (`H₂O`) diffuses out down a steep water potential gradient (`ΔΨ`) from the moist spongy mesophyll air spaces into the drier outside air (transpiration).

Over millions of generations, natural selection has shaped inherited features that solve this dilemma in radically different habitats. In Cambridge biology, an ==adaptive feature== is defined precisely as **an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment**. On the Extended curriculum, we also define an organism's ==fitness== as **the probability of an organism surviving and reproducing in the environment in which it is found**. Notice how the two definitions lock together: an inherited adaptive feature functions to increase an organism's fitness in its specific habitat.

Let us examine how two classic groups of plants—**xerophytes** and **hydrophytes**—solve opposite water-balance problems:

### 1. Xerophytes: plants adapted to dry, arid environments
A ==xerophyte== (from the Greek *xeros*, dry, and *phyton*, plant) is a plant adapted to live in environments with very scarce liquid water and high evaporation rates—such as hot deserts (cacti like *Opuntia* in Rajasthan), wind-swept coastal sand dunes (marram grass, *Ammophila arenaria*), or frozen regions where soil water is locked as ice. If a standard garden bean plant (*Phaseolus vulgaris*, a mesophyte) is planted on a dry sand dune, it loses water at `~9.8 mg cm⁻² h⁻¹`, wilts as its cells lose turgor pressure, and dies. Xerophytes survive because they inherit four linked structural and physiological adaptations:

- **Thick waxy cuticle (`~8.5 µm` vs `2.2 µm` in bean):** The epidermis secretes a thick, waterproof layer of lipid wax that blocks direct evaporation through the epidermal cell walls, cutting non-stomatal water loss by `~85%`.
- **Sunken stomata in rolled grooves lined with hairs (trichomes):** In marram grass, hinge cells lose turgor in dry wind, curling the leaf blade into a tight C-shaped tube around longitudinal grooves. Stomata sit at the bottom of **sunken pits** inside these grooves, guarded by dense interlocking **epidermal hairs (trichomes)**. Together, the rolled leaf, sunken pits, and hairs trap a pocket of still, moist water vapour right outside the stomatal pore. Retaining this humid boundary layer **reduces the water potential gradient (`ΔΨ`)** (and concentration gradient) between the internal mesophyll air spaces and the air immediately outside the stoma, drastically slowing the rate of water vapour diffusion (`transpiration drops to ~1.4 mg cm⁻² h⁻¹`, conserving `92%` of water).
- **Small, rolled, or spine-like leaves + succulent stems:** In cacti (*Opuntia*), leaves are reduced to sharp **spines** with almost zero surface area for transpiration (while also deterring thirsty desert herbivores). Photosynthesis and water storage (`mucilage-rich parenchyma`) are shifted into the thick, green, fleshy **succulent stem**.
- **Extensive deep and widespread root systems + inverted stomatal rhythm:** Deep taproots (`3.2 m` or more in marram grass and desert shrubs) reach deep underground water tables, while wide, shallow surface root networks rapidly absorb brief rain showers or morning dew before it evaporates. Many cacti also close their stomata completely during the hot day and open them only in the cool night air to fix `CO₂`.

### 2. Hydrophytes: plants adapted to wet, aquatic environments
A ==hydrophyte== (from *hydro*, water) is a plant adapted to live partially or wholly submerged in freshwater ponds, lakes, and slow rivers—such as floating water lilies (*Nymphaea*), sacred lotus (*Nelumbo nucifera*), or submerged pondweed (*Elodea* / *Hydrilla*). A hydrophyte faces the exact opposite selection pressures: liquid water is unlimited, so losing water by transpiration is no threat at all; instead, the plant struggles to keep its photosynthetic leaves up in the sunlight and to supply oxygen (`O₂`) to roots buried in waterlogged, anaerobic mud (recall from Chapter 6 that dissolved `O₂` diffuses `~10,000×` slower in water than in air). Hydrophytes inherit four contrasting adaptive features:

- **Large air spaces (`aerenchyma`) in leaves, stems, and petioles (`~58%` of tissue volume):** Specialised spongy tissue called ==aerenchyma== forms continuous honeycomb air chambers (`lacunae`) that provide **buoyancy**—floating the broad leaf blades right at the sunlit water surface—and create a low-resistance internal **gas diffusion pathway** that channels `O₂` produced during photosynthesis down to the submerged respiring roots.
- **Stomata on the upper epidermis only (`~280 mm⁻²` upper vs `0 mm⁻²` lower):** In a floating water lily leaf, the lower epidermis rests flat against liquid water where air pores would flood and be useless (`0` stomata). Instead, stomata are concentrated exclusively on the **upper epidermis** exposed to the atmosphere, allowing rapid `CO₂` uptake and `O₂` release. (Fully submerged leaves like *Elodea* have **no stomata at all**, exchanging dissolved gases directly across thin epidermal walls.)
- **Very thin or absent waxy cuticle (`~0.4 µm`):** Because the leaf is surrounded by water and has no need to conserve moisture, it does not waste energy synthesising a thick wax coat; a thin cuticle also aids direct absorption of dissolved mineral ions and gases across submerged surfaces.
- **Reduced root systems (`~0.25 m`) and minimal xylem/support tissue:** Because dense surrounding water supports the weight of the stems and leaves by upthrust, hydrophytes need very little lignified xylem or sclerenchyma for mechanical support, and because water can be absorbed directly across stems and leaves, roots are small, feathery, and serve mainly for gentle anchorage in pond mud.

Just as plants show structural and physiological adaptations to water availability, animals in extreme habitats inherit coordinated suites of **structural** (anatomical), **physiological** (internal biochemical/organ function), and **behavioural** adaptive features:

- **Thar Desert dromedary camel (*Camelus dromedarius* — hot arid `45 °C`):**
  1. *Physiological:* Kidneys with an exceptionally long loop of Henle reabsorb almost all filtered water, producing thick, syrupy concentrated urine and dry faecal pellets; body temperature is allowed to rise from `34 °C` at dawn to `41 °C` at afternoon peak before sweating starts, saving litres of water per day.

  3. *Behavioural:* Sitting facing the sun at midday to minimise the body surface area exposed to direct solar radiation.
- **Arctic polar bear (*Ursus maritimus* — polar pack ice `−40 °C`):**
  1. *Structural:* A large, compact body mass (`400–600 kg`) with tiny rounded ears and a short tail gives a **very small surface-area-to-volume (`SA : V`) ratio**, minimising heat loss per kilogram of core tissue; a `10 cm` layer of subcutaneous **blubber** plus dense underfur and hollow, translucent guard hairs trap an insulating layer of warm air while providing **white camouflage** against snow and ice when stalking seals; broad, rough-soled paws act as snowshoes on thin ice and paddles in icy water.
  2. *Physiological:* Countercurrent heat exchange in limb blood vessels transfers heat from warm outgoing arterial blood to cold returning venous blood before it reaches the paws.

## 14.4 Guided evolution and deep time: selective breeding, fossils and DNA clocks

:::scene Imperial Sugarcane Station, Coimbatore, 1930s & Institute of Palaeobotany, Lucknow, 1948

Visit two landmark Indian laboratories where scientists worked at opposite ends of evolutionary time. First, walk through the experimental sugarcane fields at the Imperial Sugarcane Breeding Station in Coimbatore in the `1930s` alongside the cytogeneticist **E. K. Janaki Ammal**. Wild Indian *Saccharum spontaneum* cane is thin, fibrous, and barely sweet (`~4–8%` sucrose), though tough enough to survive northern Indian frosts and fungal rot; tropical *Saccharum officinarum* is thick and juicy, yet delicate. By crossing selected parents, counting their polyploid chromosomes under the microscope, and picking only the sweetest, hardiest hybrid seedlings generation after generation, Janaki Ammal helped double the sucrose yield of Indian sugarcane (`8.2% → 16.4%`), turning India from a sugar importer into a self-sufficient producer. Second, travel north to Lucknow in `1948` to the worktable of palaeobotanist **Birbal Sahni**. Under his diamond saw and microscope lie `160-million-year-old` Jurassic rocks from the Rajmahal Hills of Bihar, preserving the cellular wood, leaves, and fleshy seed-cones of *Pentoxyleae*—an entire extinct plant group frozen in stone from an age when India was still welded to Africa, Australia, and Antarctica in the supercontinent Gondwana.

In Section 14.2 we saw how the natural environment—predators, drought, cold, and disease—filters heritable variation across generations. Ten thousand years ago, human farmers realised they could replace the environment as the filter. Instead of waiting for nature to select which plants or animals bred, humans chose the parents themselves. This human-directed process is called ==selective breeding==, or ==artificial selection==.

Whether a farmer in Gujarat is breeding **Gir** or **Sahiwal** dairy cattle for higher milk yield (`1,350 L → 3,650 L` per lactation across `40` generations), **E. K. Janaki Ammal** is breeding Coimbatore sugarcane for sucrose content and frost hardiness, or an agronomist is breeding dwarf, rust-resistant wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), selective breeding always follows the exact same **five-step loop**:

1. **Choose the desirable feature:** Humans decide which heritable characteristic they want (such as high milk yield in cattle, high sucrose or disease resistance in crops, docile temperament in dogs, or large flower petals).
2. **Select parents showing the feature:** From a genetically varied population, breeders select male and female individuals that display the highest level of that desired phenotype.
3. **Breed the selected parents together:** The chosen parents are crossed (by controlled pollination in plants or selective mating/artificial insemination in livestock) while preventing unselected individuals from breeding.
4. **Select the best offspring:** When the offspring mature, breeders measure their phenotypes and **select only the offspring that show the desired characteristic most strongly** to become the parents of the next generation.
5. **Repeat over many generations:** Steps `2–4` are repeated across **many generations** (`10`, `20`, `40`, or more) until the frequency of the desired alleles rises and the trait is strongly expressed across the whole bred variety.

How does selective breeding compare with natural selection? Both processes rely on heritable genetic variation, differential reproduction, and allele frequency changes over generations, yet they differ sharply in six ways:

| Dimension | Natural Selection | Selective Breeding (Artificial Selection) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Selective agent** | The **environment** (predators, pathogens, climate, food competition) | **Humans** (farmers, breeders, horticulturalists, scientists) |
| **2. Trait favoured** | Adaptive features that help the organism survive and reproduce in its wild habitat | Characteristics useful, profitable, or attractive to **humans** |
| **3. Effect on wild fitness** | **Increases** survival and reproductive fitness in the natural environment | May **reduce** wild survival fitness (e.g. seedless fruits, heavy udders, flat-faced Pugs) |
| **4. Rate of change** | Usually slower across long geological/evolutionary timescales | Rapid across tens of human-controlled generations |
| **5. Genetic diversity** | Maintained higher across large, outbreeding wild populations | **Reduced** (`gene pool narrows`), increasing risk of **inbreeding depression** |
| **6. Classic examples** | Peppered moths (*Biston betularia*), antibiotic resistance, `Hbˢ` balancing selection | Coimbatore sugarcane, Gir dairy cattle, rust-resistant wheat, *Brassica* vegetables |

The second choice. Both dangers stem from the loss of genetic diversity—a problem called ==inbreeding depression==:

- **Unmasking harmful recessive alleles:** Every organism carries a few rare, harmful recessive alleles (`a`) hidden harmlessly in the heterozygous state (`Aa`). When breeders repeatedly mate closely related top performers (inbreeding), the probability that both parents share the same hidden recessive allele rises sharply (`Aa × Aa → 25% aa`), causing homozygous recessive genetic disorders, reduced fertility, and lower vigour in the offspring.
- **Vulnerability to new pathogens or climate shifts:** In a genetically uniform crop monoculture or inbred herd, almost all individuals share the same alleles at immunity and stress loci. If a new strain of wheat stem rust (*Puccinia graminis*) or a severe drought hits the field, **no individual may carry a resistance allele**, so the entire crop can be wiped out at once. To prevent this, modern plant breeders preserve wild seed banks and periodically cross domesticated varieties back with hardy wild relatives—exactly as **E. K. Janaki Ammal** crossed sweet tropical *Saccharum officinarum* with wild Indian *Saccharum spontaneum*.

How do we trace evolution not just over `40` agricultural generations, but across `4,000 million years` of Earth's history? Two independent lines of scientific evidence—one written in rock strata, the other written directly in the `A, T, C, G` base sequences of living cells—converge on the exact same evolutionary tree:

1. **The fossil record and palaeobotany:** A ==fossil== is the preserved remains, mineralised cast, or trace of an ancient organism embedded in sedimentary rock. Because younger sedimentary layers are deposited on top of older layers, rock strata form a chronological calendar: deep Cambrian rocks (`>500 million years ago`) contain only simple marine organisms, Permian Gondwana coal seams (`~299–252 Ma`) contain seed-ferns like *Glossopteris*, and Jurassic strata (`~201–145 Ma`) in the Rajmahal Hills of Bihar contain **Birbal Sahni**'s *Pentoxyleae*—transitional gymnosperms showing intermediate anatomical features between ancient seed-ferns, cycads, and modern conifers.
2. **Molecular clocks and DNA sequence classification (Cambridge `0610` Topic `1.2` Supplement):** In Chapter 1, we saw that traditional classification grouped organisms mainly by visible morphology (anatomy). However, morphology alone can be fooled by **convergent evolution**, when distantly related species independently evolve similar streamlined bodies in similar habitats (such as a cartilaginous shark and a mammalian dolphin). Modern biologists classify organisms by comparing the **base sequences of DNA** (or `16S` ribosomal RNA, as **Carl Woese** pioneered in `1977` when he revealed the three domains of life: *Bacteria*, *Archaea*, and *Eukarya*, complementing **Lynn Margulis**'s proof of the bacterial endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts) and the **amino acid sequences of universal proteins** such as mitochondrial **Cytochrome c** (`104` amino acids) and **beta-globin** (`146` amino acids).

Because neutral DNA mutations accumulate at a roughly steady average rate over millions of years, sequence differences act as a ==molecular clock==:

- **More similar DNA base sequences (and fewer amino acid differences)** prove that two species diverged **more recently** and share a **more recent common ancestor** (e.g. Human and Chimpanzee have **`0` amino acid differences across all `104` positions of Cytochrome c**, `0` differences across `146` positions of beta-globin, and `98.8%` coding DNA sequence identity, having diverged from a common ancestor `~6.5 million years ago`).
- **Greater DNA base differences (and more amino acid substitutions)** prove that two species diverged **longer ago** and share a **more distant common ancestor** (e.g. Human vs Rhesus monkey = `1` Cytochrome c difference, `~28 Ma`; Human vs Dog = `11` differences, `~94 Ma`; Human vs Dogfish shark = `24` differences, `~420 Ma`; Human vs Baker's yeast = `45` differences, `~1,100 Ma`).

:::evidence European Sea Cliffs & Urbana, 1977: Wild Brassica oleracea divergence and the Cytochrome c / 16S rRNA molecular clock | Comparative genomic proof
Two classic datasets show artificial selection over millennia and natural selection over hundreds of millions of years:

- **One wild coastal weed, six vegetables:** Along the limestone sea cliffs of Europe grows wild mustard (*Brassica oleracea*). By selecting for different plant organs over the past `2,500` years, human farmers bred **cabbage** (enlarged terminal leaf bud), **Brussels sprouts** (lateral axillary buds), **broccoli** (immature flower clusters and thick stems), **cauliflower** (sterile flower meristems), **kale** (large open leaves), and **kohlrabi** (swollen storage stem)—all from the same ancestral species.
- **Concordance of fossils and molecules:** When palaeontologists date the earliest fossil splits between primates (`~28 Ma`), placental carnivores (`~94 Ma`), cartilaginous fishes (`~420 Ma`), and fungi (`~1,100 Ma`), those geological dates correlate linearly with the number of amino acid differences in Cytochrome c (`0, 1, 11, 24, 45`) and DNA base substitutions across living genomes. Stones and base sequences tell the exact same story.

Repeatedly breeding closely related individuals with the desired phenotype **narrows the gene pool (reduces genetic diversity)** and increases **homozygosity**, so hidden **harmful recessive alleles (`aa`)** are much more likely to come together in offspring (causing genetic defects and lower vigour). Because all individuals become genetically similar, if a new pathogen arrives to which one individual is susceptible, the **entire population lacks resistance alleles** and can be devastated.

This provides a quantitative **molecular clock** that avoids mistakes caused by superficial morphological similarity (convergent evolution).

Because **convergent evolution** under similar environmental selection pressures can mould distantly related lineages into similar outer shapes (such as streamlined bodies and dorsal fins in sharks and dolphins), while microscopic organisms (such as *Bacteria* and *Archaea*) may look like simple rods or spheres under a microscope despite having diverged billions of years ago in their `16S rRNA` and DNA sequences.

Because the genetic code is **degenerate**: silent base substitutions in the third position of DNA triplets (such as `GAA → GAG`) change the DNA base sequence without changing the amino acid incorporated into the Cytochrome c protein, so DNA base sequences record even finer recent evolutionary divergence than amino acid sequences.

:::careful
- **Misconception: "Selective breeding is the same thing as genetic engineering."**
  *Correction:* **Selective breeding** crosses whole organisms of the same (or closely related) species over **many generations** and selects offspring without directly editing DNA molecules. **Genetic engineering** (which we will study in Chapter 15) transfers or edits a specific gene directly at the molecular level (often between unrelated species, such as inserting the human insulin gene into a bacterial plasmid) in a single laboratory generation.
- **Misconception: "Humans evolved from chimpanzees, so chimpanzees are our ancestors."**
  *Correction:* No living species evolved from another modern living species. Humans and chimpanzees are **evolutionary cousins** that diverged from a **shared common ancestor** roughly `6.5 million years ago`.
- **Misconception: "Selective breeding creates new alleles to make cows give more milk."**
  *Correction:* Human breeders cannot cause new alleles to appear; selective breeding **selects and combines pre-existing alleles** (and rare spontaneous mutations) across generations.
:::

In a public-health genetics seminar at KEM Hospital in Mumbai, Kym compares two districts from the surveys of **Anthony Allison** (`1954`) and **L. D. Sanghvi**: **District H**, a cool, high-altitude mountain valley (`2,400 m`) where *Anopheles* mosquitoes and *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria are completely absent (`carrier frequency HbᴬHbˢ = 0.8%, q ≈ 0.004`), and **District M**, a warm, humid lowland forest belt where *P. falciparum* malaria has been holoendemic for centuries (`carrier frequency HbᴬHbˢ = 26.6%, q ≈ 0.158`).

Use all four sections of Chapter 14 (together with Chapters 11–13) to answer three synthesis questions:

1. **Molecular origin and non-directed mutation (`14.1` & Ch 11):** Explain how the `Hbˢ` allele originally arose on Chromosome `11`, and evaluate the claim that *"living in a malarial forest caused people's beta-globin genes to mutate into `Hbˢ` for protection."*

Predict and explain what will happen to the frequency of the `Hbˢ` allele in District M over those `20` generations.
???

1. **Molecular origin (`14.1` & Ch 11):** The `Hbˢ` allele arose by a **spontaneous, random gene mutation**—specifically a single base substitution (`CCT GAG GAG → CCT GTG GAG`, transcribed as `GUG` in mRNA) at codon `6` of the beta-globin (`HBB`) gene on Chromosome `11`, substituting hydrophobic **valine (`Val⁶`)** for hydrophilic **glutamic acid (`Glu⁶`)**. The claim that malaria *caused* the mutation is **false**: environments and pathogens never direct DNA mutations. The `GAG → GTG` glitch occurred randomly at low baseline rates (`~10⁻⁹` per base pair) in both malarial and non-malarial populations; malaria acted only afterward as an **environmental selection pressure**.
2. **Punnett inheritance & balancing selection (`14.2–14.3` & Ch 13):**
    - **Cross (`HbᴬHbˢ × HbᴬHbˢ`):** Gametes `(Hbᴬ)` and `(Hbˢ)` combine in a `2 × 2` grid to yield **`25% HbᴬHbᴬ` : `50% HbᴬHbˢ` : `25% HbˢHbˢ`** (`1 : 2 : 1` genotype ratio).
    - **In malaria-free District H:** There is no malaria mortality, so `HbᴬHbᴬ` and `HbᴬHbˢ` individuals have equal high fitness (`w = 1.00`), while `HbˢHbˢ` individuals suffer severe sickle-cell anaemia (`w ≈ 0.20`) and often die before reproducing. Every early death of an `HbˢHbˢ` individual removes two `Hbˢ` alleles from the population without any compensating advantage, driving `Hbˢ` frequency down toward zero (`q ≈ 0.004`).
    - **In malarial District M:** Two opposing selection pressures operate simultaneously. Homozygous normal (`HbᴬHbᴬ`) children suffer high mortality from severe *P. falciparum* malaria (`w ≈ 0.85`), and homozygous sickle (`HbˢHbˢ`) children suffer high mortality from sickle-cell anaemia (`w ≈ 0.20`). **Heterozygous carriers (`HbᴬHbˢ`) have the highest fitness (`w = 1.00`, heterozygote advantage)** because `~60% HbA` prevents severe anaemia while infected cells sickle prematurely and are cleared by the spleen (`60–80%` protection against severe malaria). Because `HbᴬHbˢ` survivors reproduce most successfully and pass `Hbˢ` to `50%` of their gametes, the allele is maintained at a stable equilibrium (**balancing selection**, `q ≈ 15.8%`).
3. **Prediction after malaria eradication (`14.3–14.4`):** Once *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria is eradicated from District M, the selective advantage of `HbᴬHbˢ` carriers over `HbᴬHbᴬ` individuals disappears (`HbᴬHbᴬ` and `HbᴬHbˢ` now have equal survival fitness `w = 1.00`), while natural selection against `HbˢHbˢ` (due to sickle-cell anaemia) continues to remove `Hbˢ` alleles every generation. Consequently, the frequency of the `Hbˢ` allele (`q`) and the percentage of `HbᴬHbˢ` carriers will **steadily decrease over the next `20` generations** toward the low baseline seen in District H.
