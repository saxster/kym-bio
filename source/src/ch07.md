question: How does a meal become material that cells can use, without the gut digesting its own walls?
---

A piece of buttered pav contains starch in the bread and fat in the butter. Neither a bread crumb nor a fat droplet can simply travel through an intestinal cell as a ready-made parcel of lunch. Chapter 6 followed small fuels inside cells. Before those fuels can arrive, a meal must be moved, mixed, chemically changed and taken across a barrier.

This begins Volume III, **The Human Orchestra**. Several organs will contribute different parts of one job. Keep asking two questions: **what changed chemically, and which boundary did the material cross?** Those questions will carry us from the mouth towards the cells that grow, repair themselves and regenerate ATP.

## 7.1 A tube through the body, and a barrier to cross

When you swallow food in an ordinary meal, it disappears from view. Has it therefore become part of your muscles? The tempting answer is “It is inside me, so my cells already have it.” But a coin inside a pocket has not crossed the cloth. Being enclosed and crossing a boundary are different facts.

:::predict
Imagine a starch-containing food particle in the small intestine, surrounded by fluid but still in the gut's central space. Which event would establish absorption of material from it?
- It has passed below the stomach, so absorption is already complete
- Suitable substances cross the gut's epithelial lining into the internal tissues
- The particle leaves the body as faeces
???
The second. Position along the tube is not proof of crossing its lining. Digestion can produce absorbable substances in the central space; absorption then requires passage across the barrier. Unabsorbed remnants can continue along the tube and leave.
:::

:::evidence Follow connections rather than the body outline | Anatomy and transport
Anatomical examination reveals a continuous canal from mouth to anus, bounded by living tissue. A swallowed meal follows that canal. The liver, pancreas and gallbladder connect through ducts that deliver secretions into it; the meal does not travel through those organs in turn.

Microscopy reveals a lining of cells between the contents of the intestine and deeper tissues. Measurements of substances on either side, and experiments with transport across intestinal preparations, distinguish a nutrient present in the canal from one that has crossed the lining. Merely finding food lower in the abdomen establishes movement along the canal. It does not establish uptake into blood, lymph or the cells that will use it.
:::

The route is the **mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus**. The small intestine begins with the **duodenum**; the **ileum** is its final part. Much of the large intestine is the **colon**. The rectum stores faeces before they leave through the anus. The oesophagus carries swallowed food; the **trachea** is a separate airway. These organs form the ==alimentary canal==.

:::note Alimentary canal
The connected digestive tube from mouth to anus, through which food and unabsorbed remnants pass. Associated glands deliver secretions into it.
:::

Salivary glands supply the mouth. The pancreas delivers digestive enzymes and bicarbonate to the duodenum. The liver makes bile, which can be stored in the gallbladder before reaching the duodenum. “Associated organ” describes a connection and contribution; it does not add another food compartment to the route.

:::fig c7_canal() | Trace the solid food route and the dashed secretion routes separately. This is a connection map, not an anatomical-position chart or a scale drawing. Bile can reach the duodenum without first entering the gallbladder.

The central space of the canal is its ==lumen==. It is physically inside your outline, but remains continuous with the external environment through mouth and anus. The lining separates that space from your internal tissues. That is the precise sense in which a meal in the gut is still “outside”: **it has not yet crossed this epithelial barrier**. The phrase does not mean the gut is detached from you or its contents cannot affect you.

:::note Lumen
The space inside a hollow structure. In the gut, it contains food, secretions and unabsorbed material on the external side of the epithelial barrier.
:::

Now separate the jobs. Taking food or drink into the canal is ==ingestion==. Breaking a piece of bread into smaller pieces by chewing is **physical digestion**: molecules can remain chemically unchanged while food particles become smaller. Enzyme-catalysed reactions that change larger food molecules into smaller products are **chemical digestion**. Together these processes are ==digestion==.

:::note Ingestion
Taking substances such as food and drink into the digestive system through the mouth.
:::

:::note Digestion
Physical breakdown of food and chemical breakdown of food molecules. Chemical digestion produces smaller substances suitable for absorption; chewing alone does not do all that chemistry.
:::

Moving nutrients across the intestinal lining into internal tissues is ==absorption==. Their later uptake and use by cells is ==assimilation==. An amino acid crossing the gut lining has been absorbed; using supplied amino acids to build a muscle protein is assimilation. Neither word means that a bread crumb passes intact into muscle.

:::note Absorption
Movement of substances from the intestinal lumen across its lining into internal tissues, followed by transport through blood or lymph as appropriate.
:::

:::note Assimilation
Uptake and use of supplied nutrients by cells, for example using amino acids to make cellular proteins.
:::

Follow the pav through three events: swallowing it is ingestion; glucose made from its starch crossing the lining is absorption; a cell using that supplied glucose as fuel or storing its material as glycogen is assimilation. If some fibre remains unabsorbed and leaves in faeces, that is egestion.

:::try Classify the event
A glucose molecule is still in the lumen, an amino acid has entered blood, and a muscle cell uses supplied amino acids to build protein. Which event establishes absorption, which establishes assimilation, and which has not yet crossed the barrier? Use the route drawing to justify each answer.
:::

:::fig c7_boundary() | Being inside the body's outline differs from crossing its lining. The drawing enlarges one epithelial barrier; it does not show the entire intestinal wall as one cell thick.

The removal of undigested food and unabsorbed remnants as faeces is ==egestion==. Chapter 1 distinguished **excretion**, removal of metabolic waste and substances in excess. Egested faeces also contain microbes, shed cells and some substances excreted into the gut. It would be false to say that everything in faeces has always stayed outside the body. The distinction concerns the process and material being described.

:::note Egestion
Removal of undigested food from the body as faeces. It differs from excreting waste made by metabolism.
:::

Why take in food at all? ==Nutrition== supplies chemical materials for growth, development and repair, and fuels whose reactions release energy for metabolism. Bread's starch can supply glucose; proteins can supply amino acids; fats supply useful lipid material as well as fuel. Vitamins, mineral ions and water have essential roles too. They are not all fuels. Fibre helps normal bowel function even though our enzymes do not hydrolyse cellulose as they do starch.

:::note Nutrition
Taking in and using materials that support life, including sources of chemical energy and material for growth and repair.
:::

Not every absorbed substance needs prior digestion. Water, mineral ions, glucose already present in food and many vitamins do not first need to be cut into smaller molecules. Nor is “small” enough to explain every crossing: solubility in a membrane's lipid interior, charge and available transport proteins matter. We will follow that machinery in section 7.4.

:::five Digestion and the food route
? What is it?
A sequence of ingestion, physical and chemical processing, absorption, cellular use and removal of unabsorbed remnants. Different organs contribute different jobs.
? Why is it called this?
Digestion comes from a Latin word connected with separating and arranging. The modern biological meaning concerns food processing, not merely moving food out of sight.
? What does this make possible?
It explains how the same meal can supply chemical building material and fuel while leaving some material unabsorbed.
? Why does it work?
Physical breakdown exposes surfaces; enzymes change suitable molecules; a selective lining admits substances through particular routes.
? How does it help?
You can ask whether a failure concerns movement, chemical processing or barrier crossing instead of calling all three “digestion”.
?? How do we know?
Anatomy maps the connected tube and ducts, chemical analysis identifies products, and transport experiments test movement across the lining.
?? What it isn't
Food passing through liver and pancreas, a crumb turning directly into muscle, or every nutrient requiring chemical digestion first.
?? Where does it stop working?
A route map alone does not explain each enzyme or membrane transporter. Those require mechanisms at a smaller scale.
:::



:::careful
> “Digestion makes energy, and anything left over is excretion.”

Digestion changes food's physical form and chemical molecules. Respiration is a separate set of reactions transferring chemical energy for cellular metabolism, including ATP regeneration. Egestion removes undigested remnants; excretion concerns metabolic waste or excess substances. Neither process creates energy or makes atoms disappear.
:::

The first establishes absorption across the gut barrier. The second establishes movement along the canal, not absorption; if the unabsorbed food later leaves as faeces, that is egestion. Assimilation requires uptake and use by cells, for example using glucose as fuel or incorporating its material into stored glycogen. Arrival inside the body's outline alone proves none of those later uses.

## 7.2 Moving, mixing and digesting without digesting the wall

The bread in your buttered pav can become a paste while its starch molecules remain mostly joined in chains. What turns that paste into absorbable substances? One tempting answer is “The stomach grinds everything fine enough, and acid dissolves the rest.” That gives physical force and acid every job. We need a comparison that separates them from the chemistry of digestive juice.

:::predict
Gastric juice is collected and placed with food in a warm vessel outside the stomach. If food breaks down there too, which claim is challenged?
- Digestive juice can carry out chemical work apart from muscular grinding
- All stomach digestion must be caused by its muscular wall rubbing on food
- The whole intestinal absorption route has been reproduced in the vessel
???
The second claim is challenged. Chemical activity can occur without the stomach wall doing the grinding. This observation does not tell us that acid alone is responsible, nor does it show absorption into living tissues.
:::

:::evidence Gastric juice could act outside the stomach | Experiments in 1825; book in 1833
In 1822, Alexis St. Martin, a French Canadian voyageur, survived an accidental gunshot injury at Mackinac. William Beaumont treated him. An opening into St. Martin's stomach remained. Beaumont began gastric experiments in 1825 and published his book in 1833.

In its First Series, Experiments 2–3 compare beef in collected gastric juice, kept warm in a vial in a water bath, with similar beef placed inside the stomach. Beaumont described breakdown in both settings. The collected juice could act outside the muscular organ. That supported a chemical contribution, without isolating acid from enzymes or proving complete absorption.

St. Martin supplied indispensable participation, not merely an unusual organ. Beaumont was also his doctor and employer. Payment and dependence complicate the relationship; a later contract is not proof of modern informed consent.
:::

The ordinary sequence starts in the mouth. **Incisors** have cutting edges; **canines** can tear; **premolars and molars** have surfaces that crush and grind. The hard outer crown is **enamel**, with **dentine** beneath. The inner **pulp** contains nerves and blood vessels. **Cement** covers the root, which is held in the jaw by supporting tissues; gums surround the teeth. Physical division exposes more surface to digestive fluids. It does not break every covalent bond in the food.

Once a bolus—a mass prepared for swallowing—enters the oesophagus, coordinated muscle contractions propel it. A region behind it narrows while the region ahead relaxes. The contraction progresses along the tube. This is ==peristalsis==. Other contractions mix contents locally; the stomach churns food with its secretions. Food is not simply falling through a passive pipe. No unusual eating position is needed to investigate the idea.

:::note Peristalsis
Coordinated waves of contraction and relaxation in the muscular wall of the digestive tube that propel its contents. Local mixing movements also occur.
:::

:::fig c7_movement() | The two snapshots show a contraction progressing behind a bolus, not measured positions or a transit time. The separate tooth cutaway is enlarged and simplified; mechanical division and molecular hydrolysis remain different changes.

:::five Physical digestion and peristalsis
? What is it?
Physical digestion divides food without changing its molecules. Peristalsis is coordinated muscular propulsion along the gut; mixing also brings contents into contact with secretions.
? Why is it called this?
Physical refers to changes of form. Peristalsis is linked to Greek words meaning contraction around, matching the narrowing of the tube.
? What does this make possible?
It exposes food surfaces and explains movement along a tube whose contents cannot be delivered by gravity alone.
? Why does it work?
Teeth apply force to food, and coordinated muscles change the tube's shape and push its contents onwards.
? How does it help?
You can distinguish increased enzyme access and transport from an enzyme changing a molecule's covalent links.
?? How do we know?
Anatomical and movement observations reveal teeth, muscles and progressing contractions; chemical tests distinguish unchanged molecules from new products.
?? What it isn't
Chewing creating glucose from every starch molecule, or every mixing contraction producing the same forward journey.
?? Where does it stop working?
Small food particles can still contain large molecules. Movement and surface exposure cannot replace missing digestive enzymes.
:::

Saliva moistens food and contains **amylase**. Its active site catalyses hydrolysis of suitable links within starch, producing **maltose and shorter carbohydrate chains**. It does not simply convert all starch straight into glucose. In the small intestine, pancreatic amylase continues starch processing. Enzymes on the epithelial surface finish the job: **maltase**, for example, hydrolyses maltose to glucose.

In ==hydrolysis==, water is a reactant in a change that splits a covalent connection. Part of the water contributes hydrogen and part contributes an oxygen–hydrogen group to the resulting products. Think of a chemical rearrangement involving water and the substrate, rather than tiny scissors acting without chemical partners.

:::note Hydrolysis
Chemical splitting of a molecule through reaction with water. Digestive enzymes catalyse hydrolysis of particular links in their substrates.
:::

For one small example, maltose has two joined sugar units. One maltose plus one water gives two glucose molecules:

:::eq
C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O → 2C₆H₁₂O₆
:::

Both sides contain 12 carbon, 24 hydrogen and 12 oxygen atoms. Water's atoms matter. With the chosen nominal relative atomic masses C = 12, H = 1 and O = 16, the relative-mass account is **342 + 18 = 360**. These are proportions for the reaction, not measurements of a meal. Removing a bond line from a drawing without supplying the new end groups would miss part of the chemistry.

:::recall 4
If an enzyme helps a bond undergo hydrolysis, does the energy come simply from breaking that isolated bond?
???
No. Breaking an isolated chemical bond requires energy. The overall reaction also forms bonds and changes interactions with surrounding water. Its complete free-energy change and coupling determine available energy transfer. An enzyme lowers an activation barrier; it does not supply a fresh packet of net energy each time.
:::

:::five Chemical digestion and hydrolysis
? What is it?
Enzyme-catalysed reactions change food molecules into smaller products; water participates when links are hydrolysed.
? Why is it called this?
Hydro- refers to water, and the Greek-derived ending refers to loosening or splitting. Chemical digestion specifies a change in molecules rather than only particle size.
? What does this make possible?
It supplies absorbable products from materials whose intact large molecules are unsuitable for ordinary intestinal uptake.
? Why does it work?
A suitable enzyme binds its substrate and provides a lower-barrier reaction route involving water and particular covalent links.
? How does it help?
You can identify substrate, intermediate and final product instead of assuming every food becomes glucose in one step.
?? How do we know?
Chemical analysis identifies substrates and products; controlled enzyme assays compare changes with and without an active catalyst.
?? What it isn't
A living enzyme eating food, an enzyme used as fuel, or bond breaking alone releasing energy.
?? Where does it stop working?
The enzyme needs a suitable substrate and chemical conditions. Water alone does not make every food molecule hydrolyse rapidly.
:::

The stomach supplies different conditions. Its glands secrete hydrochloric acid and the inactive enzyme precursor **pepsinogen**. Activation in the acidic lumen produces **pepsin**, a protease that hydrolyses proteins into shorter chains called **peptides**. Acid also helps unfold food proteins and kills many ingested microorganisms. It is not itself pepsin, and does not sterilise every meal.

Stomach acidity varies with food, secretion, location and time. There is no one pH that describes every stomach at every moment. Conditions favouring pepsin differ from those for pancreatic enzymes in the small intestine. Partially digested food and gastric juice form **chyme**, which is released into the duodenum in portions.

Protein processing continues there. The pancreas supplies proteases, including **trypsin**, initially made as an inactive precursor. Activation in the intestine helps keep its digestive action out of the producing tissue. These proteases and further peptidases produce smaller peptides and amino acids. Some small peptides can enter intestinal cells and be hydrolysed there; the amino acids can then leave towards blood. “Protein digestion is finished in the stomach” skips much of the work.

If the gut wall contains protein, why doesn't pepsin digest that too? It can damage tissue if the defences fail. The explanation is protection, not a special kind of indigestible wall protein.

Surface cells supply **mucus** that holds bicarbonate near the lining. Bicarbonate reacts with acid, so conditions immediately beside the cells can be less acidic than the surrounding lumen. Closely joined epithelial cells limit leakage towards deeper tissue. Renewal and repair help maintain that barrier. Producing pepsin first as inactive pepsinogen reduces premature action inside the secreting cells. These defences cooperate; no single one guarantees complete protection.

:::fig c7_protection() | Mucus, local bicarbonate, epithelial joins and renewal protect a living surface beside a reactive lumen. The layers are schematic, not a measured pH profile or thickness scale. Pepsinogen activation is shown separately from food movement.

:::five Protection of the gut wall
? What is it?
A maintained barrier and regulated enzyme activation that reduce damage to the gut's own living tissues.
? Why is it called this?
Barrier names a boundary restricting passage. An inactive precursor is a form made before the active enzyme; the terms describe roles rather than an indestructible coating.
? What does this make possible?
It explains how digestive chemistry can act beside tissues that contain the same broad classes of molecules as food.
? Why does it work?
Mucus retains buffering bicarbonate near cells; epithelial joins restrict leakage; repair maintains the surface, and some proteases begin as inactive precursors.
? How does it help?
You can explain both ordinary protection and the possibility of damage without claiming that acid and enzymes recognise food by ownership.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy, secretion measurements and physiological studies distinguish the layers, local conditions and inactive and active enzyme forms.
?? What it isn't
An acid-proof wall, perfect protection or a claim that every digestive enzyme must be secreted inactive.
?? Where does it stop working?
Damage can exceed protection or repair. Ulcers have several possible causes; the drawing cannot diagnose an individual case.
:::

## 7.3 What bile changes—and what lipase changes

The butter from the pav supplies triglycerides. A layer of melted butter or oil tends to gather separately from water. Now imagine dispersing the same fat into many small droplets. Have you made smaller fat molecules? That is the tempting mistake. A droplet is an assembly containing many molecules, just as a crumb contains many starch molecules. Changing the size of the assembly need not change the molecules themselves.

:::predict
One spherical fat droplet is replaced by eight equal spheres with the same combined volume. Which claim follows from geometry alone?
- Each sphere has one-eighth the radius and the total area is eight times as large
- Each sphere has half the radius and the total area doubles
- Fatty acids and glycerol must have formed because the droplets are smaller
???
The second. Halving each radius reduces each sphere's volume to one-eighth and its area to one-quarter. Eight of those areas total twice the original area. That calculation changes the interface, not chemical identity; it does not establish a biological reaction-rate multiplier.
:::

:::evidence Hold the amount fixed and calculate the interface | A mathematical comparison
For a sphere of radius R, volume is 4πR³/3 and surface area is 4πR². Choose a model radius of 3 mm; these are invented dimensions for readable geometry, not measured intestinal droplets. Its volume is 36π mm³ and area is 36π mm².

Divide that volume among eight equal spheres. Each has radius 1.5 mm: eight times 4π(1.5³)/3 is still 36π mm³. Their combined area is eight times 4π(1.5²), or 72π mm². The exposed interface has doubled without creating or removing fat material. This is an exact comparison under the equal-sphere assumptions, not an experiment measuring lipase activity. Unequal or distorted real droplets require a different geometric account.
:::

The liver makes ==bile==, a mixture containing bile salts and other components. The gallbladder stores and concentrates it between releases. Ducts connect the liver and gallbladder with the duodenum; food never has to pass through the gallbladder to meet bile.

:::note Bile
A liver-produced mixture delivered to the small intestine. Its components help disperse fat and carry lipid digestion products; it is not a digestive enzyme.
:::

Some bile components have regions that interact favourably with watery surroundings and regions that associate with fat. Together with gut mixing, they help establish and stabilise dispersed droplets, limiting their merging back into a large mass. This physical dispersion is ==emulsification==. The triglyceride molecules can still be triglyceride molecules afterwards.

:::note Emulsification
Dispersion of one liquid as small droplets in another. In digestion it increases the fat–water interface without itself hydrolysing the fat molecules.
:::

:::live emulsification {"lesson":true}

:::five Emulsification
? What is it?
Physical dispersion of fat into smaller droplets in watery contents, supported by mixing and bile components.
? Why is it called this?
Emulsion comes from a Latin word connected with milking, reflecting the appearance of dispersed droplets. The word does not mean that every emulsion is milk or food.
? What does this make possible?
It increases the interface at which digestive machinery can contact a fixed amount of fat.
? Why does it work?
Smaller three-dimensional droplets have more combined surface for the same volume, while suitable interfacial molecules help them remain dispersed.
? How does it help?
You can explain why physical organisation affects access without claiming that the organisation itself has hydrolysed a triglyceride.
?? How do we know?
Droplet observations show dispersion, and geometry independently predicts area changes under stated size and shape assumptions.
?? What it isn't
A digestive enzyme, a smaller fat molecule, molecular hydrolysis or a universal rate multiplier.
?? Where does it stop working?
Real sizes and shapes vary, droplets may merge, and enzyme supply, mixing and interfacial chemistry can also limit digestion.
:::

:::home Divide the amount, not the molecule
Use a small piece of familiar modelling clay or an adult-prepared piece of cool dough, a washable tray and a ruler. Keep it separate from food for eating; do not taste it. If flour irritates your skin, use suitable modelling clay instead. No knives, heating or chemicals are required.

Roll the material into one roughly spherical ball about 3 cm across, a chosen size large enough to compare with a ruler. Before dividing it, predict the change in radius and total surface area if the same amount becomes eight equal balls. Divide by hand and make eight balls as nearly equal as you can, without adding material. Compare their approximate diameters with the original diameter. Repeat the modelling with the same material; do the results vary?

The controlled quantity is the amount of material; the intended change is the number of approximately equal spheres. The model predicts about half the diameter and twice the total surface area. Your ruler does not directly measure all those surfaces: this is a geometric prediction supported only approximately by the shapes. Unequal balls, dents, cracks and material left on the tray limit the comparison. It shows neither bile chemistry nor lipase action nor intestinal absorption.

Return reusable clay to its container. Put used dough in ordinary household food waste, clean the tray and wash your hands. This proposed activity has been reviewed for feasibility, not physically trialled for the book.
:::

A triglyceride has three fatty-acid parts attached to a glycerol backbone by chemical connections called **ester links**. The backbone and tails are parts of one molecule, rather than separate droplets. Now change those molecular connections. ==Lipase== catalyses hydrolysis of ester links joining fatty-acid parts to glycerol in a triglyceride. It acts at the fat–water interface; **colipase** helps pancreatic lipase work at that interface in the presence of bile. Increased area can make more substrate accessible, but the rate still depends on enzyme supply and conditions.

:::note Lipase
An enzyme that catalyses hydrolysis of lipid molecules. Pancreatic lipase acts on triglycerides at the fat–water interface, producing mainly fatty acids and monoacylglycerols.
:::

Pancreatic lipase mainly removes the two outer fatty acids. The remaining molecule, with one fatty acid still attached, is a **monoacylglycerol**. Two water molecules take part in that two-link hydrolysis. Full hydrolysis would also remove the final fatty acid and release free glycerol. Do not confuse that full breakdown summary with the major products of this pancreatic step.

:::fig c7_lipase() | This original connectivity map separates chemical hydrolysis from droplet division. In the representative triolein equation, both sides contain C 57, H 108 and O 8. The cartoon simplifies ester links and omits fatty-acid head-group atoms; the separate formula account includes every atom and the reaction water.

The lipid products still face watery surroundings. Bile salts help form much smaller assemblies containing digestion products, called **micelles**. An emulsion droplet contains many fat molecules; a micelle carries lipid products among bile components. Neither is an intestinal cell. Section 7.4 follows products leaving micelles and entering those cells. We must not send an intact oil droplet through a membrane just because a drawing has made it look small.

:::five Lipase and lipid products
? What is it?
Lipase is a catalyst for lipid hydrolysis. The main pancreatic triglyceride step supplies fatty acids and a glycerol-containing product with one fatty acid still attached.
? Why is it called this?
The lipid-related part identifies the substrate family; -ase is a common enzyme-name ending. The reaction, rather than the suffix, explains its job.
? What does this make possible?
It changes fat molecules into products that can be presented to the absorptive surface, beyond merely exposing more unchanged fat.
? Why does it work?
At the interface, enzyme-assisted reactions with water split particular ester links. Colipase assists pancreatic lipase there.
? How does it help?
You can distinguish a fat droplet, a hydrolysis product and a micelle carrying those products towards the lining.
?? How do we know?
Chemical analysis detects changed molecular products; interfacial studies distinguish enzyme action from physical dispersion.
?? What it isn't
Bile doing enzyme chemistry, emulsification itself breaking covalent links, or every product being free glycerol immediately.
?? Where does it stop working?
Product uptake still requires contact with a functioning epithelium, and pancreatic lipase is not the only contributor to all lipid digestion.
:::

There is another compartment problem. Chyme arrives from the stomach carrying acid, while pancreatic enzymes need different conditions. Bile is alkaline, but it is not the only neutralising input. **Pancreatic bicarbonate is a major contribution**, with intestinal secretions contributing too. Bicarbonate reacts with incoming acid, reducing acidity and helping provide suitable conditions in the duodenum. Dispersing fat, neutralising acid and hydrolysing ester links are three distinguishable jobs.

:::careful
> “Bile turns fat into tiny molecules, so lipase only has to absorb them.”

Bile helps disperse droplets and present digestion products. Lipase catalyses chemical hydrolysis; it does not perform the whole absorption route. A smaller droplet still contains many fat molecules, and a larger calculated interface does not fix the measured speed of digestion.
:::

Learn that net-breakdown convention while recognising that the main pancreatic products include **monoacylglycerols**, which retain glycerol attached to one fatty acid.

A model changes one fat sphere into 64 equal spheres of the same total volume. A pupil claims there are 64 times as many exposed surfaces, so digestion must be 64 times faster and the molecules must already be hydrolysed. Repair the argument.

The radius becomes one-quarter of the original; each sphere has one-sixteenth of its area. Sixty-four such areas total four times the original area. ## 7.4 Crossing the intestinal wall

Suppose digestion has supplied glucose from the pav beside the intestinal lining. The molecule has reached the correct address. Why doesn't that finish the journey? The tempting answer is “It is now small, so it can fall through little holes into blood.” But a living barrier is selective. More available surface helps only if substances can cross it through suitable routes.

:::predict
The intestine has a large folded surface, but a glucose transporter at its lumen-facing membrane stops functioning. Does extra surface alone guarantee the same glucose absorption?
- Yes; surface area overrides all membrane chemistry
- No; area and a functioning crossing mechanism are different requirements
- Yes; villi are holes straight into blood
???
The second. More surface can provide room for more transport machinery, but it cannot replace a missing transport mechanism. Villi are tissue projections lined with cells, not open funnels into vessels.
:::

:::evidence Measure a folded surface carefully | A reassessment published in 2014
Herbert Helander and Lars Fändriks combined a literature review with microscopic measurements of normal adult gut lining. Their abstract reports an estimated total mucosal area of about **32 m²**, with about **2 m²** belonging to the large intestine. Subtracting those approximate figures leaves roughly **30 m²** for the small intestine.

The investigators challenged older textbook estimates of 260–300 m² and the familiar tennis-court comparison. Their work supports a very large folded absorptive surface, while showing how the number depends on measurement and extrapolation. An adult estimate is not a measurement of your intestine. The full paper was not available for this book's checking; the published abstract gives the method and qualified estimate used here.
:::

Start with the lining's large folds. They carry small tissue projections called ==villi==. Each villus contains blood capillaries and a lymph capillary called a **lacteal**, surrounded by supporting tissue. Its surface is covered by an epithelial layer. The absorptive epithelium can be **one cell thick**; the entire intestinal wall also has deeper tissue and muscle layers.

:::note Villi
Small tissue projections of the small-intestinal lining that increase its surface area. A villus contains vessels and is covered by epithelium.
:::

Each absorptive epithelial cell has a further level of projections from its lumen-facing membrane: ==microvilli==. Together they form a **brush border**. A microvillus is part of one cell's surface, not a miniature villus with its own capillary. The extra membrane provides space for transport proteins and some final digestive enzymes, including maltase.

:::note Microvilli
Tiny membrane projections on individual absorptive cells. They add cell-surface area and are distinct from the larger tissue projections called villi.
:::

:::fig c7_villus() | Inspect three different levels: folds bearing villi, one villus with blood and lymph routes, then a cell's microvilli. The panels use separate enlargements. Microvilli contain neither little blood vessels nor little lacteals.

:::five Villi and microvilli
? What is it?
Villi are tissue projections; microvilli are projections of individual cells' membranes. Both increase available surface at different scales.
? Why is it called this?
Villus comes from Latin for a tuft of hair; villi is the plural. Micro- marks the much smaller cell-surface projections, without making them literal hairs.
? What does this make possible?
These structures provide a large exchange surface within a compact organ, with vessels close to the absorptive cells.
? Why does it work?
Folding adds area; a thin epithelial route shortens the crossing distance; nearby transport helps carry absorbed material away.
? How does it help?
You can connect structure with transport while keeping the cell membrane, epithelial layer and entire organ wall distinct.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy resolves folds, tissue villi and cell microvilli at different enlargements. Estimating total area requires measurements and explicit extrapolation.
?? What it isn't
Open holes into blood, a one-cell-thick entire intestine or a tennis court measured inside every person.
?? Where does it stop working?
Area alone cannot supply missing transport proteins, gradients, blood flow or intact tissue. An adult area estimate is not a universal body constant.
:::

:::recall 3
If glucose moves from a low concentration in the lumen into a cell with a higher glucose concentration, can simple diffusion of glucose alone explain that net movement?
???
No. Simple diffusion alone gives net spreading down a concentration gradient when passage is possible. Glucose can enter by a coupled transporter using the downhill movement of sodium ions. An ATP-driven pump helps maintain the sodium electrochemical gradient. The glucose movement is therefore powered indirectly by ATP-supported ion transport, rather than by diffusion alone or the molecule wanting to enter.
:::

### Follow the bread-derived glucose

Follow **glucose** in more detail. A protein at the lumen-facing membrane carries sodium ions and glucose together. Sodium moving down its electrochemical gradient can drive glucose entry against glucose's own gradient. At the tissue-facing membrane, an ATP-driven sodium–potassium pump keeps the cell relatively low in sodium, maintaining that driving difference. ATP is used at the pump; a glucose molecule is not handed an ATP ticket at the first membrane.

Glucose can then leave the cell through another transport protein, enter tissue fluid and cross into a blood capillary. Blood flow carries it away, helping maintain conditions for further transfer. These are distinct steps: **lumen → epithelial cell → tissue fluid → capillary**. Blood then travels through the **hepatic portal vein** towards the liver, where nutrients can be processed or stored, before entering the wider circulation.

Amino acids use transport proteins too; different substances do not share one universal gate. Some small peptides enter cells before final hydrolysis. Fructose provides another contrast: it can enter by **facilitated diffusion**, using a transporter down its concentration gradient. “All nutrients simply diffuse” and “all nutrients are actively pumped in exactly the same way” both fail.

:::try Pause at the two cell faces
Point to the lumen-facing glucose entry route and the tissue-facing exit route. Which pump uses ATP, and which entry step uses the ion difference maintained by that pump? Keep this arrangement: the nephron and oral rehydration in Chapter 10 will use the same transport principle with different compartment labels.
:::

### Follow the butter-derived lipid material

Now follow **long-chain lipid products**. Fatty acids and monoacylglycerols leave their micelle carriers near the cell surface and enter epithelial cells. Within the cells, much of this material is reassembled into triglycerides. These combine with proteins and other lipids in new transport packages called ==chylomicrons==. The packages leave the cells into tissue fluid and enter lacteals. Lymph vessels carry them onwards, eventually joining blood near the heart.

:::note Chylomicrons
Lipid-and-protein transport particles made by intestinal cells. They carry much absorbed long-chain dietary fat material through lymph before it reaches blood.
:::

The original emulsion droplet has not squeezed unchanged into a lacteal. Nor has a whole micelle simply become a chylomicron. **Digestion products crossed the epithelium, and new packages were assembled.** Some shorter-chain fatty acids follow a more direct portal-blood route instead. Fat-derived material does not have one compulsory destination.

:::five Nutrient transport across and beyond the epithelium
? What is it?
Selective entry, processing where needed, exit into tissue fluid and onward transport through appropriate blood or lymph routes.
? Why is it called this?
Portal names a gateway route towards the liver. Chylomicron names a small chyle-related particle; its biological identity here is a newly assembled lipid-and-protein package, not a whole food droplet.
? What does this make possible?
It links absorbable products in the lumen with delivery towards distant cells, explaining why chemical digestion alone is insufficient.
? Why does it work?
Transport proteins and maintained ion gradients enable particular membrane crossings. Cells rebuild and package much long-chain lipid material before lymph transport; blood carries many water-soluble nutrients through the liver.
? How does it help?
You can trace a substance across each boundary and distinguish a membrane transporter from the flow of a carrying fluid.
?? How do we know?
Physiological transport studies, tracers, chemical analyses and microscopy distinguish carriers, products, packages and vessel routes.
?? What it isn't
All absorption being passive diffusion, all fat taking one route, or whole micelles passing unchanged into lacteals.
?? Where does it stop working?
Routes depend on the substance and tissue condition. This schematic does not predict permeability, transit time or absorption efficiency for a person.
:::

Water is absorbed along with these other transfers. Under ordinary conditions, **most water is absorbed in the small intestine**, while the colon absorbs further water from what remains. Digestive secretions also add water to the canal; water moves both ways, so absorption is a net account. The colon is not the only place that takes water back.

Return to the meal. After absorption and delivery, assimilation gives the meal's material new uses. Cells can build proteins from supplied amino acids. Glucose can contribute to glycogen storage or enter fuel-processing reactions. Lipid material can contribute to membranes or stores. Respiration transfers chemical energy into ATP-supported work and heat; that still does not turn atoms into energy. Material for growth and repair and a continuing usable energy supply are connected needs, not interchangeable substances.

:::careful
> “Villi suck everything straight into blood, and the large intestine absorbs all the water.”

Villi provide a large, thinly lined surface, but particular transport mechanisms do the crossing. Much long-chain lipid material is packaged inside epithelial cells before entering lymph; some other lipid-derived substances use portal blood. Water is absorbed in both intestines, usually mainly in the small intestine. Names for structures cannot replace these routes and mechanisms.
:::

Trace glucose and material from long-chain dietary fat from the lumen towards the wider circulation. Name one membrane event and the main carrying route for each. Why does neither route show an intact food droplet becoming part of a muscle?

Glucose can enter an epithelial cell through sodium-coupled transport, supported indirectly by ATP-maintained ion gradients. It leaves through a tissue-facing transporter, crosses tissue fluid into a capillary, and travels in portal blood towards the liver before wider circulation. ## The missing reservoir

:::case The patient whose gallbladder was removed
This is a fictional reasoning case, not advice about any individual patient. A person's gallbladder has been removed. The liver and pancreas still function, and the remaining bile ducts provide an open route to the duodenum.

One pupil says: “There can be no bile, because the gallbladder made it.” Another says: “Then fat cannot be chemically digested, because bile was the fat-digesting enzyme.” A third says: “Nothing at all has changed: production, storage and delivery are the same.”

Correct all three claims. What could continue, what has changed, and why would the drawing be insufficient to predict every person's experience after the operation?
???
The **liver makes bile**. Removing the gallbladder removes its storage and concentration function, not the liver's production. Bile can flow through the remaining ducts from liver to duodenum, without first waiting in that reservoir. The storage and meal-linked delivery pattern have therefore changed even though bile remains available.

Bile helps disperse fat and present its products; it is not lipase. Pancreatic lipase can still catalyse molecular hydrolysis when enzyme, water, substrate and suitable conditions are available. Pancreatic bicarbonate also contributes to neutralising incoming acid. Separating production, storage, delivery, physical dispersion and chemical reaction avoids all three mistakes.

The arrangement explains why gallbladder removal need not abolish fat digestion. It does not promise identical digestion or symptoms for everyone. Duct condition, other organs and individual circumstances matter; the model supplies no personal diagnosis, diet prescription or universal prediction.
:::
