Chapter 7 left glucose and amino acids in portal blood heading towards the liver, and newly packaged chylomicrons travelling through lymph towards blood near the heart. Suppose you have eaten lunch and are climbing three flights of stairs to a flat in Mumbai. Your leg muscles need that absorbed glucose—and, as Chapter 6 showed, their mitochondria also need a continuous supply of **oxygen** to regenerate ATP aerobically while clearing **carbon dioxide**.

Why can't oxygen simply diffuse from the skin or the throat all the way down to a thigh muscle? Chapter 2 gave the geometric reason: diffusion is rapid across micrometres, not across centimetres or metres. A human body therefore combines **bulk flow**—moving air in and out of the chest, and pumping blood through branching vessels—with **microscopic diffusion** across ultra-thin surfaces at each end of the journey. Yet that creates a mechanical puzzle. The gas-exchange barrier in the lungs is roughly half a micrometre thick and would leak or tear under high fluid pressure, whereas pushing blood to the brain, kidneys and legs requires high pressure. How can one transport system solve both problems at once?

## 8.1 Moving air to an exchange surface

Take a quiet breath through your nose. Air enters because pressure inside the alveoli briefly falls below pressure outside. Your respiratory muscles create that difference by expanding the chest. Follow the movement first, then ask how chest movement reaches the lungs.
:::predict
During quiet inhalation, your diaphragm contracts and flattens downward while your ribcage swings upward and outward. Which sequence explains why outside air enters the alveoli?
- Alveolar pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and drives air inward
- Thoracic volume increases, lowering alveolar gas pressure slightly below atmospheric pressure so outside air is pushed in
- Incoming air forces the passive chest wall and diaphragm to expand like someone blowing up a party balloon
???
The second. In normal breathing, respiratory muscles expand the thoracic cavity first. Increasing the enclosed gas volume lowers alveolar pressure slightly below outside atmospheric pressure (about −1 mmHg gauge), and the higher outside air pressure pushes air down the airway into the lungs.
:::

:::evidence Stereology across two lungs, not a fixed universal count | Ochs et al., 2004
Textbooks often repeat that a human has “300 million alveoli and a tennis court of lung area” as though every person at every age carried the same exact total. In 2004, Matthias Ochs and colleagues used modern stereological sampling (the Euler characteristic of the alveolar network) across six adult human lungs. Reporting totals calculated for **both lungs combined**, they found an adult mean of **480 million alveoli** with a wide range from **274 million to 790 million**, closely correlated with total lung volume.

Classic adult morphometric estimates place the combined gas-exchange surface area of both lungs around **70 m²** (often 50–100 m² depending on inflation and fixation), with a tissue-and-capillary barrier roughly **0.5 µm** (0.2–2 µm) thick. Those are adult two-lung estimates, not a single-lung count and not a direct measurement of a 12-year-old reader's smaller thorax.
:::

Before we look at pressures, separate three processes that everyday speech often tangles together under the word “breathing”:

1. ==Ventilation== (breathing) is the mechanical bulk flow of air into and out of the lungs caused by pressure differences when thoracic volume changes.
2. ==Gas exchange== is the net **diffusion** of oxygen and carbon dioxide in opposite directions across the moist alveolar and capillary walls down their own partial-pressure gradients.
3. **Cellular respiration** (Chapter 6) is the chemical oxidation of fuel molecules inside living cells (such as in mitochondria) that transfers energy to regenerate ATP.

:::note Ventilation
Mechanical movement of air into and out of the lungs by bulk flow, driven by pressure differences when respiratory muscles change the volume of the thorax.
:::

:::note Gas exchange
Net diffusion of oxygen from alveolar air into capillary blood and carbon dioxide from capillary blood into alveolar air across their shared thin barrier.
:::

Follow the incoming air along its conducting route: **nose (or mouth) → pharynx → larynx → trachea → left and right bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli**. In the nose, hairs and a warm, moist lining with a rich blood supply filter, warm and humidify the air before it reaches delicate lung tissue. The ==trachea== (windpipe) sits in front of the soft oesophagus. Incomplete, **C-shaped rings of cartilage** (and plates of cartilage in the bronchi) stiffen the airway wall so it does not collapse when internal air pressure drops during inhalation, while the flexible rear gap allows the oesophagus behind it to bulge as swallowed food passes down by peristalsis.

:::note Trachea
The main airway leading from the larynx to the two bronchi, supported by C-shaped rings of cartilage that keep the lumen open during pressure changes.
:::

Air is dusty and carries bacteria, fungal spores and smoke particles. Along the trachea, bronchi and larger bronchioles, two specialised epithelial cell types run a continuous self-cleaning conveyor belt:

- **Goblet cells** (and mucus-secreting glands) produce sticky **mucus** that traps inhaled dust and microorganisms before they can reach the gas-exchange sacs.
- **Ciliated epithelial cells** carry microscopic hair-like **cilia** that beat in coordinated waves, sweeping the mucus blanket upward towards the pharynx (throat) at the back of the mouth, where it is swallowed; stomach acid and digestive processes destroy many trapped microbes.

:::fig c8_airway_mucociliary() | Trace the conducting airways from nose to bronchioles and the enlarged mucociliary escalator. C-shaped cartilage rings keep the trachea open without pinching the oesophagus behind it. Dimensions are enlarged schematics, not a uniform scale.

Not every millilitre of a breath reaches the exchange surface. In a typical resting adult breath (a **tidal volume** V_T of about 500 mL), roughly 150 mL fills the conducting tubes—nose, pharynx, trachea, bronchi and bronchioles—where the walls are too thick for gas exchange. That 150 mL is the **anatomical dead space** (V_D). Only the remaining V_T − V_D ≈ 350 mL brings fresh outside air into the ==alveoli== at each breath.

:::note Alveoli
Microscopic air sacs at the ends of the respiratory bronchioles where gas exchange occurs between alveolar air and surrounding pulmonary capillaries.
:::

### How chest movement expands the lungs

Now examine the chest as a mechanical pump. The lungs sit inside an airtight chest chamber—the **thorax**—bounded by the **ribcage** and **intercostal muscles** around the sides and a dome-shaped sheet of skeletal muscle at the floor, the **diaphragm**. Two pairs of intercostal muscle layers angle between the ribs: the **external intercostal muscles** and the **internal intercostal muscles**. Surrounding each lung is a double-layered **pleural membrane** enclosing a thin film of **pleural fluid**. That fluid lubricates the sliding surfaces as the lungs expand and recoil, and holds the elastic outer lung surface against the inside of the chest wall.

:::try Pause and trace the air
Without using the word “suck”, explain how an expanding chest makes air enter. Which must briefly be lower: alveolar pressure or outside pressure?

The alveolar pressure must be lower. The chest and lungs expand, lowering pressure inside; the higher outside pressure drives inward flow.
:::

### A worked pressure comparison

To see why air moves, track three pressures (often quoted relative to sea-level atmospheric pressure P_atm = 760 mmHg ≈ 101.3 kPa, written as 0 mmHg gauge):

- **Atmospheric pressure (P_atm = 0 mmHg gauge):** the pressure of the outside air around your nose and mouth.
- **Alveolar pressure (P_alv):** the gas pressure inside the alveoli, which equalises with P_atm at the brief pause between breaths.
- **Intrapleural pressure (P_ip):** the pressure in the thin pleural fluid film between lung and chest wall. Because stretched elastic lung tissue constantly pulls inward while the chest wall tends to spring outward, P_ip stays slightly negative throughout quiet breathing (around −4 mmHg at rest and −6 mmHg at peak quiet inhalation), keeping the transpulmonary pressure (P_alv − P_ip) positive so the lungs stay held open.

Compare the three mechanical states of breathing:

1. **Quiet inhalation (active):** The **diaphragm contracts** and flattens downward, while the **external intercostal muscles contract**, pulling the ribcage **upward and outward**. Together these movements increase the **volume of the thorax**. Because the lungs are coupled to the thoracic wall by the pleural fluid film, lung volume expands too. By Boyle's law (P ∝ 1/V at constant temperature), expanding the alveolar gas volume drops **alveolar pressure slightly below atmospheric pressure** (to about −1 mmHg gauge, or 759 mmHg). Higher atmospheric pressure outside pushes air down the airway into the lungs until P_alv returns to 0 mmHg.
2. **Quiet exhalation (passive):** At rest, the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles simply **relax**. The stretched elastic fibres in the lung tissue and the inward settling of the ribcage and upward dome of the diaphragm (**elastic recoil**) reduce thoracic and lung volume without needing expiratory muscle contraction. Compressing the alveolar gas raises P_alv slightly above atmospheric pressure (to about +1 mmHg gauge, or 761 mmHg), pushing air out of the lungs.
3. **Forced exhalation (active, as in vigorous exercise, coughing or blowing out a candle):** The **internal intercostal muscles contract** to pull the ribcage **downward and inward**, while **abdominal muscles contract** to push the abdominal organs and relaxing diaphragm strongly **upward** against the thorax, generating a larger positive alveolar pressure and rapid airflow.

Inspect where the bell-jar model matches diaphragm pressure changes and where its rigid glass wall and wide air space misrepresent a living ribcage and pleural film.
:::live breathing_gradients {"lesson":true}

Schools often demonstrate breathing with a **bell-jar and rubber-sheet model**: a Y-tube holding two small balloons inside a rigid glass jar closed at the bottom by a flat rubber sheet. Pulling the rubber sheet downward increases the jar's internal volume, lowers the pressure of the air inside the jar, and allows outside atmospheric pressure to push air down the Y-tube into the balloons. That model captures one real physical truth—**volume expansion lowers internal pressure so outside air pushes in**—but it misrepresents four features of your body:

- The glass wall is **rigid**, whereas a real ribcage swings upward and outward through the action of external intercostal muscles.
- The bottom rubber sheet is pulled **downward from a central knot** past a flat baseline, whereas a living diaphragm is an **upward-arching muscular dome** that flattens downward when its muscle fibres contract.
- The jar contains a **wide air-filled space** around the balloons, whereas a living thorax has only a **microscopic fluid-filled pleural space** coupling the lung surface directly to the chest wall.
- New rubber balloons often resist initial inflation until stretched, whereas living alveoli are lined with a thin film of moisture containing **pulmonary surfactant** that reduces surface tension.

:::five Ventilation, dead space and the bell-jar model
? What is it?
Ventilation is bulk airflow driven by muscle-powered changes in thoracic volume and gas pressure; dead space is the conducting airway volume (~150 mL in an adult) that does not participate in gas exchange.
? Why is it called this?
Ventilation comes from Latin *ventilare*, to fan or set breeze in motion. Alveolus is a Latin diminutive meaning a little hollow or small basin.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains how fresh air reaches deep internal surfaces without claiming that lungs “suck” air molecules or that every millilitre of a breath reaches the alveoli.
? Why does it work?
Contracting the diaphragm and external intercostals increases thoracic volume, dropping alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure; subtracting the 150 mL dead space from each breath gives the fresh alveolar volume per breath.
? How does it help?
You can see why shallow panting (250 mL × 24 breaths/min = 6.0 L/min total, 2.4 L/min alveolar) delivers much less fresh alveolar air than deeper quiet breathing (500 mL × 12 breaths/min = 6.0 L/min total, 4.2 L/min alveolar).
?? How do we know?
Spirometry measures tidal volumes and rates; gas-washout measurements determine anatomical dead space; pressure transducers record alveolar and pleural pressure swings.
?? What it isn't
A vacuum cleaner pulling air by attraction, cellular respiration making ATP, or a rigid bell jar with a wide air gap around the lungs.
?? Where does it stop working?
Bulk flow stops at the terminal respiratory units; across the final half-micrometre barrier between air and blood, molecules cross by diffusion down partial-pressure gradients.
:::

### From moving air to diffusing molecules

Once fresh air reaches the alveoli, **gas exchange** takes over. Each alveolus is adapted to maximise the rate of diffusion (recall Chapter 3):

- **Very large total surface area:** hundreds of millions of tiny pouches across both lungs expose far more area than two hollow bags of the same volume.
- **One-cell-thick walls and minimal diffusion distance:** both the alveolar wall (squamous epithelium) and the closely pressed capillary wall (endothelium) are **one cell thick**, making the combined air-to-blood barrier roughly 0.5 µm across. Red blood cells squeeze through the narrow capillaries in single file, pressing close to the wall.
- **Moist inner lining:** oxygen dissolves in the thin film of moisture lining the alveolus before diffusing across the cells into plasma and red blood cells.
- **Steep concentration (partial-pressure) gradients maintained by two flows:** continuous **ventilation** replenishes alveolar O₂ and removes CO₂ on the air side, while continuous **blood flow** from the pulmonary artery brings deoxygenated, CO₂-rich blood and carries oxygenated blood away on the capillary side.

:::recall 6
Why does blood returning from working leg muscles to the lungs have a lower oxygen partial pressure and a higher carbon dioxide partial pressure than alveolar air?
???
In Chapter 6, aerobic cellular respiration inside working muscle cells uses oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor (forming water) and releases carbon dioxide as carbon compounds are oxidised to regenerate ATP. Tissue blood therefore loses oxygen to the muscle cells and picks up carbon dioxide before returning to the right side of the heart and lungs.
:::

Partial pressure describes the gas-pressure contribution associated with a gas; in blood it reflects the dissolved gas, rather than all the oxygen bound to haemoglobin. Haemoglobin greatly increases blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity without making oxygen content and oxygen partial pressure the same quantity.

Because of those maintained gradients, oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse simultaneously in opposite directions—each down its own independent partial-pressure gradient (in alveolar air, P_O₂ ≈ 104 mmHg vs 40 mmHg in arriving venous blood; in arriving venous blood, P_CO₂ ≈ 45 mmHg vs 40 mmHg in alveolar air). Comparing **inspired (inhaled)** and **expired (exhaled)** air shows the net result:

- **Oxygen (O₂):** drops from about **21%** in inspired air to about **16%** in expired air, because oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into capillary blood for use in aerobic cellular respiration.
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂):** rises a hundredfold from about **0.04%** in inspired air to about **4%** in expired air, because carbon dioxide produced by respiring cells diffuses out of blood into alveolar air.
- **Nitrogen (N₂):** stays at about **78%** in both inspired and expired air, because nitrogen gas is neither used nor produced by human cells.
- **Water vapour and temperature:** inspired air has **variable** humidity and temperature matching the room, whereas expired air is **saturated** with water vapour evaporated from the moist alveolar and airway lining (and usually warmer).

### Matching breathing to exercise

As you begin climbing the stairs, signals associated with movement and feedback from working muscles help increase breathing. Your muscles produce more CO₂, and ventilation usually rises enough to keep **arterial CO₂ partial pressure near its resting value** during moderate steady exercise. More CO₂ produced each minute does not necessarily mean a higher concentration accumulates in arterial blood.

Chemical sensors also help regulate breathing when CO₂, acidity or oxygen changes. CO₂-related changes in acidity influence the brain’s respiratory centres; peripheral sensors contribute too. At higher exercise intensities, metabolic acidity can add to the drive. Neural and chemical controls work together. Successful regulation can keep a variable nearly steady while the work needed to maintain it rises.

:::careful
> “We breathe in pure oxygen and breathe out pure carbon dioxide, and the diaphragm sucks the air into our lungs.”

Inspired air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen; expired air still contains about 16% oxygen alongside 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. And muscles cannot pull on gas molecules from afar: contracting the diaphragm and external intercostals expands thoracic volume, lowering alveolar pressure slightly below atmospheric pressure so outside air is pushed in.
:::

:::school
Cambridge 0610 links increased breathing during activity with CO₂ production and detection by the brain. Retain that school account alongside the fuller distinction: increased production is not the same as increased arterial concentration, and exercise also recruits neural control.
:::

## 8.2 Harvey’s circulation question and quantitative evidence

Once oxygen crosses the alveolar wall into pulmonary capillaries, blood must carry it—along with the nutrients absorbed in Chapter 7—to every organ in the body. Today the word “circulation” is so familiar that we forget it had to be proved. For centuries before 1628, European and Islamic physicians debated how blood moved. In the dominant tradition descending from **Galen** of Pergamon (second century CE), chyle from the gut was turned by the liver into dark nutritive blood that drifted outward through the veins to nourish the organs, while a separate vital stream moved in the arteries; most of that blood was thought to be **consumed by the tissues** rather than returning in a continuous loop.

:::predict
Suppose a physician calculates that the left ventricle ejects at least several kilograms of blood into the aorta every half hour—far more than the total blood in the body or the mass of food and drink taken in. What does that arithmetic establish by itself, and what does it still need mechanical experiments to show?
- The arithmetic alone proves the exact microscopic shape of capillaries and the role of oxygen in mitochondria
- The arithmetic rules out one-way manufacture and complete consumption of blood by tissues, requiring blood to return and recirculate, while ligature and valve experiments establish the one-way direction of flow in arteries and veins
- The arithmetic proves that Galen thought the liver made a person's entire body weight of blood every hour
???
The second. A conservative lower-bound multiplication rules out continuous one-way production from food and complete tissue consumption unless the vessels burst or run dry. Mechanical experiments with vessel pinching, arm bandages and venous valves then demonstrate that arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins return it towards the heart.
:::

:::evidence A conservative lower bound in Frankfurt, 1628 | William Harvey’s Chapter IX
In *Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus* (*De Motu Cordis*), printed at **Frankfurt** in 1628 by William Fitzer, William Harvey (who had studied at Padua under Hieronymus Fabricius and lectured in London from 1616) laid out a deliberate underestimate argument in Chapter IX.

Rather than insisting on a high stroke volume, Harvey asked his readers to assume the left ventricle ejects only half an ounce (4 drachms), three drachms, or even **one drachm** (~3.9 g in apothecaries' Troy weight) per beat. Over **1,000 beats in half an hour**, even 1 drachm per beat equals 1,000 drachms (**10 lb 5 oz Troy**, ~3.9 kg), and 4 drachms per beat equals 4,000 drachms (**41 lb 8 oz Troy**, ~15.5 kg). The larger bound exceeds a modern adult’s roughly 5 L total blood volume. The smallest bound, ~3.9 kg, does not; repeated replacement at even that rate is still far beyond what ordinary food intake could supply.
:::

Keep Harvey's historical lower bound distinct from a modern physiological example. In a modern clinic, we define **cardiac output** (CO) as the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute—the product of **heart rate** (HR, beats per minute) and **stroke volume** (SV, millilitres ejected per beat):

:::eq
Cardiac output (CO) = Heart rate (HR) × Stroke volume (SV)
:::

Using a chosen modern resting adult illustration—say HR = 70 beats min⁻¹ and SV = 60 mL beat⁻¹ (or 70 mL beat⁻¹ in a slightly larger adult):

- At 70 beats min⁻¹ × 60 mL beat⁻¹, one ventricle pumps 4,200 mL min⁻¹ = **4.20 L min⁻¹**, or **252 L h⁻¹** (at 70 mL beat⁻¹, **4.90 L min⁻¹** or **294 L h⁻¹**).
- Because a typical adult body contains only about **5 L of blood in total**, the heart pumps the entire blood volume around the body roughly once every minute at rest.

Notice both the power and the boundary of that calculation. The arithmetic rules out any model in which every beat pumps freshly manufactured blood that disappears into the tissues. It does **not** mean that Galen himself claimed the liver made a person's body weight of blood every hour (Galen assumed a slow ebb and trickle, not rapid complete ejection at every beat). Nor can multiplication by itself tell us which vessels carry blood outward, which bring it back, or how the two sets of vessels connect.

To establish **one-way direction**, Harvey combined three lines of seventeenth-century experimental and anatomical evidence in Chapters X, XI and XIII of *De Motu Cordis* (historical observations and surgical/ligature tests that are never to be reproduced on animals or with tight bands at home):

1. **Pinching vessels near the heart (Chapter X):** In a live snake, pinching the **vena cava** just before it enters the heart quickly empties the vessel segment between the pinch and the heart as the heart beats on. Pinching the **artery (aorta)** just after it leaves the heart makes the segment between the heart and the pinch swell up with blood. Therefore blood enters the heart from the veins and leaves through the arteries.
2. **Tight versus medium arm bandages (Chapter XI):** Tying a **very tight** ligature around a human upper arm compresses both the deep high-pressure arteries and the surface veins: below the band, the radial pulse disappears and the hand stays pale and cool. Loosening the band to **medium tightness** allows high-pressure arterial blood to force its way past into the arm while still blocking the low-pressure veins: now the hand flushes and the surface veins **below** the ligature swell up, while the veins above the ligature stay flat. Blood must be entering the limb deep in the arteries and returning under pressure towards the chest in the veins.
3. **Pressing forearm vein valves (Chapter XIII):** Using the venous pockets (**valves**) described by his Padua teacher Hieronymus Fabricius (1603), Harvey showed that pressing a finger on a swollen forearm vein at a lower valve node H (near the hand) and stroking a second finger upward towards the shoulder past the next valve O leaves the segment H–O flat and empty. Pushing downward against O from above cannot force blood backward past the valve toward the wrist; lifting the lower finger at H immediately refills the segment from the hand side towards the heart.

## 8.3 The heart and two connected circuits

Why do mammals and birds have a **four-chambered heart** divided down the middle by a solid muscular wall, rather than a single two-chambered pump like a bony fish?

Compare the two designs honestly—without treating a fish as a defective human. In a fish's ==single circulation==, the heart has one main atrium and one ventricle that pumps deoxygenated blood forward to the **gill capillaries**. Blood picks up oxygen across the thin gill lamellae, and then flows **directly on** into the systemic capillaries of the body before returning to the heart, passing through the heart **once** for each complete circuit of the body. Because the narrow gill capillaries offer resistance, blood pressure drops sharply across the gills (`Heart → Gills (P drops) → Body tissues (low P) → Heart`). That lower systemic pressure suits a buoyant aquatic ectotherm whose body is supported by water and whose metabolic oxygen demand is lower than a mammal's.

:::note Single circulation
A circulatory plan (as in bony fish) in which blood passes through the heart once for each complete circuit of the body, flowing from heart to gill capillaries and directly on to body capillaries before returning to the heart.
:::

A mammal or bird, however, maintains a warm body and high aerobic metabolic rates on land against gravity. If a single pump pushed blood through the delicate 0.5 µm alveolar capillaries of the lungs at the high pressure needed to drive rapid flow to the brain, kidneys and muscles (~120 mmHg), fluid would rapidly leak out into the air sacs and flood the lungs. If, instead, the single pump operated at a gentle lung-safe pressure (~25 mmHg), systemic blood flow after leaving the lungs would be too sluggish to supply active terrestrial tissues.

Mammals solve this dual-pressure dilemma with a ==double circulation==: two connected circuits arranged **in series** and powered by the two sides of one four-chambered heart:

1. **The pulmonary circulation (right side of heart → lungs → left side of heart):** The **right ventricle** pumps deoxygenated blood at **lower pressure** (peak systolic around 25 mmHg) through the **pulmonary artery** to the **lungs**, protecting the delicate alveolar capillaries while O₂ loads and CO₂ unloads. Oxygenated blood returns via the **pulmonary veins** to the **left atrium**.
2. **The systemic circulation (left side of heart → body organs → right side of heart):** The **left ventricle** re-pressurises that freshly oxygenated blood to **high pressure** (peak systolic around 120 mmHg) and pumps it into the **aorta** to supply the brain, heart muscle, liver, gut, kidneys and limbs at high flow rates before deoxygenated blood returns via the **venae cavae** to the **right atrium**.
3. In one complete journey around the body, blood passes through the heart **twice**—once through the right side and once through the left side.

:::note Double circulation
A circulatory plan (as in mammals) in which blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body: a low-pressure pulmonary circuit to the lungs and a high-pressure systemic circuit to the rest of the body.
:::

:::predict
In a healthy resting person with an intact septum, the left ventricular muscular wall is about three times thicker than the right ventricular wall. Which statement correctly compares what the two ventricles pump each minute?
- The left ventricle pumps three times as many litres per minute as the right ventricle because its wall is three times thicker
- Both ventricles pump the same volume per minute in series, but the left ventricle generates ~5 times higher systolic pressure to overcome the resistance of the whole-body systemic circuit
- The left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood because its thick muscular wall adds oxygen molecules to the blood
???
The second. Because the pulmonary and systemic circuits are connected in series in a closed loop, both ventricles must pump the same steady volume per minute (Q_p = Q_s, e.g. 4.2 L min⁻¹); otherwise blood would pile up in one circuit. The left ventricle's thicker muscle generates much higher pressure (~120 mmHg vs ~25 mmHg), while oxygenation happens in the lungs, never in the heart wall.
:::

:::evidence Series conservation and chamber pressures | Cardiac catheterisation
Direct pressure measurements inside living human heart chambers show that while the right and left ventricles eject equal stroke volumes in steady state (~60–70 mL per beat), peak systolic pressure is only about **25 mmHg** (25/8 mmHg in the pulmonary artery) on the right side versus **120 mmHg** (120/80 mmHg in the aorta) on the left side.

In a series loop without a shunt, volume conservation requires pulmonary blood flow (Q_p) to equal systemic blood flow (Q_s) averaged over time. Wall thickness matches the mechanical work (ΔP × V) required of each chamber, not the volume of blood passing through it or its oxygen content.
:::

Pressure falls as blood flows through resistance. Much of the systemic fall occurs across arterioles before blood reaches capillaries. A ventricular peak of 120 mmHg therefore does not mean each tissue capillary receives 120 mmHg. Pulmonary pressure also falls along its vessels; the lower-pressure circuit limits fluid filtration into lung tissue.

Note the named organ vessels required by 0610: pulmonary artery and vein, aorta, vena cava, hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein, hepatic vein, and renal artery and vein.

Look closely at the named vessels in the systemic circuit so you never fall for the trap that “all arteries carry oxygenated blood”:

- **Arteries** are defined by **direction**: they always carry blood **away from the heart** (the **pulmonary artery** carries **deoxygenated** blood from the right ventricle to the lungs; the **aorta**, **hepatic artery** to the liver, and **renal arteries** to the kidneys carry oxygenated blood away from the left ventricle).
- **Veins** always carry blood **towards the heart** (the **pulmonary veins** carry **oxygenated** blood from the lungs to the left atrium; the **hepatic veins** from the liver, **renal veins** from the kidneys, and **superior/inferior venae cavae** carry deoxygenated blood back to the right atrium).
- The **hepatic portal vein** (which we met in Chapter 7) is a special vein connecting two capillary beds: it carries nutrient-rich, partially deoxygenated blood from the **small intestine capillaries directly to the liver**, before the **hepatic vein** returns blood from the liver to the inferior vena cava.

### Trace the route, then compare the simultaneous pumps

Now examine the internal architecture of the mammalian heart. When you look at a front-facing anatomical diagram of the chest, remember that it shows the **patient's own right and left**: the **right atrium and right ventricle are on the viewer's left**, and the **left atrium and left ventricle are on the viewer's right**.

Trace one red blood cell through all four chambers and four valves in sequence. This follows its journey over time; the right and left atria contract together, then the right and left ventricles contract together. The two sides do not wait to take turns:

1. **Venae cavae → Right atrium (RA):** Deoxygenated blood from the head, arms and trunk enters the thin-walled **right atrium**.
2. **Right atrioventricular (tricuspid) valve → Right ventricle (RV):** When right atrial pressure exceeds right ventricular pressure, the **tricuspid valve** is pushed open and blood fills the right ventricle.
3. **Right ventricle → Pulmonary semilunar valve → Pulmonary artery:** When the right ventricle contracts (**ventricular systole**), rising ventricular pressure snaps the **tricuspid valve shut** (preventing backflow into the right atrium; **tendons / chordae tendineae** stop the valve flaps from turning inside out) and pushes the **pulmonary semilunar valve** open, ejecting blood into the **pulmonary artery** towards the lungs.
4. **Lungs → Pulmonary veins → Left atrium (LA):** After gas exchange in the alveolar capillaries, oxygenated blood returns through the **pulmonary veins** into the **left atrium**.
5. **Left atrioventricular (bicuspid / mitral) valve → Left ventricle (LV):** When left atrial pressure exceeds left ventricular pressure, the **bicuspid (mitral) valve** opens and blood fills the **left ventricle**.
6. **Left ventricle → Aortic semilunar valve → Aorta:** When the thick-walled left ventricle contracts, rising ventricular pressure (~120 mmHg) slams the **bicuspid valve shut** and forces the **aortic semilunar valve** open, ejecting oxygenated blood into the **aorta**. When the ventricles relax (**diastole**), ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure and the **pulmonary and aortic semilunar valves snap shut**, preventing arterial blood from falling back into the ventricles.

:::try Pause at the two pumps
Both ventricles pump 4.2 L each minute in a steady intact circuit. Does equal flow require equal pressure?

No. The left ventricle generates a greater pressure difference to overcome the greater resistance of the systemic circuit. Otherwise blood would accumulate in one circuit.

Compare the muscular walls of the chambers:

- **Ventricles have much thicker muscular walls than atria**, because atria only need to push blood a few centimetres downward through open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles right below them, whereas ventricles must pump blood out into entire organ networks.
- **The left ventricle has a much thicker muscular wall (~12–15 mm) than the right ventricle (~4–5 mm)**, because the left ventricle must generate high pressure (~120 mmHg) to overcome the resistance of the long systemic circulation all over the body, whereas the right ventricle pumps through the short, low-resistance pulmonary circulation close to the heart at lower pressure (~25 mmHg).
- **The septum** is the muscular wall separating the right and left sides of the heart, preventing deoxygenated blood on the right from mixing with oxygenated blood on the left.

:::five The four-chambered double pump and its valves
? What is it?
Two muscular atria and two muscular ventricles separated by a septum, with four pressure-operated one-way valves connecting the low-pressure pulmonary circuit and high-pressure systemic circuit in series.
? Why is it called this?
Atrium is the Latin word for an entrance hall; ventricle (*ventriculus*) means a little belly; septum means an enclosing partition or fence.
? What problem did it solve?
It allows the two circuits to operate at different pressures while maintaining equal average flow and separating oxygenated from deoxygenated blood. The quoted 25 and 120 mmHg are ventricular systolic pressures, not pressures in every capillary.
? Why does it work?
Valves open whenever upstream pressure exceeds downstream pressure and snap shut when downstream pressure is higher; the left ventricle's thick myocardium generates systemic pressure while both ventricles pump equal volumes per minute in series.
? How does it help?
You can predict valve opening, valve closing sounds (“lub-dub”), chamber wall thicknesses and circuit flows from pressure differences rather than memorising disconnected facts.
?? How do we know?
Dissection reveals wall thicknesses and valve flaps; catheterisation and Doppler echocardiography measure pressures and flow directions; stethoscopes and ECGs record mechanical and electrical activity.
?? What it isn't
A pump that oxygenates blood inside its own left chambers, or two independent pumps moving different steady volumes per minute.
?? Where does it stop working?
If a valve leaks (regurgitation) or a gap remains in the septum (a septal defect), pressure differences drive abnormal shunting or backward flow that adds volume work to the heart.
:::

How is the heartbeat initiated, regulated and monitored? Heart muscle is **myogenic**—a small patch of specialised muscle cells in the wall of the right atrium, the **pacemaker (sinoatrial node, SAN)**, initiates regular electrical impulses on its own without needing a nerve impulse for every beat. During **physical activity**, however, working muscles produce more CO₂ and need more O₂ and glucose; the brain sends **autonomic nerve signals** and the adrenal glands release the hormone **adrenaline** into the blood, both acting on the pacemaker to **increase heart rate and stroke volume** (a preview of Chapter 9's nervous and hormonal coordination).

Clinicians and students can **monitor heart activity** in three complementary ways:

1. **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** skin electrodes record the electrical waves spreading across the atria and ventricles that trigger each contraction.
2. **Pulse rate:** each left ventricular ejection sends a pressure wave along elastic arterial walls, felt where an artery passes over bone at the wrist (radial artery) or neck (carotid artery).
3. **Listening to valve closing sounds:** a stethoscope hears two distinct sounds each cycle—“**lub**” when the **atrioventricular (tricuspid and bicuspid) valves close** at the start of ventricular systole, and “**dub**” when the **semilunar (pulmonary and aortic) valves close** at the start of ventricular diastole.

Even though gallons of blood rush through the heart's chambers every minute, the thick muscular wall of the heart (**myocardium**) cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion from the chamber lumens. Instead, the heart muscle has its own dedicated blood supply: the ==coronary arteries== branch off the base of the aorta and wrap over the outside of the heart wall, feeding a dense capillary bed inside the cardiac muscle.

:::note Coronary arteries
Arteries branching from the base of the aorta that supply oxygenated blood and glucose to the heart's own muscular wall (myocardium).
:::

In **coronary heart disease (CHD)**, fatty deposits (**atheromatous plaques** containing cholesterol) build up inside the lining of the coronary arteries, narrowing the lumen and restricting blood flow to part of the heart muscle. If a plaque ruptures and triggers a blood clot (thrombosis) that blocks a coronary branch, the downstream heart muscle cells are starved of oxygen, cannot respire aerobically to make ATP, and stop contracting—a myocardial infarction (heart attack). Epidemiological and clinical studies identify several **risk factors** for CHD: a **diet high in saturated fats** (and trans fats), **smoking** (nicotine and carbon monoxide damage vessel linings and reduce oxygen carriage), **lack of regular exercise**, **chronic stress**, **obesity**, **increasing age**, **biological sex**, and **genetic predisposition**. While age and genes cannot be changed, eating a balanced diet lower in saturated fat, not smoking, and taking regular physical exercise help reduce the risk.

:::careful
> “The left ventricle has a thicker wall because it contains more blood, and the heart muscle absorbs its oxygen straight from the blood inside the left ventricle.”

Both ventricles pump the same stroke volume (~60–70 mL) and the same cardiac output per minute in series; the left ventricular wall is thicker because it must generate ~5 times higher **pressure** (~120 mmHg vs ~25 mmHg) to drive blood through the whole body. And the ventricular wall is far too thick for oxygen to diffuse from the chamber lumen: cardiac muscle depends on the **coronary arteries** branching from the aorta.
:::

Vessel names depend on **direction relative to the heart**, not oxygen content: the **pulmonary vein** is a vein because it carries blood **towards the heart** (from the lungs into the left atrium), even though that blood is oxygenated. The “lub-dub” sounds heard through a stethoscope are caused by the **closing of the heart valves** as pressure differences reverse—“lub” when the atrioventricular (tricuspid and bicuspid) valves snap shut at the start of ventricular contraction, and “dub” when the semilunar valves snap shut as the ventricles relax.

## 8.4 Vessels, capillaries, tissue fluid and blood as a multi-job tissue

Once the left ventricle ejects blood into the aorta, the blood travels through a branching tree of vessels—**arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins**—before returning to the heart. Why doesn't the body use the same kind of pipe everywhere? Because three different mechanical jobs require three different wall designs.

:::predict
A single capillary is microscopic (about 8 µm in internal diameter, barely wide enough for one red blood cell), whereas the aorta is about 2.5 cm wide. When blood leaves the aorta and branches into the body's capillary beds, does the blood speed up like water squirting out of a pinched garden hose, or slow down to a crawl?
- It speeds up dramatically because each capillary is much narrower than the aorta
- It slows down ~1,000-fold because billions of capillaries in parallel have a combined cross-sectional area roughly 1,000 times larger than the single aorta
- It keeps the exact same linear speed (in mm/s) in every vessel because cardiac output is constant
???
The second. Pinched-hose intuition applies only when the **entire flow** is forced through **one** narrow nozzle. In the body, the single aorta (A ≈ 4.5 cm²) branches into billions of capillaries arranged in parallel whose **combined cross-sectional area** is roughly 4,500 cm². By conservation of volume flow (v = Q / A_total), a ~1,000-fold increase in total area reduces average linear speed ~1,000-fold (from ~180 mm s⁻¹ to ~0.2 mm s⁻¹), giving time for diffusion across the capillary wall.
:::

:::evidence Continuity across a branching vascular tree | Area and velocity
For an incompressible fluid moving at volume flow rate Q (for example Q = 4.9 L min⁻¹ ≈ 81.7 cm³ s⁻¹) through a level of the vascular tree whose parallel branches have combined cross-sectional area A_total, average linear speed is v = Q / A_total:

- **Single aorta (A_total ≈ 4.5 cm²):** v ≈ 81.7 / 4.5 ≈ **181 mm s⁻¹** (18.1 cm s⁻¹).
- **Systemic capillary beds (A_total ≈ 4,500 cm²):** v ≈ 81.7 / 4,500 ≈ **0.18 mm s⁻¹**—about a thousand times slower, so a red blood cell spends roughly 2.8 seconds traversing a chosen 0.5 mm capillary in this illustrative calculation.
- **Venae cavae (A_total ≈ 18 cm²):** as venules and veins merge back into two large trunks, A_total narrows again and blood accelerates back up to **~45 mm s⁻¹**.
:::

Compare how structure matches function across the three main vessel types (plus the smaller **arterioles** and **venules** that connect them):

1. ==Arteries== carry blood **away from the heart** at **high, pulsatile pressure** (around 120/80 mmHg in large systemic arteries). They have **thick outer and middle walls** packed with **smooth muscle** and **elastic fibres**, and a **relatively narrow lumen** (with no valves except the semilunar valves at the heart exit). When a ventricular beat surges into an artery, the **elastic fibres stretch** to absorb the pressure peak and **recoil** during diastole to keep blood flowing smoothly onward. In smaller **arterioles**, rings of smooth muscle contract (**vasoconstriction**) or relax (**vasodilation**) to adjust how much blood enters each organ's capillary bed.
2. ==Capillaries== link arterioles to venules inside every tissue. Their wall is **one endothelial cell thick** (supported only by a thin basement membrane, with no muscle or elastic layer) and their **microscopic lumen** (~8 µm) forces red blood cells to pass in **single file** pressed right against the wall. That ultra-short diffusion distance, enormous total surface area, and slow flow (~0.2 mm s⁻¹) allow rapid exchange of O₂, CO₂, glucose, amino acids, ions and water.
3. ==Veins== carry blood **back towards the heart** at **low pressure** (~5–15 mmHg), after pressure has dropped across the high-resistance arterioles and capillary beds. They have **thinner walls** with much less muscle and elastic tissue, a **wide lumen** that offers **low resistance** to flow, and, in many peripheral veins, **one-way pocket valves** along their length that prevent low-pressure blood from flowing backward, assisted by the squeezing of surrounding **skeletal muscles** when you walk or move.

:::note Arteries
Blood vessels carrying blood away from the heart. Their muscular and elastic walls withstand pressure; large systemic arteries have higher pressure than pulmonary arteries.
:::

:::note Capillaries
Microscopic blood vessels with walls one cell thick that link arterioles and venules, allowing exchange of materials between blood and tissue fluid.
:::

:::note Veins
Blood vessels returning blood towards the heart, usually at lower pressure than arteries. Many peripheral veins contain valves that limit backflow.
:::

How do glucose and oxygen actually get from a capillary into a muscle cell or liver cell that sits a couple of cell widths away from the vessel? They travel through an intermediate bath: ==tissue fluid== (interstitial fluid) that surrounds all the body's cells:

- **Across the capillary barrier**, blood pressure tends to drive fluid outward, while the barrier’s properties and plasma-protein osmotic effects oppose filtration. In many ordinary tissue beds there is a small **net outward filtration in steady conditions**, including towards the venular side. Lymphatic return is therefore central to keeping fluid from accumulating around cells.
- **Between blood and tissue cells**, oxygen and other substances diffuse along their own gradients. Oxygen diffusion is distinct from water filtering under pressure. Tissue fluid provides the short route between capillaries and cells; cells take up supplied material and release products such as CO₂.
- **Lymphatic return** carries net filtered fluid and escaped proteins back to blood. Blind-ended lymphatic capillaries collect fluid, now called **lymph**. Larger valved vessels pass through **lymph nodes**, containing immune cells, and eventually join veins near the heart.
- **Conditions matter.** Some specialised capillaries absorb fluid, and a sudden fall in pressure can cause transient absorption. Fluid exchange cannot be calculated by subtracting two universal pressure numbers; the endothelial surface barrier and surrounding conditions matter too.

### One fluid tissue, several kinds of work

:::note Tissue fluid
The watery fluid formed from blood plasma at capillaries that bathes body cells, supplying them with oxygen and nutrients and receiving carbon dioxide and other metabolic wastes.
:::

:::recall 2
In Chapter 2 we saw that a mature mammalian red blood cell has a biconcave disc shape and lacks a nucleus. How do both features suit its job in a capillary?
???
The biconcave disc shape (thinner in the centre than at the rim) provides a larger surface-area-to-volume ratio than a sphere of the same volume, shortening the diffusion distance for oxygen entering and leaving every part of the cell, and lets the cell flex through narrow 8 µm capillaries. Lacking a nucleus (and mitochondria) leaves more internal room for millions of **haemoglobin** molecules and ensures the red blood cell does not consume the oxygen it is transporting.
:::

Finally, inspect **blood** itself—a specialised liquid tissue composed of four main parts suspended or dissolved together:

1. **Plasma (~55% of blood volume):** the straw-coloured liquid matrix (mostly water) that transports **blood cells** (red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets), dissolved **mineral ions** (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻), soluble **nutrients** (glucose, amino acids), **urea** (from the liver to the kidneys for excretion), **hormones** (such as adrenaline and insulin), **plasma proteins** (albumin, antibodies, soluble fibrinogen), **carbon dioxide** (mainly as hydrogencarbonate ions HCO₃⁻ in plasma), and **heat**.
2. **Red blood cells (erythrocytes):** flexible **biconcave discs** without a nucleus, packed with the iron-containing protein ==haemoglobin==. In the high-P_O₂ capillaries of the lungs, haemoglobin binds oxygen reversibly to form bright red **oxyhaemoglobin** (Hb + 4O₂ ⇌ Hb(O₂)₄); in the lower-P_O₂ capillaries of respiring tissues, oxyhaemoglobin releases oxygen to diffuse into tissue fluid and cells.
3. **White blood cells (leukocytes):** nucleated immune cells that defend against pathogens, distinguishable under a microscope by their nuclei and actions. The pictured **neutrophil**, one kind of **phagocyte**, has a **lobed (multi-lobed) nucleus** and granular cytoplasm; phagocytes enter tissues and carry out **phagocytosis**—engulfing bacteria and cellular debris into a vacuole and digesting them with enzymes. **Lymphocytes** have a **large round nucleus** that nearly fills the cell with only a thin rim of clear cytoplasm; **B lymphocytes** can develop into plasma cells that secrete specific **antibodies**; memory B cells support later responses. Other lymphocytes, including T cells, have different roles. Shape alone does not identify all these functions.
4. **Platelets (thrombocytes):** tiny, non-nucleated **cell fragments** that trigger **blood clotting** when a vessel wall is cut. Exposed collagen and damaged tissue activate platelets to form a plug and release clotting factors that catalyse the conversion of the **soluble** plasma protein **fibrinogen** into long, sticky threads of **insoluble fibrin**. The fibrin mesh traps red blood cells to form a clot (which dries into a scab), **preventing further blood loss** and **blocking the entry of pathogens** through the wound.

:::note Haemoglobin
The iron-containing protein in red blood cells that binds oxygen reversibly in the lungs (forming oxyhaemoglobin) and releases it in respiring tissues.
:::

:::fig c8_capillary_bed_blood() | Follow net fluid filtration, molecular diffusion and lymphatic return (top), alongside enlarged views of red blood cells, neutrophils (lobed nucleus), lymphocytes (large round nucleus), platelets and the fibrinogen-to-fibrin clotting mesh (bottom).

:::five Capillary exchange, lymph and the four jobs of blood
? What is it?
Exchange across thin capillary barriers, including molecular diffusion and pressure-dependent fluid filtration, paired with lymphatic return and the division of labour among plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
? Why is it called this?
Capillary comes from Latin *capillaris* (of hair); plasma from Greek for something moulded; erythrocyte/leukocyte from Greek *erythros* (red) and *leukos* (white) plus *kytos* (hollow vessel/cell); phagocyte from *phagein* (to eat).
? What problem did it solve?
It explains how nutrients and oxygen reach cells that do not touch a major artery, why tissues do not swell up with trapped fluid, and how one liquid tissue can carry gases, fight infection and seal wounds.
? Why does it work?
Continuity slows blood in the vast parallel capillary bed; barrier properties and pressure govern fluid filtration while lymphatics return net filtered fluid; haemoglobin, leukocytes and fibrinogen each handle a distinct chemical job.
? How does it help?
You can distinguish blood plasma from tissue fluid and lymph (which lack red blood cells and most large plasma proteins), and identify blood cells on a photomicrograph by their shape and nucleus.
?? How do we know?
Micro-cannulation measures capillary pressures; microscopy distinguishes biconcave red blood cells, lobed-nucleus neutrophils and round-nucleus lymphocytes; biochemical assays track soluble fibrinogen turning into insoluble fibrin.
?? What it isn't
Red blood cells crawling out into healthy tissue fluid, veins pumping blood by active muscular beats, or platelets being complete nucleated cells.
?? Where does it stop working?
If venous pressure rises too high, plasma proteins are lost in severe malnutrition, or lymphatic vessels are blocked, fluid filtration exceeds return and tissue swells (**oedema**).
:::

:::careful
> “Tissue fluid is just whole blood that leaked out of broken capillaries, and blood clots because red blood cells dry up in the air.”

Healthy capillaries are not torn open: water and small solutes filter through microscopic gaps between endothelial cells, while **red blood cells and large plasma proteins stay inside the capillary**. And clotting is an enzyme-controlled chemical cascade triggered by **platelets**, converting **soluble fibrinogen** into an **insoluble fibrin mesh** that traps red blood cells even while wet.
:::

## A hole between the ventricles

:::fig c8_septal_defect_case() | Compare an intact ventricular septum (left) with an uncomplicated post-natal ventricular septal defect (right). Because left ventricular systolic pressure (~115–120 mmHg) is much higher than right ventricular pressure (~25–35 mmHg), oxygenated blood jets left-to-right into the right ventricle and recycles through the lungs.

:::case The child with a hole in the septum
This is a fictional reasoning case, not clinical advice about any individual patient. A paediatric cardiologist uses echocardiography to examine a child who becomes breathless during playground games and has a loud harsh sound (a **systolic murmur**) between the normal “lub” and “dub” heart sounds. The scan shows an opening large enough to produce the stipulated extra pulmonary flow and symptoms in the muscular wall between the left and right ventricles—an uncomplicated post-natal **ventricular septal defect (VSD)**—while all four heart valves are normal.

One pupil says: “Because the right ventricle holds deoxygenated blood, a hole in the septum must send dark deoxygenated blood straight from the right ventricle into the left ventricle and aorta, turning the child's lips blue immediately.”

A second pupil says: “No blood will cross the hole at all, because both ventricles contract at the exact same moment and cancel each other out.”

A third pupil says: “Only the systemic circuit is affected; the lungs receive the exact same blood flow per minute as before (Q_p = Q_s).”

Use the pressures of the two ventricles to correct all three claims. Which way does blood actually shunt across the defect during ventricular systole, what happens to pulmonary blood flow (Q_p) relative to systemic blood flow (Q_s), and why does the child feel breathless on exertion?
???
Fluid flows from **higher pressure to lower pressure**, even when two chambers contract at the same time. During post-natal ventricular systole, the thick-walled **left ventricle** generates roughly **115–120 mmHg**, whereas the thinner-walled **right ventricle** generates only about **25–35 mmHg**.

1. **Why the first and second pupils are wrong:** Because left ventricular pressure is more than three times right ventricular pressure during systole, the pressure difference drives a **left-to-right shunt**: already-oxygenated blood from the left ventricle jets across the septal defect **into the right ventricle**, not from right to left into the aorta. (This case assumes lower right-sided pressure. Other haemodynamic situations can change shunt direction.) The turbulent high-velocity jet from left to right during contraction produces the **systolic murmur** heard between “lub” and “dub”.
2. **Why the third pupil is wrong (Q_p > Q_s):** The right ventricle now receives both the normal venous return from the venae cavae **plus** the shunted oxygenated blood from the left ventricle. It therefore pumps an abnormally large volume into the **pulmonary artery and lungs** (Q_p > Q_s, for the explorer’s chosen example, 6.72 L min⁻¹ through the lungs while 4.2 L min⁻¹ enters the aorta; this assumed ratio is not predicted from every defect’s diameter).
3. **Why exertion causes breathlessness:** Recycling extra blood through the pulmonary circuit overloads the pulmonary capillaries and increases the work of the ventricles, while leaving less reserve capacity to boost systemic oxygen delivery to working muscles during exercise.
:::
