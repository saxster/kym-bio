In Chapter 9 you met the body's nervous and hormonal communication systems. Electrical impulses travel along neurones, with chemical messages relaying signals across the synapses described there. Hormones travel in blood plasma and affect cells with suitable receptor proteins.

Now put that communication network to work on a single May day in Mumbai. At noon you step out onto a Dadar pavement where the shade thermometer reads 38 °C—one degree hotter than your own internal organs—and the Arabian Sea breeze is thick with water vapour. At lunch you eat a bowl of sweet Alphonso *aamras* after four hours without food, pouring dozens of grams of glucose through your hepatic portal vein, followed by *dal tadka* rich in amino acids that your body has no tank to store. On the way home you ride a crowded local train where thousands of people breathe, cough and touch the same steel grab-handles.

Without regulation, internal heat production, changing supplies and continuing waste production would disturb the liquid around your cells. Yet your core temperature holds steady near 37.0 °C, your fasting blood glucose stays close to 5.0 mmol/L, your kidneys normally recover nearly all filtered glucose while concentrating urea over sixty-fold into urine, and your immune system remembers viruses you met years ago. This concluding chapter of Volume III follows the three connected survival systems that hold that line: **homeostasis**, **excretion**, and **disease defence and immunity**.

## 10.1 The thermostat and the pantry: homeostasis, negative feedback, and blood glucose

:::scene Paris, 1859–1865, and Toronto, January 1922

Why do thirty-seven trillion human cells care so much about keeping the liquid around them steady? A single-celled amoeba in a huge pond can let the pond absorb its waste and buffer its temperature. Your brain neurones, heart muscle fibres and kidney cells do not live in an open pond; as the French physiologist Claude Bernard pointed out in Paris between 1859 and 1865, multicellular animals live inside an enclosed liquid **internal environment** (*milieu intérieur*)—the blood plasma and **tissue fluid** you traced in Chapter 8.

If the concentration of glucose, water or mineral ions in that internal bath drifts too far, or if its temperature shifts by more than a few degrees, cellular functions can be disrupted, with some tissues more vulnerable than others:

- If blood plasma and tissue fluid become **too concentrated** (too little water or too many dissolved solutes, lowering their **water potential**), water leaves your cells by **osmosis** (Chapter 3) and the cells shrink and dehydrate. If plasma becomes **too dilute** (too high a water potential), water enters cells by osmosis and brain cells swell inside the rigid skull.
- If blood **glucose concentration** falls too low (**hypoglycaemia**, below ~3.0 mmol/L), brain neurones—which rely almost entirely on glucose for aerobic respiration to recharge **ATP** (Chapters 1 and 6)—can receive too little fuel, leading to symptoms ranging from dizziness and confusion to coma in severe cases. If blood glucose stays far too high (**hyperglycaemia**), it lowers the water potential of blood and kidney filtrate, drawing water out of cells and into urine.
- If **core body temperature** falls far below 37 °C, particles move with lower average kinetic energy so enzyme–substrate collisions slow down; severe overheating disrupts cellular processes and can damage proteins and membranes. Damage depends on temperature, duration and the particular tissue; proteins do not all denature at one temperature (Chapter 4).

In 1926 the physiologist Walter B. Cannon coined a single word for the active maintenance of this steady internal state: ==homeostasis== (from Greek *homoios*, meaning "similar, with room for small variation", and *stasis*, "standing condition").

:::note Homeostasis
The maintenance of a constant internal environment within narrow limits around a norm or **set point** (such as core body temperature near 37 °C, blood glucose concentration near 4.0–5.5 mmol/L, and blood water potential), despite changes in the external environment or the body's activity.
:::

Notice why Cannon chose *homoio-* ("similar") rather than *homo-* ("frozen at an identical number"). A living body never freezes its blood glucose at 5.000 mmol/L or its temperature at 37.000 °C like a painted line. Instead, it uses ==negative feedback== to correct every drift away from the **set point**:

1. **Set point (norm):** the target value or narrow normal range for an internal variable (for example, fasting blood plasma glucose around **5.0 mmol/L**, within a normal fasting range of **4.0–5.5 mmol/L**, or **72–99 mg/dL**).
2. **Stimulus:** a change above or below that set point (such as blood glucose rising after a mango meal, or falling during an afternoon cricket match).
3. **Receptor (sensor):** cells that detect the stimulus and measure how far the variable has drifted from the set point.
4. **Coordinator (control centre):** a gland or nervous centre (such as the pancreas islets or the brain's hypothalamus) that compares the receptor signal against the set point and sends out an instruction (via a hormone in blood or an electrical impulse along a motor neurone).
5. **Effector:** a muscle or gland/organ (such as liver cells, sweat glands, or smooth muscle in skin arterioles) that carries out a **corrective response in the opposite direction** to the stimulus, bringing the internal condition back toward the set point. Once the set point is restored, the corrective signal is switched down so the correction does not overshoot endlessly.

:::note Negative feedback
A homeostatic control mechanism in which any change away from a **set point** (norm) is detected by a receptor and triggers an effector response that **opposes (reverses) the change**, returning the internal condition toward the set point.
:::

:::predict
Compare a homeostatic **negative feedback** loop with a **positive feedback** loop. After you eat a sweet mango and your blood glucose rises from 5.0 mmol/L to 8.5 mmol/L, what happens under negative feedback versus what would happen if blood glucose were controlled by positive feedback?
- Under negative feedback, effectors push blood glucose higher to 12.0 mmol/L; under positive feedback, effectors bring it back to 5.0 mmol/L
- Under negative feedback, the pancreas and liver oppose the rise by storing glucose as glycogen so blood glucose returns toward 5.0 mmol/L; under positive feedback, a rise in blood glucose would trigger further glucose release into the blood, driving a runaway climb away from the set point
- Negative feedback means only decreases are allowed, whereas positive feedback means only increases are allowed
???
The second choice. In engineering and physiology, "negative" does not mean "bad" or "downward only"—it means the corrective action has the **opposite sign** (`−`) to the error from the set point. When blood glucose rises **above** 5.0 mmol/L, negative feedback pushes it **down**; when blood glucose falls **below** 5.0 mmol/L, negative feedback pushes it **up**. A **positive feedback** loop amplifies whatever change just happened (like a microphone held too close to a speaker), which drives a system further away from stability rather than maintaining homeostasis.
:::

### Follow one rise, then one fall

:::recall 3
If glucose falls, must negative feedback make it fall further?
???
No. Negative feedback opposes the disturbance; low glucose calls for a response that raises it.
:::

Trace how the **pancreas** and the **liver** work together as a two-way negative-feedback partnership to control **blood glucose concentration**:

- **Route 1 · When blood glucose rises above the set point (after a carbohydrate meal):**
  Digested starch and sugars from your meal are absorbed across the small intestine villi into capillaries that join the **hepatic portal vein** leading directly to the **liver** (Chapter 7). As blood glucose concentration climbs above the set point (say, from 5.0 mmol/L to 8.5 mmol/L), endocrine clusters inside the **pancreas** called the **islets of Langerhans** detect the rise. Their **beta (β) cells** secrete the protein hormone ==insulin== directly into the blood plasma (while suppressing glucagon secretion). Carried in the bloodstream, insulin binds to complementary receptor proteins on the cell membranes of **liver cells** and **skeletal muscle cells**, causing them to:

    1. **Increase glucose uptake in skeletal muscle** through regulated transport proteins; in the liver, favour glucose processing and storage while reducing glucose production,
    2. **Increase the rate of cellular respiration** of glucose, and
    3. **Activate enzymes that link soluble glucose molecules into long, branched chains of insoluble ==glycogen==** stored inside the liver and muscle cells (**glucose → glycogen**).
  As liver and muscle cells lock glucose away as glycogen, blood glucose concentration falls back toward the **5.0 mmol/L set point**, which removes the stimulus on the beta cells and slows insulin secretion.

- **Route 2 · When blood glucose falls below the set point (between meals, overnight, or during exercise):**
  As respiring brain and muscle cells consume glucose from the blood and plasma glucose dips below the set point (say, to 3.5 mmol/L), the **alpha (α) cells** in the pancreatic islets of Langerhans detect the fall and secrete a second protein hormone, ==glucagon==, into the blood (while insulin secretion drops). Glucagon binds to complementary receptors on **liver cells** and activates enzymes that **break down stored insoluble glycogen back into soluble glucose** (**glycogen → glucose**, alongside building glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors such as glycerol and amino-acid carbon skeletons). The liver releases that free glucose into the **hepatic vein** and general circulation, raising blood glucose concentration back toward the **5.0 mmol/L set point**. (Remember from Chapter 9 that during sudden stress or exercise, **adrenaline** from the adrenal glands also stimulates the liver to break down glycogen into glucose for working muscles.)

:::note Insulin, glucagon, and glycogen
- **Insulin:** hormone secreted by the **pancreas** when blood glucose concentration rises above the set point; it causes the **liver** (and muscles) to take up glucose and convert **glucose into insoluble glycogen**, **decreasing** blood glucose concentration.
- **Glucagon:** hormone secreted by the **pancreas** when blood glucose concentration falls below the set point; it causes the **liver** to break down stored **glycogen into soluble glucose** and release it into the blood, **increasing** blood glucose concentration.
- **Glycogen:** the insoluble, branched polysaccharide storage form of glucose kept inside liver and muscle cells (joining many glucose units into glycogen greatly reduces their osmotic effect compared with keeping them all as free glucose).
:::

:::fig c10_negative_feedback_glucose() | The figure-of-eight negative-feedback loop that holds blood plasma glucose near its 5.0 mmol/L (4.0–5.5 mmol/L) set point. Top loop: when a meal raises blood glucose, pancreatic beta cells secrete insulin so liver and muscle cells take up glucose and convert it into stored glycogen. Bottom loop: when fasting or exercise lowers blood glucose, pancreatic alpha cells secrete glucagon so liver cells break down glycogen back into glucose.

What happens when the insulin half of that loop breaks? In **Type 1 diabetes**, the person's own immune system mistakenly destroys the insulin-secreting **beta cells** in the islets of Langerhans of the pancreas (usually beginning in childhood or adolescence). Without beta cells, the pancreas cannot secrete enough **insulin** when blood glucose rises after a meal:

- **After eating**, glucose absorbed from the gut pours into the blood, but without insulin's signal at cell-surface receptors, muscle glucose uptake and liver glucose handling are disrupted, including insufficient restraint of liver glucose production. Blood glucose concentration climbs to dangerously high levels (**hyperglycaemia**, often 14–25 mmol/L) and stays high for hours.
- **In the kidneys**, as you will measure quantitatively in Section 10.3 and the end-of-chapter case, the massive load of filtered glucose overwhelms the carrier proteins in the kidney tubules, so glucose spills into the urine (**glycosuria**). That dissolved glucose lowers the water potential of the urine, dragging extra water out of the body by osmosis and causing frequent, large-volume urination (**polyuria**), fierce **thirst**, dehydration, and rapid **weight loss** and fatigue as cells break down fats and proteins for energy instead.
- **Between meals or if too much insulin is injected**, because normal automatic beta/alpha islet feedback is disrupted, blood glucose can also swing dangerously low (**hypoglycaemia**).

:::evidence Toronto, January 1922: Frederick Banting, Charles Best, James Collip and Leonard Thompson | Clinical & biochemical proof
In the summer of 1921 at the University of Toronto, young surgeon **Frederick Banting** and physiology student **Charles Best**, working in J. J. R. Macleod's laboratory, began investigating extracts of the islet hormone **insulin** from animal pancreases. **James Collip joined in December 1921** and improved purification before the successful January treatment.

On **23 January 1922**, fourteen-year-old **Leonard Thompson**—who weighed only 29.5 kg (`65 lb`) at Toronto General Hospital with severe Type 1 diabetes—received Collip's purified insulin extract by injection. Within 24 hours, his blood glucose dropped from `520 mg/dL` (`28.9 mmol/L`) to `120 mg/dL` (`6.7 mmol/L`), his urinary glucose loss fell from over `100 g/day` to near zero, and his strength returned. Today, human insulin is manufactured by genetically engineered bacteria or yeast carrying the human insulin gene (Chapter 15).
:::

:::story banting-mahalanabis-lifelines

How is **Type 1 diabetes treated** day to day? Because the pancreas cannot make insulin, treatment replaces the missing negative-feedback signal manually through three linked steps (**0610 14.4.5**):

1. **Regular testing of blood glucose concentration** (using a finger-prick blood glucose meter or a continuous interstitial glucose sensor) so the person knows where their blood glucose sits relative to the set point before and after meals.
2. **Regular injections of insulin** (or delivery via a subcutaneous insulin pump) matched to carbohydrate intake—administered **by injection into subcutaneous tissue, never swallowed as a tablet**, because insulin is a **protein** hormone that would be hydrolysed into inactive peptides and amino acids by stomach **pepsin** and intestinal **trypsin** before it could reach the blood.
3. **Careful regulation of diet and physical exercise**—eating planned meals with steady, complex carbohydrates (which digest and absorb more gradually than pure sugars) and adjusting food or insulin around exercise so blood glucose neither spikes into hyperglycaemia nor drops into hypoglycaemia.

:::five Homeostasis and negative feedback around a set point
? What is it?
The maintenance of a nearly constant internal environment (blood plasma and tissue fluid) by control loops in which any drift away from a target **set point** triggers a corrective effector response that opposes the change.
? Why is it called this?
Walter Cannon coined *homeostasis* in 1926 from Greek *homoios* (similar, allowing small physiological oscillation) and *stasis* (condition). *Negative feedback* comes from control engineering, where the corrective output is subtracted from (`−`) the deviation.
? What problem did it solve?
Multicellular cells cannot survive wide swings in temperature (which slows or denatures enzymes), water potential (which shrinks or bursts cells by osmosis), or blood glucose (which starves brain cells or causes osmotic water loss).
? Why does it work?
Receptors monitor the internal variable continuously; when it rises above the set point, coordinators activate effectors that lower it (e.g. **insulin** signalling cells to convert **glucose → glycogen** in the liver), and when it falls below the set point, they activate effectors that raise it (**glucagon** signalling liver cells to convert **glycogen → glucose**).
? How does it help?
It lets a mammal walk from an air-conditioned room into 38 °C Mumbai sunshine or fast for twelve hours overnight while regulating the temperature and glucose supply around brain and heart cells within suitable ranges.
?? How do we know?
Removing or damaging the sensor/coordinator (such as loss of pancreatic beta cells in Type 1 diabetes) abolishes stability, and replacing the missing hormone (as in the insulin treatment of 1922) can restore control of blood glucose. The treatment restores a signal, rather than creating a new target value.
?? What it isn't
A frozen, unchanging number, or **positive feedback** (which amplifies a change instead of reversing it). Negative feedback opposes a disturbance; the response can approach its target smoothly or fluctuate around it.
?? Where does it stop working?
If the effector organ or hormone is missing (Type 1 diabetes) or if external stress exceeds the maximum capacity of the effectors (such as extreme heat combined with 90% humidity), the loop saturates and the internal state drifts into danger.
:::

:::careful
> "Glycogen and glucagon are just two spellings of the same word, and people with Type 1 diabetes can simply take insulin tablets after lunch."

Look closely at the endings of the three words—the similar names describe different things:

- **Glucose** (`-ose` = sugar) is the **soluble monosaccharide** carried in blood plasma.
- **Glycogen** (`glyco-gen` = sugar-former) is the **insoluble storage polysaccharide** kept inside liver and muscle cells.
- **Glucagon** (`gluca-gon` = glucose-driver) is the **protein hormone** secreted by pancreatic alpha cells when blood glucose is low (`glucagon` tells the liver to turn `glycogen` back into `glucose`).
And **insulin** is a folded **protein** hormone: if swallowed as a pill, stomach acid and protease enzymes (**pepsin** and **trypsin**, Chapter 7) would digest it into amino acids in the gut before it could ever reach the bloodstream. That is why insulin must be **injected**.
:::

## 10.2 38 °C in Dadar: skin structure, evaporative cooling, and thermoregulation

:::scene Outside Dadar railway station, Mumbai, 2:30 p.m. in May

Step out of the shade outside Dadar station at half past two in May. The weather screen reads **38 °C** with **65% relative humidity**. Your own heart, brain, liver and kidneys are sitting at a core temperature of **37.0 °C**.

Pause for a second and ask the question a physicist from Book 2 ([*The Moving World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-physics/), Chapter 8) would ask. Whenever two objects are at different temperatures, thermal energy flows spontaneously by **conduction, convection and radiation** from the **hotter** one to the **colder** one—never the other way around. When you stood in a 22 °C room this morning, your 37 °C body was warmer than the room, so your skin steadily gave out thermal energy to the air.

Now, at 38 °C on the Dadar pavement, the outside air is **hotter than your body**, and meanwhile your own cells are continuously generating another **100 to 200 watts** of internal thermal energy through **aerobic respiration** (Chapter 6). Dry conduction and convection are now pushing thermal energy *into* you from the street, not taking it away. How can your body possibly stay at **37.0 °C** while preventing dangerous heat accumulation?

The answer combines the physics of **latent heat of vaporisation** from Book 2 Chapter 8 with the negative-feedback machinery of mammalian **skin** and the **brain**.

First, inspect the anatomy of mammalian **skin** in Figure 10.2 (**0610 Topic 14.4.6**). Skin is not a simple plastic wrapper; it is a multi-layered homeostatic organ divided into three main zones:

1. **The epidermis (outer layer):** Tough, continually renewed layers of epithelial cells whose dead outer surface is packed with waterproof **keratin** protein, protecting against mechanical damage, water loss and pathogen entry, and pierced by tiny **sweat pores** and emerging **hairs**.
2. **The dermis (middle living layer):** Connective tissue housing five crucial structures for sensing and thermoregulation:
    - **Coiled sweat glands** deep in the dermis that draw water, sodium chloride (`NaCl`) and traces of urea from surrounding capillaries and secrete **sweat** up a narrow **sweat duct** to open onto the skin surface through a **sweat pore**.
    - **Hair follicles** holding each **hair**, with a small band of smooth muscle—the **hair erector muscle**—attached diagonally between the hair follicle and the upper dermis.
    - **Sensory receptors** (separate microscopic endings sensitive to **temperature rise**, **temperature fall**, **touch** and **pressure**) connected to myelinated **sensory neurones** that send electrical impulses to the central nervous system.
    - **A three-tier network of blood vessels:** muscular **arterioles** rising from deeper arteries, branching into dense **surface capillary loops** just beneath the epidermis, and draining into **venules** (with deeper **shunt vessels** linking arterioles to venules).
3. **Subcutaneous fatty tissue (adipose layer beneath the dermis):** A layer of cells packed with lipid droplets that acts as both a long-term chemical energy store and a permanent **thermal insulator** (fat and trapped tissue have low thermal conductivity compared with muscle and blood, slowing heat transfer between the deep body core and the outside world).

Left: when core temperature rises above 37.0 °C, arterioles supplying skin surface capillaries vasodilate so more warm blood flows near the surface, sweat glands secrete sweat whose evaporation absorbs latent heat (2,420 J/g), and hair erector muscles relax. Right: when core temperature falls below 37.0 °C, skin arterioles vasoconstrict, sweating stops, hair erector muscles contract to stand hairs upright, and skeletal muscles shiver.

How does the **brain** coordinate these skin structures to hold core temperature at **37.0 °C**?

Deep inside the base of the brain sits the **hypothalamus**—the body's thermoregulatory control centre. It receives two streams of temperature data at the same time:

- **Internal thermoreceptors inside the hypothalamus itself** monitor the temperature of the **blood plasma** flowing directly through the brain (the true deep core temperature), and
- **Temperature receptors in the skin** send electrical impulses along **sensory neurones** warning the brain immediately when you step into hot sunshine or a cold room, even before your deep blood temperature has had time to shift.

Compare the two negative-feedback responses orchestrated by the brain via motor neurones:

- **When core temperature rises above the 37.0 °C set point (or skin enters hot surroundings):**
    1. **Vasodilation of arterioles supplying skin surface capillaries:** The smooth muscle in the walls of the **arterioles** leading to the skin surface capillaries **relaxes**, widening the arteriole lumen (**vasodilation**). Because the arteriole doorway is wider, **much more warm blood flows through the surface capillary loops** close to the epidermis (giving flushed skin), increasing thermal energy transfer from the blood to the surroundings by **radiation, conduction and convection** whenever the air is cooler than the skin.
    2. **Sweating and evaporative cooling:** Motor impulses stimulate coiled **sweat glands** to secrete liquid **sweat** (mostly water with dissolved salts and a trace of urea) onto the surface of the epidermis. As the fastest-moving water molecules break free from the liquid film and escape into the air as water vapour (**evaporation**), each gram of evaporated water carries away about **2,420 joules (2.42 kJ)** of thermal energy—the **latent heat of vaporisation** of water at body temperature. That lowers the average kinetic energy of the molecules left behind, cooling the skin and the blood flowing through the surface capillary loops even when the outside air is 38 °C.
    3. **Hair erector muscles relax:** The **hair erector muscles** relax so hairs lie flat against the skin, trapping as little insulating air as possible.
- **When core temperature falls below the 37.0 °C set point (in cold surroundings):**
    1. **Vasoconstriction of arterioles supplying skin surface capillaries:** The smooth muscle in the walls of the **arterioles** supplying the surface capillaries **contracts**, narrowing the arteriole lumen (**vasoconstriction**). Very little warm blood enters the surface capillary loops (most blood stays deep beneath the insulating **subcutaneous fatty tissue** via shunt pathways), minimising thermal energy loss from the skin surface.
    2. **Sweating stops:** Sweat glands reduce secretion to near zero so energy is not lost by evaporation.
    3. **Shivering:** The brain sends rapid bursts of nerve impulses to **skeletal muscles**, causing involuntary rhythmic contractions (**shivering**). Working muscle fibres increase their rate of **aerobic cellular respiration** of glucose to regenerate ATP, releasing large amounts of **thermal energy** that warms the blood returning to the core.
    4. **Insulation by hairs and fatty tissue:** **Hair erector muscles contract**, pulling hairs upright on end ("goosebumps" in humans). In furry mammals, upright hairs trap a thick layer of **still air** next to the skin; because still air is an extremely poor thermal conductor (`~0.023 W/(m·K)` in Book 2 Chapter 8), that trapped air blanket slows heat loss by conduction and convection. (In humans, whose body hair is sparse, goosebumps are an evolutionary vestige, so we pull on a woollen sweater to trap the still air instead and rely on subcutaneous **fatty tissue** for internal insulation.)

:::note Vasodilation and vasoconstriction
- **Vasodilation:** relaxation of smooth muscle in the walls of **arterioles** supplying skin surface capillaries, widening their lumens so **more blood flows through surface capillaries** and more thermal energy is transferred to the surroundings.
- **Vasoconstriction:** contraction of smooth muscle in the walls of **arterioles** supplying skin surface capillaries, narrowing their lumens so **less blood flows through surface capillaries** and less thermal energy is lost across the skin.
:::

:::predict
On two different afternoons, the dry-bulb air temperature in the shade is identical: **38 °C**. Afternoon A is in dry inland Nagpur (**20% relative humidity**, wet-bulb temperature **21 °C**). Afternoon B is on the humid Mumbai coast (**80% relative humidity**, wet-bulb temperature **34.8 °C**). A walker generates **180 W** of metabolic heat on both afternoons and secretes plenty of sweat. Why is the walker in far greater danger of overheating (heat exhaustion / heatstroke) in humid Mumbai than in dry Nagpur at the exact same 38 °C air temperature?
- Because humid air has a higher temperature than dry air at 38 °C
- Because sweating cools the body only when the water in sweat **evaporates** (absorbing 2,420 J/g of latent heat); at 80% relative humidity the air is already nearly saturated with water vapour, so evaporation slows down and sweat merely drips off the skin without cooling it
- Because sweat glands stop secreting sweat whenever the air is humid
???
The second choice. Evaporation supplies the large cooling effect of sweat: escaping water vapour carries away about **2,420 J per gram** of latent heat. Liquid dripping from skin provides no evaporative latent-heat removal. With air hotter than skin, convection adds heat; radiation depends separately on surrounding surface temperatures (in fact, 38 °C air transfers heat *into* 35.6 °C skin). In the chosen dry-air example, sufficient sweat evaporation can remove over 220 W of heat. Humidity alone does not specify the rate: airflow, clothing and sweat supply matter too. In humid Mumbai (80% relative humidity), the air is already crowded with water vapour molecules returning to the liquid surface, so the air–skin conditions restrict net evaporation. If evaporation removes less heat than your body gains and produces, heat accumulates even while sweat drips from your skin.
:::

:::evidence Latent heat of sweat evaporation and the wet-bulb limit | Thermal physiology
In Book 2 (*The Moving World*, Section 8.6), you calculated why a clay *matka* cools water by evaporation and why it works far better in dry air than in July monsoon humidity. Human skin obeys the exact same physical law.

At 37 °C, the **latent heat of vaporisation** of water is approximately **2,420 J g⁻¹** (`2.42 kJ g⁻¹`). A brisk walker whose cells and environment add **225 J of thermal energy every second (225 W = 810 kJ per hour)** must **evaporate** `810,000 J ÷ 2,420 J g⁻¹ ≈ 335 g` (about one-third of a litre) of sweat every hour just to keep core temperature flat at 37.0 °C. If high relative humidity limits actual evaporation to only `140 g per hour` (`~95 W`), even if the sweat glands pour out `600 g per hour` of dripping liquid sweat, the remaining `130 W` of uncompensated thermal energy stays inside the body, raising the core temperature of a 50 kg person by more than **2 °C per hour** toward the enzyme-denaturation danger zone.
:::

:::five Mammalian skin and thermoregulation
? What is it?
Negative-feedback regulation of internal body temperature around a **37.0 °C set point**, coordinated by the **brain (hypothalamus)** using skin blood vessels (arterioles and capillaries), sweat glands, hair erector muscles, subcutaneous fatty tissue and skeletal muscles.
? Why is it called this?
*Thermoregulation* means heat/temperature rule; *vasodilation* and *vasoconstriction* come from Latin *vas* (vessel) + widening or narrowing.
? What problem did it solve?
Internal temperature affects reaction rates and cellular function. Severe overheating can damage cells; there is no universal denaturation temperature for all human enzymes.
? Why does it work?
When too hot, **arteriole vasodilation** brings more warm blood to fixed surface capillaries and **sweat evaporation** removes `2,420 J/g` of latent heat; when too cold, **arteriole vasoconstriction** keeps blood deep beneath insulating **fatty tissue**, hairs trap still air, and **shivering** muscles respire glucose to generate heat.
? How does it help?
It helps maintain suitable internal temperatures through changing conditions, within the limits of the effectors.
?? How do we know?
Infrared thermography and laser Doppler flowmetry measure skin blood flow surges during vasodilation; calorimetry confirms that blocking sweat evaporation in humid air causes core temperature to rise even when sweat secretion is maximal.
?? What it isn't
Capillaries crawling up and down through the dermis, or liquid sweat cooling the body merely by dripping off onto the floor.
?? Where does it stop working?
Heat accumulates when available heat loss cannot balance heat production and environmental heat gain. Dangerous conditions can occur below 35 °C wet-bulb temperature; there is no universal safe cutoff for every person and activity.
:::

:::careful
> "When you get hot, your skin capillaries move closer to the surface of the skin and dilate; when you get cold, the capillaries move deeper down."

The vessels remain in place. Explain the change in blood flow through them.

1. **Capillaries are fixed in place** in the dermis—they do not swim or migrate up and down through tissue.
2. **Capillary walls are only one endothelial cell thick** and contain **no muscle fibres**, so capillaries cannot actively constrict or dilate on their own.
3. What actually changes is the ring of **smooth muscle in the walls of the arterioles** that supply the skin surface capillaries: during **vasodilation**, the **arterioles** relax and widen so **more blood flows through the surface capillaries**; during **vasoconstriction**, the **arterioles** contract and narrow so **less blood flows through the surface capillaries**.
:::

## 10.3 The chemical cleanup crew: liver deamination, urea, and the kidney’s two-stage nephron ledger

:::scene Philadelphia, 1924, and a Mumbai lunch table

At lunch after your walk in Dadar, you eat a bowl of *dal tadka* and *paneer*. In Chapter 7 you followed how stomach **pepsin** and intestinal **trypsin** and peptidases hydrolyse those dietary proteins into twenty kinds of **amino acids**, which cross the small-intestinal villi into capillaries and travel straight up the **hepatic portal vein** to the **liver**.

Now compare what your body can do with excess carbohydrate or fat versus excess amino acids:

- Extra **glucose** can be polymerised into **glycogen** in the liver and muscles, or converted into **fat** stored in adipose tissue.
- Extra **fatty acids and glycerol** are stored compactly in adipose tissue.
- **Excess amino acids, however, cannot be stored in a waiting tank** in the human body. Every day you eat protein, your cells take the exact amino acids they need right now for **assimilation**—building new cellular enzymes, structural proteins, and blood plasma proteins such as **albumin** and **fibrinogen** in the liver—and amino acids not used for synthesis can be broken down, with their nitrogen handled safely by the liver.

Before tracing how the liver and kidneys handle that dismantle-and-export job, lock in the strict biological boundary you first met in Chapter 1 (Section 1.4): **excretion is NOT egestion**.

- ==Excretion== is the removal from organisms of the **waste products of metabolism** (chemical reactions that took place *inside* living cells), **toxic materials**, and **substances in excess of requirements**.
- **Egestion** is the passing out of **undigested food** (such as plant cellulose fibre) as **faeces** through the anus—the process of removing unabsorbed food remnants. Faeces also contain living microbes, shed cells and substances excreted into the gut; Chapter 7 explains why they do not all share the same history.

In humans (**0610 Topic 13.1.1–13.1.2**), two major organ pairs carry out almost all metabolic excretion:

1. **The lungs** excrete **carbon dioxide (`CO₂`)** (and water vapour) produced by aerobic cellular respiration inside aerobic tissues (Chapter 8). If `CO₂` were not excreted continuously across the alveoli, it would react with water in blood plasma (`CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻`), disturbing blood acidity and impairing cellular function.
2. **The kidneys** excrete **urea**, **excess water**, and **excess mineral ions** (`Na⁺`, `K⁺`, `Cl⁻`) dissolved together as **urine**. (Sweat glands in the skin also release water, salts and small traces of urea, though sweat's primary regulated job is evaporative thermoregulation rather than waste clearance.)

How does **urea** get made in the first place? Never say that the kidneys make urea—**urea is made in the liver and excreted mainly through the kidneys**:

- Inside **liver cells (hepatocytes)**, needed amino acids from the hepatic portal vein undergo **assimilation**, being stitched on ribosomes into new proteins (such as plasma **fibrinogen** for blood clotting).
- Any **excess amino acids** undergo ==deamination== (**0610 13.1.6–13.1.8**): liver reactions remove the **nitrogen-containing amino part (`−NH₂`)**, separating nitrogen handling from the use of the remaining carbon skeleton:
    1. The removed **nitrogen-containing part** initially forms **ammonia (`NH₃`)**, a **toxic** nitrogen compound whose accumulation can disrupt brain function. Liver enzymes immediately combine ammonia with carbon dioxide (in the urea cycle) to convert it into ==urea== (`(NH₂)₂CO`)—a much less toxic, neutral, water-soluble nitrogen compound.
    2. The remaining **carbon skeleton** of the amino acid is not wasted: the liver converts it into **glycogen** or **fat** for storage, or respires it aerobically to release usable energy.
- Still, **urea itself becomes toxic** if allowed to build up in the blood (**uraemia** in untreated kidney failure damages cell membranes, nerves and the heart), and unexcreted **excess water and ions** disrupt the osmotic water potential of blood plasma. So liver cells release newly formed **urea** into the **hepatic vein**, and the bloodstream carries it to the **kidneys** for excretion.

:::note Deamination and urea
- **Deamination:** the **removal of the nitrogen-containing part of excess amino acids to form urea**, carried out by enzymes in the **liver** (while the remaining carbon skeleton is respired or converted to glycogen).
- **Urea:** the nitrogenous metabolic waste compound `(NH₂)₂CO` formed in the **liver** from excess amino acids, transported in blood plasma, and excreted by the **kidneys** in urine.
:::

Right: the human urinary system (renal arteries, kidneys with outer cortex and inner medulla, renal veins, ureters, urinary bladder, sphincter and urethra) above the Excretion vs Egestion ledger.

Look at the gross anatomy of the human urinary system in Figure 10.4B (**0610 13.1.3–13.1.4**):

- Oxygenated blood carrying dissolved urea, glucose, amino acids, water and mineral ions branches off the **aorta** into the left and right **renal arteries** (`renal` = kidney) and enters the two bean-shaped **kidneys** at the back of the upper abdomen.
- Sliced lengthwise, each kidney shows two distinct zones: a darker outer **cortex** and a paler, striped inner **medulla** arranged in cone-shaped pyramids that drain into a funnel-like central cavity (the renal pelvis).
- Cleaned blood with much less urea and adjusted water/ion levels leaves each kidney through the **renal vein** into the **inferior vena cava**.
- **Urine** trickles continuously out of each kidney down a muscular tube, the **ureter** (one left ureter, one right ureter), into a stretchy muscular bag, the **urinary bladder**, where urine is stored temporarily behind rings of **sphincter muscle** until it is released to the outside through a single tube, the **urethra**. (Keep **ureter** and **urethra** crystal clear: two **ureters** carry urine *into* the bladder from the two kidneys; one **urethra** carries urine *out* of the bladder.)

### Follow blood beside filtrate

:::recall 3

???

Now zoom inside the kidney's **cortex** and **medulla**, where about **one million** microscopic filtering-and-rescue units—called ==nephrons==—work in parallel inside each kidney (**0610 13.1.5**).

Follow two routes on the nephron drawing: blood continues through vessels, while some water and small dissolved substances cross into the capsule and become **filtrate**. Material can later cross from the tubule back towards blood. The main model here follows filtration and reabsorption. Kidneys also secrete selected substances from blood into tubules; filtration is affected by binding to proteins and by barrier properties, not molecular mass alone.

1. **Stage 1 (Glomerular ultrafiltration):** Push a massive volume of protein-free liquid plasma—containing many freely filterable small dissolved substances—through a simple size-selective sieve into a tubule (`~125 mL per minute`, or **`180 litres per day`** across your two kidneys).
2. **Stage 2 (Selective reabsorption):** Use specific membrane carrier proteins and water channels along the length of the tubule to **rescue (`reabsorb`) nearly all filtered glucose at ordinary loads and ~99% of the water and needed ions** back into the blood, leaving the unwanted **urea, excess water and excess ions** behind to drain out as **`~1.5 litres per day` of urine**.

:::predict
Your entire bloodstream contains only about **3 litres of liquid blood plasma**, yet your two kidneys filter **180 litres of fluid every single day** (60 times your total plasma volume) out of the blood into the nephron capsules. Look at the size of dissolved molecules in blood plasma:
- **Plasma proteins (such as albumin):** relative molecular mass `M_r ≈ 66,500` (large folded chains)
- **Glucose:** `M_r = 180` (small six-carbon sugar)
- **Urea:** `M_r = 60` (tiny nitrogen waste)
- **Water & sodium/chloride ions:** `M_r = 18 to 35.5` (tiny)

Which explanation fits glucose in filtrate but ordinarily no glucose detected in urine?
- The glomerulus never admits glucose
- Glucose filters, then tubule transport recovers it
- Tubules turn every glucose molecule into urea
???
The second choice. The glomerular barrier admits glucose along with water, urea and ions. Tubule transporters normally recover nearly all that glucose. For our chosen adult illustration, 5.0 mmol/L equals 0.09 g/100 mL; filtering 180 L/day delivers about **162 g glucose per day** into tubules. That is a filtered load, not a daily loss. Insufficient recovery can cause glucose loss and increase urinary water loss; glucose reabsorption alone does not determine a dehydration timescale.
:::

:::evidence Philadelphia, 1924: Joseph Wearn and Alfred Newton Richards puncture a single living renal capsule | Direct experimental proof
In 1844 the German physiologist **Carl Ludwig** proposed that the kidney's **glomerulus** acts as a physical pressure filter and that the tubule concentrates the filtrate by reabsorbing water. For eighty years, however, critics argued that no one had ever sampled fluid straight out of a renal capsule to prove it.

In **1924**, at the University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia, **Joseph T. Wearn and Alfred Newton Richards** placed the exposed kidney of an anaesthetised frog under a stereoscopic microscope and used a micromanipulator to guide a sharpened quartz capillary pipette (`10–20 µm` wide) directly into the space of a single **Bowman's (renal) capsule**. Collecting tiny droplets of capsular fluid while simultaneously collecting bladder urine from the ureter of the same kidney, they ran micro-chemical tests on both fluids:

1. **Protein test:** Blood plasma had `~8 g/100 mL` protein, whereas **no protein was detected in capsular fluid**, supporting selective filtration rather than passage of whole plasma unchanged.
2. **Glucose test:** **Capsular fluid tested strongly positive for glucose** (matching blood plasma), whereas **bladder urine from the very same kidney tested completely negative (`0.00`) for glucose**—proving directly that glucose is filtered in the glomerulus and then nearly all recovered within the sensitivity of the tests as the fluid travels down the **renal tubule**.
:::

Trace the separate routes of blood and filtrate through all parts of a single **nephron** and its associated blood vessels in Figure 10.5 (**0610 13.1.5**):

1. **The glomerulus and renal (Bowman's) capsule (in the kidney cortex) — Ultrafiltration:** A branch of the renal artery feeds an **afferent arteriole** that enters a cup-shaped sac called the **renal capsule (Bowman's capsule)** in the kidney **cortex** and tangles into a tight knot of leaky capillaries called the ==glomerulus==. Because the **efferent arteriole** draining blood *out* of the glomerulus is narrower than the afferent arteriole bringing blood *in*, **blood hydrostatic pressure builds up high** inside the glomerulus capillaries (`~55 mmHg`). That pressure forces part of the liquid plasma through microscopic pores in the capillary wall and basement membrane into the hollow cup of the **renal capsule**:
    - **What passes into the glomerular filtrate:** **water, glucose, urea, and mineral ions** (plus amino acids).
    - **What stays behind in the blood:** **red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and large plasma proteins** (such as albumin and fibrinogen), which are too large to fit through the filtration barrier.
2. **The renal tubule (proximal tubule in the cortex, loop of Henle dipping into the medulla, and distal tubule) — Selective reabsorption:** Wrapped tightly around the outside of the **renal tubule** is a dense second capillary bed (the **peritubular capillaries** and *vasa recta* branching from the efferent arteriole) waiting to take rescued materials back into the blood:
    - **Nearly all filtered glucose at ordinary loads** is selectively reabsorbed in the first coiled section of the renal tubule (**proximal convoluted tubule**) back into the blood. How can the tubule recover glucose even when glucose concentration inside the tubule drops below blood concentration? By **active transport** (coupled to sodium ions, just like the intestinal villus cells you studied in Chapter 7): the tubule lining cells have a dense **microvilli** brush border for huge surface area and are packed with **mitochondria** making **ATP** to power sodium–potassium pumps, while **sodium–glucose carrier proteins** recover filtered glucose back into the blood (up to a maximum transport **rate**, expressed as amount per time, not a concentration).
    - **Some of the mineral ions** (`Na⁺`, `Cl⁻`, `K⁺`) are selectively reabsorbed by active transport and diffusion in the exact amounts needed to keep blood plasma ion concentration steady, while **excess ions** are left in the filtrate.
    - **Most of the water** (`~178.5 L` out of `180 L/day`, over `99%`) is reabsorbed back into the blood by **osmosis** down the water potential gradient created by the reabsorbed solutes and the salty kidney **medulla**, passing rapidly through membrane water-channel proteins—the **aquaporins** discovered by **Peter Agre** that you met in Chapter 3.
3. **The collecting duct (running through cortex and medulla to the ureter) — Formation of urine:** Several nephron tubules join a shared **collecting duct** that plunges through the salty **medulla** toward the renal pelvis and **ureter**. When you are dehydrated on a hot Mumbai afternoon, more **ADH** is released from the posterior pituitary into blood. ADH is made by hypothalamic cells. This hormone signals collecting duct cells to insert more **aquaporin** channels into their membranes so even more water is reabsorbed into the blood, producing a **small volume of concentrated, dark yellow urine**. When you drink plenty of water on a cool day, fewer aquaporins are inserted so **excess water** stays in the tubule, producing a **larger volume of pale, dilute urine**. The final fluid leaving the collecting duct into the ureter is **urine**, containing **urea, excess water, and excess ions** (with very little protein and normally no glucose detected by ordinary tests).

:::note Glomerulus, nephron, and selective reabsorption
- **Nephron:** one of roughly a million microscopic filtering-and-reabsorbing tubules spanning the **cortex** and **medulla** of each kidney.
- **Glomerulus:** a high-pressure knot of capillaries sitting inside the cup-shaped **renal (Bowman's) capsule** in the kidney cortex; it filters **water, glucose, urea and mineral ions** out of the blood while retaining blood cells and large plasma proteins.
- **Selective reabsorption:** the process in the **renal tubule** by which **nearly all filtered glucose at ordinary loads**, **most of the water**, and **some (needed) mineral ions** are taken back from the filtrate into the surrounding blood capillaries, leaving **urea, excess water and excess ions** to pass down the **collecting duct** as **urine**.
:::

## 10.4 The invisible border guard: pathogens, barriers, cholera, antibodies, and immunological memory

:::scene Parel, Mumbai, 1958, Bongaon, 1971, and Vellore, 2014

Homeostasis and excretion keep your internal temperature, glucose, water and urea in balance. Yet a warm, nutrient-rich bath of blood plasma and tissue fluid held at 37.0 °C with 5.0 mmol/L glucose is also a five-star hotel for microscopic invaders.

Every time the June monsoon floods a street, or someone sneezes in a crowded carriage between Dadar and Churchgate, you encounter microorganisms seeking to enter that internal environment. In **Cambridge IGCSE (`0610 Topic 10.1`)**, keep two foundational definitions crisp:

- A ==pathogen== is a **disease-causing organism** (including disease-causing **bacteria**, **fungi**, **protoctists** such as the malaria parasite *Plasmodium*, and—by functional extension in medicine and exams—non-living **viruses**).
- A ==transmissible disease== is a **disease in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another** (in contrast to non-transmissible diseases such as Type 1 diabetes, scurvy or coronary heart disease, which cannot be caught from another person).

:::note Pathogen and transmissible disease
- **Pathogen:** a disease-causing organism (or virus).
- **Transmissible disease:** a disease in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another.
:::

How does a pathogen travel from an infected host to a new host? Epidemiologists group transmission into two routes (**0610 10.1.3**), each blocked by specific **community and personal hygiene barriers (`0610 10.1.5`)**:

1. **Direct contact:** the pathogen passes directly from one person to another without an intermediate object—through **direct physical skin contact** (such as ringworm fungus) or through **blood and other body fluids** (such as HIV, which you can investigate in a separate account of infection, or hepatitis B virus).
2. **Indirect transmission:** the pathogen travels via an intermediate medium between hosts:
    - **Through contaminated water** (such as the **cholera** bacterium *Vibrio cholerae* in water contaminated with untreated sewage/faeces) → controlled by a **clean water supply** (filtration and chlorination/boiling) and **sewage treatment** (preventing human faeces from entering rivers, wells and pipes).
    - **Through contaminated food** (such as *Salmonella* or *Campylobacter* bacteria on undercooked poultry or food touched by unwashed hands) → controlled by **hygienic food preparation** (washing hands before handling food, keeping raw meat separate from cooked food, cooking thoroughly to kill bacteria, and refrigerating food to slow bacterial reproduction) and **safe waste disposal** (keeping household rubbish in covered bins so flies and rats cannot breed on waste and transfer pathogens to food).
    - **From contaminated surfaces (fomites)** (such as cold or influenza viruses on door handles and handrails) → controlled by **good personal hygiene** (washing hands with soap and water, which disrupts viral lipid envelopes and washes microbes off skin, and not touching eyes, nose or mouth with unwashed hands).
    - **Through the air in airborne droplets** (released when an infected person coughs, sneezes or talks, carrying influenza, measles or tuberculosis pathogens) → controlled by covering coughs and sneezes with a tissue and ventilating crowded rooms.
    - **By animals (vectors)** (such as female *Anopheles* mosquitoes transmitting the malaria protoctist, or rabid dogs transmitting rabies virus in saliva) → controlled by vector control (draining stagnant water, sleeping under insecticide-treated nets) and animal vaccination.

When pathogens do reach your body surface, they first face your **non-specific first line of defence**—**mechanical barriers** and **chemical barriers** that work around the clock against *all* pathogens alike (**0610 10.1.4**):

- **Mechanical barriers (physical obstacles):**
    1. **Skin:** the tough, dead, keratinised outer layer of the **epidermis** forms a continuous waterproof shield that bacteria and viruses cannot penetrate while unbroken. If the skin is cut, **platelets** immediately trigger **blood clotting** (converting soluble **fibrinogen** into an insoluble **fibrin** mesh that traps red blood cells to seal the wound and block pathogen entry, Chapter 8).
    2. **Hairs in the nose:** coarse hairs inside the nostrils filter out larger inhaled dust particles and microbe-laden droplets before air reaches the deeper airways.
- **Chemical barriers (trapping and destroying):**
    1. **Mucus:** **goblet cells** lining the trachea, bronchi and nasal passages secrete sticky **mucus** that traps inhaled bacteria, viruses and dust spores; **ciliated epithelial cells** then beat rhythmically to sweep that pathogen-laden mucus upward to the back of the throat to be swallowed (Chapter 2 and Chapter 8).
    2. **Stomach acid:** glands in the stomach lining secrete concentrated **hydrochloric acid (`HCl`, pH ~1.5–2.0)**, which denatures the enzymes of most swallowed bacteria in food, water or swallowed mucus (while tears and saliva also contain the antibacterial enzyme **lysozyme**).

What happens when a water-borne bacterium slips past stomach acid—for instance, when someone drinks water heavily contaminated with the comma-shaped bacterium ***Vibrio cholerae***, causing **cholera** (**0610 10.1.16–10.1.17**)?

Trace the exact cellular and osmotic chain of events inside the small intestine in Figure 10.6E:

1. **Ingestion and colonisation:** *Vibrio cholerae* bacteria transmitted in **contaminated water** (or food washed in contaminated water) survive passage through the stomach and multiply in the lumen of the **small intestine**.
2. **Secretion of cholera toxin:** The bacteria secrete a protein exotoxin—**cholera toxin**—that binds to the membrane of the epithelial cells lining the small intestine (without tearing holes in the intestinal wall, as **Sambhu Nath De** proved in Kolkata in 1959).
3. **Active secretion of chloride ions (`Cl⁻`):** Inside the intestinal epithelial cells, cholera toxin changes intracellular signalling, increasing channel-mediated secretion of **chloride ions (`Cl⁻`)** into the lumen of the small intestine (followed by sodium ions, `Na⁺`).
4. **Osmotic movement of water into the gut:** The flood of `Cl⁻` and `Na⁺` ions increases the solute concentration of the intestinal lumen, **lowering the water potential (`Ψ_w`) of the gut lumen** below the water potential of the epithelial cells and blood plasma. By **osmosis** (Chapter 3), water pours **out of the cells and bloodstream into the gut lumen**.
5. **Consequences and Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT):** That osmotic water outflow produces litres of profuse watery **diarrhoea**, causing rapid, life-threatening **dehydration** (loss of blood volume and circulatory shock) and **loss of mineral ions (`Cl⁻`, `Na⁺`, `K⁺`) from the blood**. As **Dilip Mahalanabis** demonstrated on a historic scale in the **Bongaon** refugee camp in 1971, rehydration treatment can use **oral rehydration solution (ORS)**—drinking a carefully formulated glucose–electrolyte solution, prepared from standard ORS packets exactly as directed—because the brush-border **`Na⁺`–glucose cotransporter** stays intact and pulls `Na⁺` and glucose back into the intestinal cells, drawing water back into the blood by osmosis.

Bottom: how cholera toxin causes chloride ion secretion and osmotic water loss into the small intestine, and how Oral Rehydration Solution (water + measured salt and glucose) reverses that flow.

B lymphocytes can become antibody-secreting plasma cells; T lymphocytes have other roles, including coordinating responses and attacking infected cells. The following antibody account focuses on B cells.

### From barriers to a specific response

:::recall 3
Does receiving ready-made antibody automatically build immune memory?
???
No. Passive antibody protection does not itself create the recipient’s specific memory response.
:::

Now suppose a pathogen breaches the mechanical and chemical barriers and enters your **blood or tissue fluid**. Here it meets the **cellular immune system**—the two types of **white blood cells** you learned to identify by their nuclei in Chapter 8:

1. **Phagocytes — non-specific phagocytosis:** Neutrophils are the lobed-nucleus example; other phagocytes differ. Phagocytes patrol blood and squeeze through capillary walls into infected tissue fluid. When a phagocyte encounters a bacterium, it flows around it, **engulfs** it into an internal membrane sac (a phagocytic vacuole), and **digests** it using hydrolytic enzymes—**phagocytosis**. Phagocytes act fast (within minutes to hours), but they do not form specific molecular memory of one pathogen species versus another.
2. **Lymphocytes (large round nucleus) — specific antigen–antibody immunity and memory:** Every pathogen carries complex molecules (usually proteins or glycoproteins) on its outer surface called ==antigens==. Crucially, **each pathogen has its own antigens, which have specific three-dimensional shapes** (**0610 10.1.7**). Many **B lymphocytes** circulate through your blood and lymph nodes, each wearing surface receptor proteins of one specific complementary shape. When a pathogen enters the body, only the few lymphocytes whose receptors have a **complementary shape** that fits the pathogen's specific **antigen** bind to it. Those selected B lymphocytes are stimulated to divide repeatedly by mitosis (building a huge clone of identical cells) and differentiate into two groups:
    - **Antibody-secreting lymphocytes (plasma cells)** that print and release thousands of Y-shaped protein molecules per second into the blood plasma called ==antibodies==. Each **specific antibody has a binding site with a complementary shape that fits the specific antigen** like a 3D lock and key (**0610 10.1.8–10.1.9**). Binding can neutralise a toxin or prevent a pathogen entering cells. Antibodies can also clump pathogens, help activate membrane-damaging defence mechanisms, or **mark (opsonise) pathogens for phagocytes**, which recognise attached antibody tails. Clumping is not itself the same as killing.
    - **Long-lived ==memory cells==** that remain circulating quietly in the blood and lymph nodes for years—often for your entire life.

:::note Antigen, antibody, and active immunity
- **Antigen:** a molecule (usually a surface protein) on a pathogen or foreign cell that has a **specific shape** and stimulates an immune response by lymphocytes.
- **Antibody:** a specialised **protein** secreted by plasma cells formed from **B lymphocytes**. Its binding sites recognise particular molecular features of an **antigen**. Binding can neutralise a pathogen or toxin, support other clearance mechanisms, or mark a pathogen for phagocytes.
- **Active immunity:** **defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body**, gained either after catching an infection or by **vaccination**, and accompanied by the production of **memory cells** that give **long-term immunity**.
:::

Look at how **vaccination** uses this exact memory mechanism to give you **active immunity** without your having to suffer the dangerous disease first (**0610 10.1.11–10.1.12**):

1. **A harmless form of the pathogen's antigen—such as weakened (attenuated) or dead pathogens, or their isolated surface antigens**—is put into the body (by injection or oral drops). Killed organisms and isolated antigen pieces cannot replicate. Live attenuated organisms can replicate and usually do not cause the corresponding severe disease in the intended recipient.
2. **The antigens stimulate a primary immune response by lymphocytes**, which recognise the specific antigen shape, divide, and **produce complementary antibodies** (while phagocytes clear the vaccine antigens).
3. **Memory cells are produced** and remain in the blood and lymph for years or decades, giving **long-term active immunity**: if the real, live pathogen ever enters the body later, memory B cells can rapidly form antibody-secreting cells that can reduce illness; prevention of infection and onward transmission varies.
4. **Controlling the spread of disease in a community (herd immunity):** When a high proportion of people in a city or school are vaccinated and immune, a transmissible pathogen has very few susceptible hosts left to infect; chains of transmission break down, protecting even newborn babies or medically vulnerable neighbours who cannot yet be vaccinated.

Finally, compare **active immunity** with ==passive immunity== in Figure 10.8 (**0610 10.1.6, 10.1.10, 10.1.13–10.1.15**):

- Suppose a newborn baby has just entered a world full of bacteria and viruses, or a farmer in rural Maharashtra is bitten by a venomous Russell's viper or a rabid dog. Waiting 7 to 14 days for naive lymphocytes to mount a slow primary response would be too late.
- Nature and medicine solve that urgent gap with **passive immunity**—**short-term defence against a pathogen by ready-made antibodies acquired from another individual**:
    1. **Natural passive immunity (mother to baby):** During pregnancy, maternal **antibodies** pass across the **placenta** from the mother's blood into the fetus's blood; after birth, the mother's **breast milk** (especially the thick yellowish **colostrum** produced in the first days) is rich in ready-made antibodies that coat the infant's gut and protect the baby against some pathogens encountered in the family’s environment while the infant's own immune system is still maturing.
    2. **Artificial passive immunity (injected serum/antitoxin):** If someone is exposed to rabies virus, tetanus toxin or snake venom, clinicians inject purified, pre-formed **antibodies** harvested from an immune donor or animal.
- Why is passive immunity always **short-term** (lasting only a few weeks to a few months)? Because **antibody transfer itself does not generate the recipient’s antigen-specific memory** (**0610 10.1.15**), and the borrowed antibody proteins are gradually broken down and cleared by the liver and phagocytes (with a half-life of about 3 weeks).

:::note Passive immunity
**Short-term defence against a pathogen by ready-made antibodies acquired from another individual** (such as maternal antibodies crossing the **placenta** or transferred in **breast milk** to an infant, or injected antitoxin/antivenom). Protection is **immediate**, **the antibody transfer itself does not create memory**, and protection fades as the borrowed antibodies are broken down.
:::

:::five Active immunity, vaccination, and passive immunity
? What is it?
Two ways of obtaining antibody protection, in blood or at surfaces such as the gut: **active immunity** (your own **B lymphocytes** give rise to antibody-secreting cells and **memory cells** after meeting an **antigen** in an infection or **vaccine**) versus **passive immunity** (you receive ready-made **antibodies from another individual**, such as across the **placenta** or in **breast milk**).
? Why is it called this?
*Active* means your own immune cells actively divide and synthesise the antibody proteins; *passive* means your body receives pre-made antibody proteins without your lymphocytes doing the manufacturing.
? What problem did it solve?
Active immunity can provide lasting protection via **memory cells**, sometimes needing boosters, while passive immunity bridges urgent windows (newborn infancy or emergency venom/rabies exposure) when there is no time to wait 10 days for a primary lymphocyte response.
? Why does it work?
Antibodies bind molecular features of antigens, some of which can be shared between pathogens. Binding can neutralise a pathogen or toxin and help other defences clear it, including by marking it for **phagocytes**. After active immunisation, **memory cells** can support a stronger later response. Borrowed antibodies can act immediately but are gradually cleared.
? How does it help?
It explains why antibodies in breast milk can protect an infant’s gut without replacing the infant’s own vaccinations. It also explains how clinicians can combine ready-made antibodies for immediate defence with vaccination to stimulate a longer-lasting response after certain exposures.
?? How do we know?
Measuring blood antibody titres over time shows an immediate peak followed by a ~21-day half-life decay after passive antibody transfer (with no booster surge), versus a delayed primary peak and a huge, rapid secondary memory surge after vaccination.
?? What it isn't
A vaccine injecting ready-made antibodies (vaccines contain **weakened pathogens or their antigens**, never passive antibodies), or passive immunity creating memory cells.
?? Where does it stop working?
If a virus mutates the 3D shape of its surface coat antigens rapidly (as influenza does), old antibodies and memory cells may no longer fit the new antigen shape closely enough, requiring an updated vaccine.
:::

:::careful
> "A vaccine works by injecting ready-made antibodies so your white blood cells learn how to copy them, and breast milk gives a baby permanent active immunity."

Correct both parts—this is a classic Cambridge 0610 Supplement contrast:

1. **What is inside a vaccine:** A vaccine contains **weakened or dead pathogens, or their antigens** (Gives **active immunity**). Your own **lymphocytes** must bind those antigens, divide, and produce the **antibodies** and **memory cells** themselves.
2. **What is inside breast milk (and crosses the placenta):** Ready-made **antibodies** made by the mother's body (Gives **passive immunity**). They protect the infant **immediately**, antibody transfer itself does not create the baby’s specific memory response, and the protection is **short-term** because the borrowed antibody proteins are gradually broken down.
:::

One pupil says glucose in urine requires a torn glomerular filter. Another says the transporters must be destroyed. A third suggests that excess glucose delivery can overwhelm functioning transporters. Which explanation fits these stipulated measurements? Calculate the glucose loss, explain the extra water loss, and identify what the negative protein test cannot prove.
???
Glucose normally enters filtrate, so it needs no tear to reach the tubule. No abnormal protein detected gives no evidence for a major protein leak, but is not proof that every kidney structure is normal. The independent transport measurements, rather than that negative test, establish the functioning carriers assumed here.

Filtered glucose is concentration × filtration rate: **2.88 mg/mL × 125 mL/min = 360 mg/min**. With the chosen recovery maximum of **330 mg/min**, excretion is **30 mg/min**, or **43.2 g/day**. In the comparison state the filtered load is below capacity. This sharp-capacity model omits differences between nephrons and the gradual onset of glucose spillage in real kidneys. A plasma concentration threshold is not the same quantity as a maximum transport rate.

Glucose remaining in tubular fluid lowers its water potential and reduces water recovery by osmosis. This can increase urine volume and promote thirst. **3.8 L/day is an observation supplied in the case**, not a volume calculated from glucose alone; water intake, ADH and other solutes matter. Glucose concentration also changes as water is recovered, so we cannot subtract plasma concentrations and carry the difference unchanged down the tubule.

Restoring an appropriate insulin signal improves muscle glucose uptake and liver glucose handling, including reducing excessive liver glucose production. Lower plasma glucose reduces the filtered load and can end the glucose loss without repairing a tear that was never required by the explanation. This case illustrates mechanisms, not a personal treatment or diagnostic rule.
