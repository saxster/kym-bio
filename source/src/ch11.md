In Volume III you watched a living human body hold the line against heat, starvation, dehydration and microbial invasion. Every single step in that defence depended on a specific **protein** folded into an exact three-dimensional shape: **amylases** and **proteases** hydrolysing food in your gut (Chapter 7), **haemoglobin** ferrying oxygen inside red blood cells (Chapter 8), voltage-gated **sodium channels** and postsynaptic **neurotransmitter receptors** passing electrical signals across 20 nm synapses (Chapter 9), and **insulin**, **glucagon**, **aquaporins**, **sodium–glucose carrier proteins** and **antibodies** guarding your internal environment (Chapter 10).

Think back to the backbone constraints mapped by **G. N. Ramachandran** in Chapter 4, and Anfinsen’s sequence-directed refolding experiments: a protein folds into its working three-dimensional shape—whether an enzyme active site, a membrane channel or an antibody binding tip—because of the exact linear sequence of the twenty kinds of **amino acids** linked along its polypeptide chain. And think back to what **Obaid Siddiqi** discovered in *Drosophila* fruit flies at Caltech and TIFR Colaba in Chapter 9: a single change in one **gene** (*para*) altered one voltage-gated sodium channel protein so that the fly's nerve axons stopped firing at 29 °C.

Those discoveries point to the central question of Volume IV (*The Master Code & The Four-Billion-Year Case File*): **Where does a cell keep the master instruction book for building thousands of different proteins, what is that instruction book made of, and how do tiny ribosomes in the cytoplasm read a four-letter chemical script to print the right protein on demand?**

## 11.1 The hidden instruction book: chromosomes, DNA and genes

:::scene A pathology bench in London, 1928, a biochemical laboratory in New York, 1944, and a Mumbai kitchen counter

Look inside the **nucleus** of a plant or animal cell under a microscope (Chapter 2). When the cell is about to divide, the grainy tangle inside the nucleus condenses into dark, thread-like structures called ==chromosomes== (from Greek *chroma*, "colour", and *soma*, "body", because nineteenth-century microscopists noticed how strongly they soaked up coloured aniline dyes). Chemical analysis of isolated cell nuclei soon showed that chromosomes contain two main classes of giant molecules: **proteins** (built from 20 different amino acids) and **deoxyribonucleic acid** (**DNA**, built from just 4 different nucleotide building blocks).

For more than forty years, almost every biologist in the world bet on the wrong molecule. Because proteins are built from a rich twenty-letter alphabet of amino acids that fold into countless shapes, while DNA uses only a four-letter alphabet, most researchers assumed that **chromosomal proteins** must carry the cell's hereditary instructions (**genes**), while DNA was merely a dull wooden frame holding the proteins in place.

How do you test which molecule in a chromosome actually carries the genes? You cannot guess from how complicated a molecule looks on paper; you have to isolate the hereditary signal in a test tube, destroy one candidate molecule at a time, and see which destruction erases the instructions.

In 1928 in London, the medical officer **Frederick Griffith** was studying two strains of the pneumonia bacterium *Streptococcus pneumoniae*:

- A **Smooth (S) strain**, whose cells build a smooth, glistening polysaccharide capsule around themselves that shields them from the host's phagocytes (Chapter 10), causing fatal pneumonia in mice.
- A **Rough (R) strain**, whose cells lack the gene for building the protective capsule, form rough-edged colonies on agar, and are harmlessly cleared by a mouse's immune system.

Griffith found that injecting **heat-killed Smooth (S)** bacteria alone did not harm mice, and injecting **live Rough (R)** bacteria alone did not harm mice. Yet when he injected a mixture of **heat-killed S bacteria** together with **live R bacteria**, the mice developed severe pneumonia—and their blood teemed with **living Smooth (S) bacteria** whose descendants kept making smooth capsules generation after generation. Some heat-stable chemical molecule—which Griffith called the **transforming principle**—had passed from the dead S cells into the living R cells and handed them the working hereditary instruction for building a capsule.
:::

:::predict
In 1944 at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, **Oswald Avery**, **Colin MacLeod**, and **Maclyn McCarty** broke open heat-killed Smooth (S) bacteria, removed the lipids and polysaccharide coats, and divided the active cell-free transforming extract into three test tubes before adding live Rough (R) bacteria:

**Tube 1** was treated with **protease enzymes** (trypsin and chymotrypsin, Chapter 7), which digest **proteins**.
**Tube 2** was treated with **ribonuclease (RNase)**, an enzyme that digests **RNA**.
**Tube 3** was treated with **deoxyribonuclease (DNase)**, an enzyme that digests **DNA**.

If **DNA**—and not protein or RNA—is the molecule that carries genes, which tubes will still transform harmless Rough (R) bacteria into capsulated Smooth (S) bacteria, and which tube will fail?
- Tube 1 (protease-treated) will fail to transform R bacteria, while Tubes 2 and 3 will still produce live S bacteria
- Tubes 1 and 2 will still transform R bacteria into live Smooth (S) bacteria because destroying protein or RNA leaves the DNA intact, whereas Tube 3 (DNase-treated) will produce only Rough (R) bacteria because destroying DNA destroys the gene
- All three tubes will fail because genes require protein, RNA and DNA to stay glued together at all times
???
The second choice. Because enzymes have complementary active sites that are strictly specific to their substrates (Chapter 4), **proteases** destroy only proteins, **RNase** destroys only RNA, and **DNase** destroys only DNA. When Avery, MacLeod and McCarty destroyed all the protein in Tube 1 or all the RNA in Tube 2, the extract transformed Rough (R) cells into Smooth (S) cells just as strongly as the untreated control. Only when **DNase** chopped up the **DNA** in Tube 3 did hereditary transformation vanish completely.
:::

:::evidence New York, 1944, and Cold Spring Harbor, 1952: Avery–MacLeod–McCarty and Hershey–Chase | Biochemical & isotopic proof
In their landmark February 1944 paper in the *Journal of Experimental Medicine*, **Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty** combined three independent lines of evidence on the purified transforming substance from *Streptococcus pneumoniae*:

1. **Enzymatic elimination:** Crystalline **trypsin**, **chymotrypsin** (proteases) and **ribonuclease (RNase)** had zero effect on transforming activity, whereas trace amounts of **deoxyribonuclease (DNase)** permanently abolished transformation.
2. **Elemental stoichiometry:** Pure proteins contain nitrogen (`N`) and sulfur (`S`) but almost no phosphorus (`P`), whereas DNA contains phosphorus in every nucleotide of its backbone and no sulfur. The purified transforming substance had a nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio (`N : P ≈ 1.67`) matching pure sodium deoxyribonucleate (`DNA`).
3. **Eight years later (1952), Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase** confirmed the result using bacteriophage **T2**—a virus made of a protein coat enclosing a DNA core (Chapter 1). They grew one batch of phages with radioactive sulfur (**`³⁵S`**, which labels **protein** coats because the amino acids cysteine and methionine contain sulfur, while DNA contains zero sulfur) and a second batch with radioactive phosphorus (**`³²P`**, which labels **DNA**, while phage protein contains zero phosphorus). After allowing the phages to infect *E. coli* bacteria and shearing off the empty virus coats in a kitchen blender, roughly **80 percent of the `³⁵S` protein stayed outside** in the liquid, whereas the **`³²P` DNA entered the bacterial cells** and directed the assembly of new phages.
:::

Trace the exact **scale hierarchy** in the middle panel of Figure 11.1 from largest to smallest so you never muddle where these structures sit in a eukaryotic cell (**0893 `9Bs.01, 9Bs.02`; 0610 `17.1 Core 1–2`**):

1. **Cell (`~10–50 µm` across):** a living plant, animal or fungal cell bounded by a cell membrane.
2. **Nucleus (`~5–10 µm` diameter):** the membrane-bound compartment inside a eukaryotic cell that houses the cell's hereditary archive. (Remember from Chapter 2 that prokaryotic bacterial cells have no nucleus; their main circular DNA molecule and tiny circular **plasmids** float in the cytoplasm.)
3. **Chromosome:** a long, coiled thread-like structure found **inside the nucleus** and **made of DNA** wrapped around packaging proteins. Each chromosome is a single, unbroken, extraordinarily long DNA molecule containing hundreds or thousands of genes arranged in a line like recipes in a multi-volume encyclopaedia.
4. **Gene:** a specific **length (segment) of DNA** at a particular position on a chromosome that **codes for a specific protein**—and by coding for that protein (alongside other genes and the organism's environment), helps to determine the **characteristics** of the organism.
5. **DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid, `2.0 nm` diameter):** the chemical polymer molecule itself, whose sequence of chemical **bases** along each gene spells out the genetic code.

:::note Chromosome, DNA, and gene
- **Chromosome:** a thread-like structure located in the **nucleus** of a cell, **made of DNA**, which carries genetic information in the form of **genes**.
- **DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid):** the giant double-stranded polymer molecule that chromosomes and genes are made of.
- **Gene:** a **length of DNA that codes for a protein** (and therefore helps to determine a characteristic of an organism).
:::

Why is it important to say at Lower Secondary level (`0893 9Bs.02`) that genes **help to determine** an organism's characteristics rather than acting alone in a vacuum? A gene supplies the DNA code for building a specific protein—for instance, an enzyme that synthesises chlorophyll in a young mango leaf, or growth proteins in human bones. Whether the plant actually turns deep green also depends on environmental supplies of light and magnesium ions (`Mg²⁺`, Chapter 5), and how tall a child grows depends on dozens of genes interacting with childhood nutrition and health (Chapter 7). Some characteristics (such as your ABO blood group or the sequence of your beta-globin protein) are set directly by your genes, while most whole-body traits emerge from **genes and environment working together**.

:::five Chromosomes, DNA, and genes inside the nucleus
? What is it?
The three-level filing system of heredity inside a eukaryotic cell: the **nucleus** holds thread-like **chromosomes**, each chromosome is **made of a long coiled molecule of DNA**, and each **gene** is a specific **length of that DNA that codes for one protein**.
? Why is it called this?
*Chromosome* means "coloured body" (from how strongly chromosomal threads take up microscope dyes); *gene* comes from Greek *genos* ("birth / origin"), coined by Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909 for the unit of heredity.
? What problem did it solve?
A single human cell nucleus is only ~6 µm across, yet it must store roughly **2 metres** of DNA carrying around 20,000 protein-coding recipes without tangling or losing a single recipe. Coiling long DNA molecules into chromosomes keeps the library organised inside the nucleus.
? Why does it work?
Each chromosome is one continuous DNA double helix divided into thousands of distinct **genes** along its length; when the cell needs a particular enzyme, hormone or membrane channel, it looks up just that gene's segment of DNA.
? How does it help?
Keeping the hierarchy straight (**Nucleus → Chromosome → Gene → DNA base sequence**) prevents one of the most common biology exam confusions—mixing up which structure is made of which, or which one is larger.
?? How do we know?
Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (1944) proved that **DNase** (which destroys DNA) erases gene function while proteases and RNase do not; Hershey and Chase (1952) proved that phage **`³²P`-labelled DNA** enters cells to direct new virus production.
?? What it isn't
A gene is **not** larger than a chromosome (a single chromosome carries hundreds or thousands of genes), and a gene does not "turn into" a protein—it is a permanent DNA instruction segment that **codes for** a protein.
?? Where does it stop working?
Red blood cells in mammals eject their nucleus as they mature to pack in more haemoglobin (Chapter 2), so mature red blood cells contain zero chromosomes and zero genes—they must print all their haemoglobin proteins while they are still young nucleated bone-marrow cells.
:::

:::careful
> "DNA is made of chromosomes, and each chromosome is inside a gene."

Reverse that hierarchy completely. Think of a **multi-volume recipe encyclopaedia** locked inside a library reading room:

- **The library room = the cell nucleus.**
- **Each bound volume = one chromosome** (made of one continuous, tightly coiled **DNA** thread).
- **One recipe printed on a few pages of that volume = one gene** (a specific **length of DNA** that codes for one protein).
- **The paper and ink letters themselves = the DNA molecule and its bases.**

Therefore, from largest to smallest: **Cell → Nucleus → Chromosome → Gene → DNA nucleotide bases**. Chromosomes are made of DNA, and each chromosome contains many genes along its length.
:::

1. 2. Pour this lysis mixture into the mashed banana bag, rock gently for five minutes, and filter the liquid through a fine tea strainer or coffee filter into a narrow glass (**Step 2:** detergent dissolves the fatty **lipid bilayers** of both the cell membrane and the **nuclear membrane**, releasing DNA into solution; **Step 3:** positively charged sodium ions **`Na⁺`** from the salt shield the negatively charged phosphate groups along the DNA backbones so the strands can come together instead of repelling each other).
3. Tilt the narrow glass and slowly pour an equal volume of **ice-cold rubbing alcohol** (chilled in the freezer for 30 minutes) down the inside wall of the glass so it floats as a clear top layer above the cloudy banana filtrate (**Step 4:** DNA is soluble in salty water but **insoluble in cold alcohol**).
4. Watch the boundary between the two layers for two minutes: millions of microscopic DNA molecules precipitate out of solution together as **cloudy white, mucus-like threads** rising into the cold alcohol layer, which you can spool around a wooden toothpick. Remember what you are looking at: a single DNA double helix is only **`2.0 nm` wide**—far too thin to see with the naked eye or a school light microscope—so the white thread on your toothpick is millions of DNA strands clumped together, mixed with other cellular material. ## 11.2 The twisted ladder: Photo 51, Chargaff's ratios and the double helix

:::scene Columbia University, New York, 1950, King's College London, May 1952, and the Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, April 1953

Once Avery's team proved that genes are made of **DNA**, a fierce race began to solve a physical puzzle: **how are the atoms inside a DNA molecule arranged in three-dimensional space, and how can a molecule built from just four kinds of bases store thousands of different protein recipes?**

Start with what chemists already knew about the building blocks of DNA. DNA is a long biological polymer (macromolecule, Chapter 4) assembled from repeating monomer units called **nucleotides**. Each DNA nucleotide consists of three parts joined together:

1. A **phosphate group** (which gives DNA its acidic nature and negative electrical charge),
2. A five-carbon sugar called **deoxyribose** (which joins with alternating phosphate groups to form the strong outer **sugar–phosphate backbone** of each strand), and
3. One of four nitrogen-containing organic **bases** projecting inward from the sugar:
   - **Adenine (`A`)** — a larger, **two-ring** base (a **purine**),
   - **Guanine (`G`)** — a larger, **two-ring** base (also a **purine**),
   - **Thymine (`T`)** — a smaller, **one-ring** base (a **pyrimidine**), or
   - **Cytosine (`C`)** — a smaller, **one-ring** base (also a **pyrimidine**).

Look closely at the three experimental clues that converged between 1950 and 1953 to reveal how two DNA strands lock together (**0610 `4.1 Core & Supplement`; `17.1 Core 3`**):

- **Clue 1 · Erwin Chargaff's base-equivalence rules (New York, 1950):**
  Using paper chromatography and ultraviolet light, **Erwin Chargaff** measured the exact percentages of `A`, `T`, `G`, and `C` in DNA extracted from humans, cattle, salmon, maize, yeast, and bacteria. Across every species, he found that the percentage of **adenine always equalled thymine (`%A ≈ %T`)** and the percentage of **guanine always equalled cytosine (`%G ≈ %C`)**. For example, in human thymus DNA, Chargaff measured **`30.9% A` and `29.4% T`**, alongside **`19.9% G` and `19.8% C`**. In the GC-rich bacterium *Sarcina lutea*, `%A` was only `13.4%` and `%T` was `13.1%`, while `%G` was `37.1%` and `%C` was `36.4%`—yet `%A` still matched `%T` and `%G` still matched `%C`.
- **Clue 2 · Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's Photo 51 (London, May 1952):**
  At King's College London, the physical chemist **Rosalind Franklin** and her graduate student **Raymond Gosling** shone a narrow beam of X-rays through a single microscopic fibre of calf-thymus DNA held at **92 percent relative humidity** (the hydrated **B-form** that DNA takes inside living cells). After a 62-hour exposure, their film—**Photo 51**—revealed a sharp **X-shaped cross of dark spots**. In X-ray crystallography, an X-shaped diffraction pattern is the unmistakable signature of a **helix** (a spiral coil). From the spacing of the horizontal layer lines and the dark arcs at the very top and bottom of **Photo 51**, **Rosalind Franklin** measured the exact geometry of the B-DNA helix:
  - One complete helical turn (`360°`) repeats every **`3.4 nm`** (`34 Å`) along the fibre axis.
  - The vertical step from one stacked base to the next is **`0.34 nm`** (`3.4 Å`). Dividing `3.4 nm` by `0.34 nm` proves there are **approximately `10` base pairs per turn in this historical fibre model** of the helix (each base pair rotated by `36°` relative to its neighbour).
  - The cylinder has a uniform diameter of **`2.0 nm`** (`20 Å`), with the water-loving **sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside** and the bases stacked flat like steps on the inside.
- **Clue 3 · James Watson and Francis Crick's complementary base-pairing model (Cambridge, April 1953):**
  At the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, **Francis Crick** and **James Watson** combined Franklin's B-DNA X-ray dimensions and antiparallel chain symmetry with Jerry Donohue's keto base chemistry and **Erwin Chargaff**'s ratios. They realised why the double helix has a completely uniform **`2.0 nm`** diameter from end to end:

    - If you try to pair two large **two-ring purines** (`A` with `G`, or `A` with `A`), the rung is **too wide (`> 2.0 nm`)** and forces a bulge in the sugar–phosphate backbone.
    - If you try to pair two small **one-ring pyrimidines** (`T` with `C`, or `T` with `T`), the rung is **too narrow (`< 2.0 nm`)** and the bases cannot reach across the cylinder to form hydrogen bonds.
    - Only when a **two-ring purine** pairs with a **one-ring pyrimidine** does the pairing maintain the **approximately `2.0 nm` overall helix diameter**—and the hydrogen-bond positions on the rings allow only two specific partnerships: **Adenine (`A`) always pairs with Thymine (`T`)** via **two hydrogen bonds (`A=T`)**, and **Cytosine (`C`) always pairs with Guanine (`G`)** via **three hydrogen bonds (`C≡G`)**.
:::

:::predict
Suppose a biochemist analyses a double-stranded DNA molecule from a newly discovered deep-sea fish and finds that **22% of all the bases in the DNA are cytosine (`C`)**. Using complementary base pairing and Chargaff's rules, what are the percentages of **guanine (`G`)**, **adenine (`A`)**, and **thymine (`T`)** in that DNA molecule?
- 22% G, 22% A, and 34% T, because C, G and A are always equal
- 28% G, 25% A, and 25% T, because C and G always add up to 50%
- 22% G, 28% A, and 28% T, because C always pairs with G (so %G = %C = 22%, totalling 44%), and the remaining 56% of bases must be split equally between A and T pairs (%A = %T = 28%)
???
The third choice. Work through the two-step arithmetic:

1. Because every **cytosine (`C`)** on one strand is paired across the rung with a **guanine (`G`)** on the opposite strand (`C≡G`), `%G` must equal `%C = 22%`. Together, `C + G` account for `22% + 22% = 44%` of all bases.
2. That leaves `100% − 44% = 56%` of the bases for `A` and `T`. Because every **adenine (`A`)** pairs with a **thymine (`T`)** (`A=T`), `%A` must equal `%T = 56% ÷ 2 = 28%`. Check the total: `22% C + 22% G + 28% A + 28% T = 100%`.
:::

:::evidence London and Cambridge, 25 April 1953: Photo 51 and the double-helix papers in Nature | Crystallographic & chemical proof
In the 25 April 1953 issue of *Nature*, three back-to-back papers laid out the structure of DNA: **James Watson and Francis Crick**'s model paper (*"Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid"*), **Maurice Wilkins, Alec Stokes, and Herbert Wilson**'s fibre diffraction paper, and **Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling**'s paper (*"Molecular Configuration in Sodium Thymonucleate"*), which published **Photo 51** and proved that the phosphate groups lie on the outside of a hydrated **`2.0 nm`** B-form helix with **`3.4 nm`** turns and **`0.34 nm`** vertical spacing between stacked bases.

Notice the two biological problems that complementary base pairing (`A=T` and `C≡G`) solved immediately:

1. **Unrestricted information storage along one strand:** The chemical geometry of the double helix places **zero restriction** on the order of bases (`A, T, C, G`) running lengthwise along a single strand—a gene can spell `G–T–G–C–A–C–C–T–G...` or any other sequence of thousands of letters.
2. **Automatic complementarity between the two strands:** Once the sequence of bases along Strand 1 is fixed, the sequence of bases along Strand 2 is automatically determined (`A` opposite `T`, `T` opposite `A`, `C` opposite `G`, `G` opposite `C`). As Watson and Crick noted in a famous one-sentence understatement near the end of their paper, this complementary pairing immediately suggests how the genetic material can be copied before a cell divides (which you will trace in Chapter 12).

Summarise the four essential structural features of a **DNA molecule** that you need for **Cambridge IGCSE Biology (`0610 4.1`)**:

1. **Two strands coiled together to form a ==double helix==:** imagine a flexible rope ladder whose two side rails are twisted around a central axis into a spiral staircase (`2.0 nm` wide, completing one full turn every `3.4 nm` or `10` base pairs). The two sugar–phosphate strands run in opposite directions (**antiparallel**, one running `5' → 3'` and its partner running `3' → 5'`).
2. **Each strand contains chemicals called ==bases==:** projecting inward from the sugar–phosphate backbone of each strand is a linear sequence of four bases—**adenine (`A`)**, **thymine (`T`)**, **cytosine (`C`)**, and **guanine (`G`)**.
3. **Cross-links (bonds) between the strands are formed by pairs of bases:** the two strands are held together across the middle of the helix by **hydrogen bonds** between paired bases (forming the rungs of the twisted ladder).
4. **The bases always pair up in the same complementary way (`A` with `T`, and `C` with `G`):** **adenine (`A`) always pairs with thymine (`T`)** (`2` hydrogen bonds), and **cytosine (`C`) always pairs with guanine (`G`)** (`3` hydrogen bonds).

:::note DNA double helix and complementary base pairing
- **Double helix:** the three-dimensional structure of DNA, consisting of **two strands coiled together** around a common axis, with sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside and paired **bases** forming cross-links on the inside.
- **Complementary base pairing:** the invariant rule by which DNA bases form cross-links between the two strands: **A (adenine) always pairs with T (thymine)**, and **C (cytosine) always pairs with G (guanine)**.
:::

:::five The DNA double helix and complementary base pairing
? What is it?
A two-nanometre-wide twisted ladder (**double helix**) made of **two sugar–phosphate strands coiled together**, linked across the middle by pairs of chemical **bases** that obey a strict partnership rule: **A always pairs with T (`A=T`), and C always pairs with G (`C≡G`)**.
? Why is it called this?
*Helix* means a three-dimensional spiral curve (like a corkscrew or spiral staircase); *double* helix because **two** polynucleotide strands wind around the same axis; *complementary* (with an *e*, from "complement", meaning matching counterpart) because each base's shape and hydrogen-bonding groups fit only its specific partner.
? What problem did it solve?
A genetic archive must satisfy two demands that seem to pull in opposite directions: its **outer geometry must be completely regular** so it can pack neatly into chromosomes inside a 6 µm nucleus, yet its **inner letter sequence must be completely free** to spell out any protein recipe. Putting the sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside (`2.0 nm` apart) and pairing a two-ring purine with a one-ring pyrimidine on the inside (`A=T`, `C≡G`) satisfies both demands at once.
? Why does it work?
Standard complementary pairing maintains an **approximately `2.0 nm` overall helix diameter**, so any sequence of bases can run along one strand without bulging or pinching the helix, while weak individual hydrogen bonds (`2` per `A=T`, `3` per `C≡G`) add up over millions of rungs to hold the two strands firmly together at 37 °C yet still allow enzymes to unzip a single gene when needed.
? How does it help?
If you know the sequence of bases on one strand of a DNA double helix (for example, `5'-G–T–G–C–A–C–C–T–G-3'`), you can write down the exact complementary sequence on the opposite strand (`3'-C–A–C–G–T–G–G–A–C-5'`) and calculate the percentages of all four bases from just one base percentage.
?? How do we know?
**Erwin Chargaff** (1950) proved chemically that `%A = %T` and `%G = %C` across all double-stranded cellular DNA; **Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling**'s **Photo 51** (1952) proved the B-DNA helix has a **`3.4 nm` pitch** and **`0.34 nm` base step** (`10` base pairs per turn).
?? What it isn't
`A` never pairs with `C` or `G` in normal DNA, and the genetic information does **not** lie in the sugar–phosphate backbone (which is identical in every gene and every organism)—the genetic information lies in the **sequence (order) of bases along the length of a strand**.
?? Where does it stop working?
A few tiny single-stranded DNA viruses (such as bacteriophage φX174) carry only **one** strand of DNA inside their protein coat instead of a double helix; in those single-stranded viral genomes, `%A` does not have to equal `%T` until the virus synthesises its complementary second strand inside a host cell.
:::

:::careful
> "Different organisms have different proteins because their DNA has different sugar–phosphate backbones or pairs A with G instead of A with T."

Neither is true. From a banana tree to an *E. coli* bacterium to your own neurones, **every DNA double helix uses the exact same deoxyribose sugar–phosphate backbone and the exact same complementary base-pairing rule (`A` with `T`, `C` with `G`)**. What makes a human insulin gene different from a human beta-globin gene—or a human different from a banana—is the **sequence (order) of bases (`A, T, C, G`) running lengthwise along the DNA strands** of each gene (`0610 17.1 Core 3`).
:::

## 11.3 Cracking the triplet cipher and printing the protein: from mRNA to the ribosome

:::scene The Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, 1961, Bethesda, Maryland, May 1961, and Madison, Wisconsin, 1964–1968

Solving the double helix in 1953 revealed how the master instruction book is bound, but it immediately exposed two new puzzles:

- **The coding puzzle:** DNA has an alphabet of only **four bases** (`A`, `T`, `C`, `G`), whereas proteins are built from **twenty different amino acids** (Chapter 4). How can a four-letter alphabet spell out twenty different amino-acid names?
- **The location puzzle:** In every eukaryotic plant, animal and fungal cell, the chromosomes and their **genes stay locked inside the nucleus** (`11.1`), whereas the **ribosomes**—the tiny molecular machines that actually link amino acids together into proteins (Chapter 2)—sit **outside the nucleus in the cytoplasm** (and on the rough endoplasmic reticulum). If the DNA gene never leaves the nucleus, how does the instruction reach the ribosome?

Take the coding puzzle first. Imagine trying to write a code for **20 different amino acids** using an alphabet of **4 letters**:

- If each code word is **1 base long (a singlet code)**, there are only **`4¹ = 4`** possible words (`A`, `T`, `C`, `G`)—leaving 16 amino acids with no code at all.
- If each code word is **2 bases long (a doublet code)**, there are **`4² = 4 × 4 = 16`** possible two-letter words (`AA`, `AT`, `AC`, `AG`, and so on)—still 4 short of the 20 amino acids.
- If each code word is **3 bases long (a ==triplet== or ==codon==)**, there are **`4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64`** possible three-letter words—more than enough to specify all **20 amino acids** plus "stop" punctuation marks at the end of a gene.
:::

:::predict
Because **`4³ = 64`** three-letter triplets are available to code for only **20 amino acids** (plus 3 stop signals), which description matches the experimentally established standard code’s relationship between triplets (codons) and amino acids?
- 44 of the 64 triplets are unused blanks that pause the ribosome, while each of the 20 amino acids has only 1 triplet
- Each single triplet codes for 3 different amino acids at random, so the same gene makes many different proteins
- Most amino acids are coded for by more than one triplet (for example, 6 different triplets code for leucine), while every individual triplet specifies one unambiguous meaning (either one specific amino acid or STOP)
???
The third choice. Biologists call this property of the genetic code **degeneracy** (or **redundancy**):

- **Unambiguous in the forward direction (`codon → amino acid`):** Every one of the 61 sense codons specifies **one and only one amino acid** (for instance, `GAG` always specifies glutamic acid, never valine or leucine).
- **Degenerate / redundant in the reverse direction (`amino acid → codons`):** Because 61 sense codons share 20 amino acids, most amino acids have **2, 3, 4, or 6 synonymous codons** (usually differing at the third base of the triplet). As you will see in Section 11.4 and the end-of-chapter case, this degeneracy shields living cells against many single-base mutations.
:::

:::evidence Cambridge & Bethesda (1961) and Madison (1964–1968): Crick & Brenner, Nirenberg, and Har Gobind Khorana | Genetic & synthetic biochemical proof
Three classic experiments proved that the genetic code is a **non-overlapping triplet code** and deciphered all 64 codons:

1. **Crick, Brenner, Barnett, and Watts-Tobin (1961) — the `+1 / +2 / +3` frameshift proof:**
   Using chemical mutagens that insert (`+`) or delete (`−`) single bases in the `rII` gene of bacteriophage T4, **Francis Crick** and **Sydney Brenner** showed that a ribosome reads a gene from a fixed starting point in **non-overlapping groups of three bases**. If you represent a triplet message with three-letter words—say, `CAT · CAT · CAT · CAT · CAT`—inserting **one extra letter (`+1`, `X`)** shifts every triplet boundary after that point (`CAX · TCA · TCA · TCA...`), scrambling every downstream amino acid and destroying the protein. Inserting **two letters (`+2`)** (`CAX · YTC · ATC · ATC...`) leaves the downstream frame scrambled. Only when they combined **three single-base insertions (`+3`)** (or three deletions, `−3`) did the triplets after the third change snap back into their original reading frame (`CAX · YZT · CAT · CAT...`), restoring a working phage protein.
2. **Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei (May 1961) — cracking the first codon with `poly-U`:**
   At the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda, **Marshall Nirenberg** and Heinrich Matthaei added a synthetic RNA chain composed entirely of uracil bases (**`UUUUUUUUUUUU...`**, `poly-U`) to a cell-free *E. coli* ribosome extract containing amino acids and energy (`ATP`). The ribosomes assembled a polypeptide chain made purely of **phenylalanine (`Phe–Phe–Phe...`)**, proving that the RNA triplet **`UUU` codes for phenylalanine (`Phe`)**.
3. **Har Gobind Khorana (1964–1968) — synthetic repeating copolymers crack the full 64-codon dictionary:**
   At the University of Wisconsin–Madison, **Har Gobind Khorana** (born in Raipur, Punjab and trained at Punjab University in Lahore) solved the hardest part of the cipher: determining the **exact order of letters inside mixed triplets**. By chemically synthesising short DNA templates of known repeating sequence and copying them with polymerases into long repeating **mRNA** chains, Khorana fed ribosomes messages whose triplets alternated in a mathematically known way:
   - Repeating dinucleotide **`(UC)ₙ = UCUCUCUCUCUC...`** is read in triplets as alternating **`UCU | CUC | UCU | CUC`**. Ribosomes printed strictly alternating **serine–leucine–serine–leucine (`Ser–Leu–Ser–Leu`)**, proving `UCU = Ser` and `CUC = Leu`.
   - Repeating dinucleotide **`(UG)ₙ = UGUGUGUGUGUG...`** (`UGU | GUG`) yielded alternating **cysteine–valine (`Cys–Val–Cys–Val`)**; **`(AG)ₙ`** (`AGA | GAG`) yielded alternating **arginine–glutamic acid (`Arg–Glu–Arg–Glu`)**; **`(AC)ₙ`** (`ACA | CAC`) yielded alternating **threonine–histidine (`Thr–His–Thr–His`)**.
   - Synthetic templates containing **`UAA`, `UAG`, and `UGA`** proved that these three triplets code for **no amino acid at all**—they are the three **stop (nonsense) codons** that signal the ribosome to release the finished polypeptide chain, matching **Obaid Siddiqi**'s genetic discovery of nonsense codons (Chapter 9).
:::

Now solve the **location puzzle**: how does a gene sitting inside the **nucleus** direct a **ribosome** sitting out in the **cytoplasm** to assemble a specific protein?

Think of the DNA chromosomes in the nucleus as a **priceless, irreplaceable master reference encyclopaedia** kept inside a locked archive room. If the cell dragged an entire chromosome out into the busy cytoplasm every time a ribosome needed to print a few molecules of amylase or insulin, cytoplasmic enzymes and reactive chemicals would quickly nick and damage the master DNA. Instead, the cell keeps the master **gene safely inside the nucleus**, makes a short, disposable working copy of just that one gene in the form of ==messenger RNA (mRNA)==, and sends the **mRNA** copy out through a microscopic doorway (**nuclear pore**) in the nuclear membrane to feed through the **ribosomes** in the cytoplasm.

Our beta-globin excerpts use the traditional mature-protein numbering: an initial methionine is removed, leaving valine as residue 1. They are not complete messages beginning at the AUG start codon. Human RNA is processed before export: introns are removed and coding portions joined.

Trace the **six-step protein synthesis pathway** required by **Cambridge IGCSE Biology (`0610 17.1 Supplement 5`)** in Figure 11.4:

1. **The gene coding for the protein remains in the nucleus:**
   When a cell needs to make a specific protein (such as human beta-globin or insulin), enzymes inside the nucleus locate that single **gene** on its chromosome and temporarily unzip the hydrogen bonds between the two DNA strands across just that gene. The master DNA gene itself **never leaves the nucleus**.
2. **Messenger RNA (`mRNA`) is a copy of a gene:**
   Inside the nucleus, the enzyme **RNA polymerase** moves along the unzipped DNA template strand and links free RNA nucleotides into a **single-stranded ==messenger RNA (mRNA)==** molecule whose base sequence is a complementary copy of the template strand (and therefore matches the coding strand of the gene, except that RNA uses the base **uracil (`U`)** in place of thymine (`T`)). For example, where the coding strand of the human beta-globin gene reads **`G–T–G · C–A–C · C–T–G · A–C–T · C–C–T · G–A–G · G–A–G`** (and the template strand reads `C–A–C · G–T–G · G–A–C · T–G–A · G–G–A · C–T–C · C–T–C`), the transcribed **mRNA** copy reads **`G–U–G · C–A–C · C–U–G · A–C–U · C–C–U · G–A–G · G–A–G`**. Making this mRNA copy from a DNA gene in the nucleus is called **transcription**.
3. **mRNA molecules are made in the nucleus and move to the cytoplasm:**
   Once the mRNA copy of the gene is complete, it detaches from the DNA (which zips right back up into its protected double helix) and **moves out of the nucleus through a nuclear pore into the cytoplasm**.
4. **The mRNA passes through ribosomes:**
   In the cytoplasm (or on the rough endoplasmic reticulum), a **ribosome** clamps onto the start of the mRNA strand (`AUG`) and **the mRNA passes through the ribosome** like a punched tape, three bases (one triplet **codon**) at a time.
5. **The ribosome assembles amino acids into protein molecules:**
   As each three-base mRNA codon sits inside the ribosome's reading slot, small adapter molecules in the cytoplasm called **transfer RNA (`tRNA`)**—each carrying one specific amino acid at one end and a complementary three-base **anticodon** loop at the other—match up with the mRNA codon. The **ribosome links each incoming amino acid to the growing chain by forming a peptide bond**, assembling the amino acids one by one into a long polypeptide (**protein**) molecule. Reading the mRNA codon sequence at the ribosome to build a polypeptide chain is called **translation**.
6. **The specific sequence of amino acids is determined by the sequence of bases in the mRNA:**
   Because each three-base codon on the mRNA specifies one exact amino acid (`GUG → Valine`, `CAC → Histidine`, `CUG → Leucine`, `ACU → Threonine`, `CCU → Proline`, `GAG → Glutamic acid`), **the sequence of bases in the DNA gene determines the sequence of bases in the mRNA, which in turn determines the specific sequence of amino acids in the protein**. When the ribosome reaches a **stop codon** (`UAA`, `UAG`, or `UGA`), it releases the completed polypeptide chain.

:::note Messenger RNA (mRNA) and ribosomes in protein synthesis
- **Messenger RNA (`mRNA`):** a single-stranded nucleic acid molecule that is **made in the nucleus as a copy of one gene** (using bases `A, U, C, G`) and **moves to the cytoplasm**, where it **passes through ribosomes** to direct protein synthesis.
- **Ribosome:** the cytoplasmic organelle through which **mRNA passes**; the ribosome reads the **sequence of bases in the mRNA** three bases (one codon) at a time and **assembles amino acids into protein molecules** in the exact order specified by the mRNA.
:::

Why does controlling the **sequence of amino acids** allow DNA to control **everything a living cell does** (**0610 `17.1 Supplement 4`**)?

Follow the causal chain in Figure 11.5 back to **G. N. Ramachandran**'s discovery in Chapter 4 (story `p-ramachandran`): as soon as a ribosome links amino acids into a polypeptide chain, the chemical environment, molecular helpers, sizes, electrical charges, and water-loving (**hydrophilic**) or water-repelling (**hydrophobic**) side chains of those amino acids constrain the backbone angles (`φ, ψ`) and favour a working range of three-dimensional conformations held together by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges. That three-dimensional shape determines the working pocket or channel of every protein machine in the cell:

1. **Enzymes (biological catalysts, Chapter 4):**
   DNA codes for intracellular and extracellular enzymes such as **amylase**, **pepsin**, **catalase**, and **DNA/RNA polymerases**. The amino-acid sequence folds the enzyme's **3D active site** so it is complementary to its specific substrate. By controlling which enzymes a cell builds, DNA controls every metabolic chemical reaction in the cell.
2. **Membrane carriers and channel proteins (Chapters 2, 3, 9, and 10):**
   DNA codes for the **carrier proteins** that perform active transport and facilitated diffusion across cell membranes—such as the **`Na⁺`–glucose cotransporters** in your small intestine and kidney nephrons, **Peter Agre**'s **aquaporins** (`p-agre`), and **Obaid Siddiqi**'s voltage-gated **sodium channels** (`siddiqi-para-channels`). If a gene changes the amino-acid sequence of a membrane carrier, the cell can no longer pump or gate that ion or molecule properly.
3. **Receptors for neurotransmitters and hormones (Chapters 9 and 10):**
   DNA codes for the **receptor proteins** embedded in postsynaptic neurone membranes (which bind **neurotransmitters** diffusing across a synaptic gap to trigger the next impulse) and in target-organ membranes (such as liver **insulin** and **glucagon** receptors). The 3D binding cleft of each receptor must be complementary to its chemical messenger.
4. **Antibodies and structural/transport proteins (Chapters 8 and 10):**
   DNA codes for **antibodies** secreted by lymphocytes (whose variable tips fold into a 3D shape complementary to one specific pathogen **antigen**), oxygen-carrying **haemoglobin** in red blood cells, and structural fibres like **collagen** and **keratin**.

:::five Transcription (mRNA copy in nucleus) and translation (protein assembly at the ribosome)
? What is it?
The two-stage pipeline by which a eukaryotic cell builds a protein from a gene: first making a single-stranded **mRNA copy of the gene inside the nucleus**, and then running that **mRNA through a ribosome in the cytoplasm** to link **amino acids** into a protein in the exact order specified by the mRNA bases.
? Why is it called this?
*Transcription* means copying a message within the same language (4-letter nucleotide script in **DNA** copied into 4-letter nucleotide script in **mRNA**); *translation* means converting a message into a completely different language (4-letter nucleotide **codons** translated into the 20-letter **amino-acid** alphabet of proteins).
? What problem did it solve?
It allows a cell to print thousands of copies of a needed protein (like insulin after a mango meal or antibodies during an infection) using dozens of cytoplasmic ribosomes simultaneously, **without ever taking the master DNA chromosome out of the protective nucleus**.
? Why does it work?
Complementary base pairing ensures that the **mRNA** made in the nucleus faithfully preserves the gene's base sequence (`U` replacing `T`), and complementary codon–anticodon pairing inside the **ribosome** ensures that each three-base mRNA triplet inserts the exact right **amino acid**.
? How does it help?
It explains how every protein machine you met in Chapters 4–10—**enzymes**, **membrane carriers**, **receptors for neurotransmitters**, and **antibodies**—is built from the cell's DNA archive.
?? How do we know?
Radioactive pulse-chase experiments in the 1950s and 1960s (using radioactive uridine to label newly made RNA) showed that RNA is synthesised first inside the **nucleus** and then migrates into the **cytoplasm** to associate with **ribosomes**, where radioactive amino acids are incorporated into growing polypeptide chains.
?? What it isn't
The DNA gene does **not** travel to the ribosome, and mRNA is **not** converted chemically into amino acids—mRNA is the instruction tape that passes through the ribosome while free amino acids from the cytoplasm are linked together by peptide bonds.
?? Where does it stop working?
If a toxin (such as α-amanitin from the death-cap mushroom *Amanita phalloides*) blocks **RNA polymerase** in the nucleus, the cell cannot transcribe new **mRNA** copies of its genes, and protein synthesis grinds to a halt even though the DNA genes and cytoplasmic ribosomes are still intact.

## 11.4 One letter changed: beta-globin, silent triplets and why sequence controls shape

:::scene The Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, 1956–1957, and a haematology laboratory in Mumbai

Now put the entire causal spine of Chapter 11—**DNA base sequence → mRNA codon sequence → amino-acid sequence → 3D folded protein shape → cellular function**—to a quantitative test on a single human protein you already know from Chapter 8: **haemoglobin**.

Each red blood cell in your bloodstream is packed with roughly 280 million molecules of **adult haemoglobin (`HbA`)**. Each haemoglobin molecule is a four-subunit protein tetramer made of two **alpha-globin (`α`)** polypeptide chains (141 amino acids each) and two **beta-globin (`β`)** polypeptide chains (**146 amino acids** each, specified by `146 × 3 = 438` coding bases for the **mature beta-globin sequence**, excluding the removable initiator methionine and stop codon, in the **`HBB` gene** on human chromosome 11), with each subunit cradling an iron-containing **haem** group (`Fe²⁺`) that reversibly binds oxygen (`O₂`).

In **sickle-cell anaemia**, a person's red blood cells produce an altered version of haemoglobin called **sickle-cell haemoglobin (`HbS`)**. When `HbS` carries oxygen in the lungs and arteries, it stays dissolved. But when `HbS` releases its oxygen in respiring capillaries where oxygen concentration is low, the deoxygenated `HbS` molecules stick to one another head-to-tail, zipping up into long, rigid insoluble rods that stretch the flexible biconcave red blood cell into a stiff crescent (**sickle**) shape that can jam narrow capillaries and cause painful tissue oxygen starvation.

In 1949, Linus Pauling, Harvey Itano, S. J. Singer, and Ibert Wells showed that `HbS` moves more slowly toward the positive electrode than normal `HbA` during electrophoresis, proving that `HbS` carries a different electrical charge. But out of 146 amino acids in each beta-globin chain, *how many amino acids are different between normal `HbA` and sickle-cell `HbS`?*

In 1956 and 1957 at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, the biochemist **Vernon Ingram** answered that question by inventing **peptide fingerprinting**. He used the protease **trypsin** (Chapter 7) to snip both normal `HbA` and sickle-cell `HbS` beta-globin chains into 26 short peptide pieces, separated the 26 spots on filter paper using electric current in one direction and chromatography solvent in the other, and sequenced the **single peptide spot (#4)** that shifted position.

Instead of inserting **glutamic acid (`Glu⁶`)**—whose side chain carries a negative electrical charge (`−COO⁻`) and loves water (**hydrophilic**)—the ribosome inserts **valine (`Val⁶`)**, whose hydrocarbon side chain carries zero charge and repels water (**hydrophobic**).
:::

:::evidence Cambridge, 1956–1957: Vernon Ingram's peptide fingerprint of beta-globin Codon 6 | Molecular sequence proof
In his 1956 and 1957 *Nature* papers, **Vernon Ingram** proved for the first time in the history of biology that a human hereditary condition results from changing **a single amino acid** in a protein molecule:

- **Normal adult beta-globin (`HbA`, Codons 1–7):**
  - Coding DNA: **`5'- G T G · C A C · C T G · A C T · C C T · G A G · G A G -3'`**
  - mRNA codons: **`5'- G U G · C A C · C U G · A C U · C C U · G A G · G A G -3'`**
  - Amino acids 1–7: **`Val¹ – His² – Leu³ – Thr⁴ – Pro⁵ – Glu⁶ – Glu⁷`**
  - At position 6, **glutamic acid (`Glu⁶`)** sits on the outside of the folded beta-globin subunit; its negatively charged, **hydrophilic** side chain interacts smoothly with surrounding water molecules in the red blood cell cytoplasm so `HbA` tetramers remain separate and soluble.
- **Sickle-cell beta-globin (`HbS`, Codons 1–7):**
  - Coding DNA: **`5'- G T G · C A C · C T G · A C T · C C T · G T G · G A G -3'`** *(single base change at Codon 6: `GAG → GTG`)*
  - mRNA codons: **`5'- G U G · C A C · C U G · A C U · C C U · G U G · G A G -3'`** *(mRNA Codon 6: `GAG → GUG`)*
  - Amino acids 1–7: **`Val¹ – His² – Leu³ – Thr⁴ – Pro⁵ – Val⁶ – Glu⁷`** *(Residue 6: `Glu⁶ → Val⁶`)*
  - At position 6, **valine (`Val⁶`)** puts an oily, non-polar **hydrophobic patch** on the outer surface of the beta-globin subunit. When `HbS` releases oxygen in tissue capillaries, that sticky hydrophobic `Val⁶` patch locks into a complementary hydrophobic pocket on a neighbouring `HbS` tetramer, zipping millions of `HbS` molecules into stiff polymers.
:::

Now ask a subtler question that tests whether you truly understand how the **64-codon triplet dictionary** works. Does *every* single-base substitution in the coding sequence of a gene alter the protein?

Compare the **three possible molecular outcomes** of substituting a single base inside a protein-coding gene, shown side by side in Figure 11.8:

1. **Outcome A · ==Silent (synonymous) substitution== — no change in the amino-acid sequence:**
   Look at **Codon 3** of the normal human beta-globin gene (`GTG · CAC · CTG · ACT · CCT · GAG · GAG`), where the coding DNA triplet **`CTG`** is transcribed into the mRNA codon **`CUG`**, coding for the third amino acid, **leucine (`Leu³`)**. Suppose a single-base substitution changes the third base of Codon 3 from **`G` to `T`** on the coding DNA strand (**`CTG → CTT`**), so the mRNA codon changes from **`CUG` to `CUU`**.
   When the ribosome reads **`CUU`**, which amino acid does it insert? Because the 64-codon genetic code is **degenerate**, **six different mRNA codons (`UUA, UUG, CUU, CUC, CUA, CUG`) all code for leucine (`Leu`)**. The ribosome still inserts **leucine (`Leu³`)** at position 3, the 146-amino-acid sequence (`Val¹–His²–Leu³–Thr⁴–Pro⁵–Glu⁶–Glu⁷...`) is **100 percent identical** to normal beta-globin, RNA processing, production and folding are assumed unaffected in this example, and the red blood cell carries **the usual beta-globin amino-acid sequence**.
2. **Outcome B · ==Missense substitution== — one amino acid replaced by a different amino acid:**
   When a single-base substitution changes a codon into a codon that specifies a **different amino acid**—as in beta-globin **Codon 6 (`GAG → GTG`, mRNA `GAG → GUG`, `Glu⁶ → Val⁶`)**—the ribosome prints a full-length polypeptide chain with **one altered amino acid**. If the new amino acid has a very different charge, size, or hydropathy (like hydrophobic valine replacing hydrophilic glutamic acid, or if the change sits right inside an enzyme's active site or a voltage-gated ion channel), the 3D surface or folding of the protein changes.
3. **Outcome C · ==Nonsense (stop) substitution== — premature chain termination:**
   When a single-base substitution turns an amino-acid-coding triplet into one of the three **stop codons (`UAA`, `UAG`, or `UGA`)**—for example, changing beta-globin **Codon 39** from coding DNA **`CAG`** (mRNA `CAG`, which codes for **glutamine, `Gln³⁹`**) to **`TAG`** (mRNA **`UAG`**, an amber **stop codon**)—the ribosome halts translation at residue 38 and releases a short, incomplete polypeptide fragment missing more than 70 percent of its amino acids. That truncated fragment cannot fold into a working globin subunit (reducing functional beta-globin; the clinical severity depends on both inherited copies), just as **Obaid Siddiqi**'s nonsense mutations halted phage and bacterial protein synthesis in Chapter 9.

:::five Why one base change can be silent, missense, or nonsense
? What is it?
The three different ways a **single-base substitution** in a DNA gene can affect the protein printed by a ribosome: **synonymous** (same amino acid; other biological effects remain possible), **missense** (one amino acid swapped for another), or **nonsense** (an amino-acid codon turned into a premature **STOP** codon).
? Why is it called this?
*Silent* (or *synonymous*) because the new triplet is a synonym for the exact same amino acid, leaving the amino-acid sequence unchanged; *missense* because one triplet now sends a different amino-acid meaning; *nonsense* (coined by Brenner, Crick and Siddiqi's generation) because a stop codon (`UAA, UAG, UGA`) specifies no amino acid at all.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why some DNA spelling changes leave the amino-acid sequence unchanged while others change a protein's chemistry or length. A synonymous change can still affect RNA processing or the amount of protein made; the codon table alone does not establish whether it is harmless. The beta-globin Codon 6 missense change and Codon 39 stop change show how different the consequences can be.
? Why does it work?
A ribosome does not inspect the DNA directly; it reads **three-base mRNA codons** using the 64-codon table. If the mutated mRNA codon maps to the **same amino acid** (`CUG → CUU = Leu`), the polypeptide chain has the exact same side chains and has the same amino-acid sequence; normal folding is assumed here. If it maps to a **different amino acid** (`GAG → GUG`, `Glu → Val`) or to **STOP** (`CAG → UAG`), the polypeptide's side-chain chemistry or length changes.
? How does it help?
Whenever you are given a DNA or mRNA base change in a genetics problem, you can trace **`DNA → mRNA codon → amino acid → 3D shape`** step by step instead of assuming that every DNA mutation breaks a protein.
?? How do we know?
**Vernon Ingram**'s (1956–1957) peptide fingerprinting proved the `Glu⁶ → Val⁶` missense change in `HbS`, while **Marshall Nirenberg** and **Har Gobind Khorana**'s 64-codon table explained why third-base changes such as `CUG → CUU` leave the amino acid unchanged.
?? What it isn't
A silent mutation does **not** mean "the cell repaired the DNA back to the original letter"—the DNA base change (`CTG → CTT`) and mRNA change (`CUG → CUU`) are real and permanent, but both codons specify the same amino acid (`Leu`).
?? Where does it stop working?
Unlike a single-base *substitution* (which changes only 1 codon), a single-base **insertion (`+1`) or deletion (`−1`)** inside a coding sequence is almost never silent because it shifts the three-base **reading frame** for every codon downstream (Section 11.3).
:::

:::careful
> "Every mutation in a gene's DNA base sequence changes the amino-acid sequence and alters the 3D shape of the protein."

Not every single-base substitution changes the amino-acid sequence. Because **64 triplets (`4³`)** code for only **20 amino acids** (plus 3 stop signals), most amino acids are specified by several synonymous codons (for example, `CUU`, `CUC`, `CUA`, and `CUG` all code for **leucine**). If a single-base substitution changes one codon into another codon for the **same amino acid** (a **silent / synonymous** change), the **amino-acid sequence is completely unchanged**, but this alone does not establish how much protein is produced or exclude effects on RNA processing.
:::

A paediatric haematology laboratory in Mumbai sequences the first seven codons (**Codons 1 to 7**, 21 bases) of the **beta-globin (`HBB`) gene** from a healthy reference standard and from two twelve-year-old patients, **Child X** and **Child Y**. Remarkably, **both Child X and Child Y have exactly 1 base out of the 21 bases changed** compared with the standard reference sequence:

A relevant excerpt of the standard **mRNA genetic code dictionary** is provided alongside the sequencing report:

- `GUG`, `GUU`, `GUC`, `GUA` = **Valine (`Val`)** *(neutral, hydrophobic side chain)*
- `CAC`, `CAU` = **Histidine (`His`)**
- `CUG`, `CUU`, `CUC`, `CUA`, `UUA`, `UUG` = **Leucine (`Leu`)** *(hydrophobic interior residue)*

A classmate looks at the table and says: *"The laboratory must have mixed up Child X's blood sample. Child X has a one-letter DNA mutation (`G → T`) in the beta-globin gene just like Child Y (`A → T`), so Child X's ribosomes must also be printing a mutated, abnormally folded beta-globin protein."*

Use the molecular mechanisms of Sections 11.2, 11.3, and 11.4 to prove why the laboratory did **not** mix up the samples:

1. For both **Child X** and **Child Y**, write down **(a)** the complementary **template DNA strand (`3' → 5'`)** inside the nucleus and **(b)** the **mRNA codon sequence (`5' → 3'`)** that moves through a nuclear pore to pass through the **ribosomes** in the cytoplasm.
2. Translate both **mRNA** sequences into their seven-amino-acid chains (`Residues 1–7`) using the codon table, and explain why **Child X's single-base DNA mutation leaves the amino-acid sequence unchanged** (with normal RNA processing, production and folding assumed in this case), whereas **Child Y's single-base DNA mutation alters the 3D surface behavior of haemoglobin**.
3. Suppose a third patient, **Child Z**, had a single base **deleted (`−1`)** from **Codon 3** (so Codon 3 lost its `G` and became `CT`, instead of `G` being substituted by `T`). Would Child Z's mutation also be silent like Child X's? Explain why or why not using **Francis Crick and Sydney Brenner's (1961)** discovery.
???

1. **Template DNA strands (`3' → 5'`) in the nucleus and transcribed mRNA codons (`5' → 3'`) in the cytoplasm:**
   Using DNA complementary pairing (`A ↔ T`, `C ↔ G`) for the template strand, and RNA transcription (`U` replacing `T` of the coding strand) for the **mRNA** copy that moves from the nucleus to the cytoplasmic ribosomes:
   - **Child X:**
     - Template DNA (`3' → 5'`): **`3'- CAC · GTG · GAA · TGA · GGA · CTC · CTC -5'`** *(Codon 3 template changed from `GAC` to `GAA`)*
     - Transcribed mRNA (`5' → 3'`): **`5'- GUG · CAC · CUU · ACU · CCU · GAG · GAG -3'`** *(mRNA Codon 3 changed from `CUG` to `CUU`)*
   - **Child Y:**
     - Template DNA (`3' → 5'`): **`3'- CAC · GTG · GAC · TGA · GGA · CAC · CTC -5'`** *(Codon 6 template changed from `CTC` to `CAC`)*
     - Transcribed mRNA (`5' → 3'`): **`5'- GUG · CAC · CUG · ACU · CCU · GUG · GAG -3'`** *(mRNA Codon 6 changed from `GAG` to `GUG`)*

2. **Translation at the ribosome and why Child X is silent while Child Y is missense:**
   - **Child X polypeptide (`Residues 1–7`):** Reading `GUG · CAC · CUU · ACU · CCU · GAG · GAG` at the ribosome gives **`Val¹ – His² – Leu³ – Thr⁴ – Pro⁵ – Glu⁶ – Glu⁷`**. Because the 64-codon genetic code is **degenerate**, both `CUG` (Reference) and `CUU` (Child X) code for the **exact same amino acid: Leucine (`Leu³`)**, and Codon 6 is still `GAG` (**hydrophilic `Glu⁶`**). The displayed change leaves the mature beta-globin amino-acid sequence unchanged (**synonymous substitution**). Under this case's stated assumptions about RNA processing, protein production and folding, the chain supplies normal beta-globin. Ramachandran's work explains constraints on possible backbone conformations; it does not prove that every synonymous DNA change has no biological effect.
   - **Child Y polypeptide (`Residues 1–7`):** Reading `GUG · CAC · CUG · ACU · CCU · GUG · GAG` at the ribosome gives **`Val¹ – His² – Leu³ – Thr⁴ – Pro⁵ – Val⁶ – Glu⁷`**. At **Codon 6**, changing `GAG` to `GUG` is a **missense substitution** that replaces negatively charged, water-loving **glutamic acid (`Glu⁶`)** with neutral, water-repelling **valine (`Val⁶`)**. That exposed **hydrophobic `Val⁶` patch** causes deoxygenated `HbS` molecules to stick together into rigid fibres that distort red blood cells into sickles.

3. **Why a single-base deletion (`−1`) at Codon 3 in Child Z would NOT be silent (Crick & Brenner 1961 frameshift):**
   Deleting the `G` at the end of Codon 3 leaves only 2 bases (`CT`), so the ribosome—which reads mRNA in **non-overlapping triplets without commas**—pulls the first base of Codon 4 into Codon 3 and shifts the **reading frame** of every single triplet downstream (`GTG · CAC · CTA · CTC · CTG · AGG · AG...` → `mRNA GUG · CAC · CUA · CUC · CUG · AGG...`). Instead of `Val–His–Leu–Thr–Pro–Glu–Glu`, Child Z's ribosome prints `Val–His–Leu–Leu–Leu–Arg...`, changing the reading frame and usually many subsequent amino acids, often introducing an early stop and destroying the 3D beta-globin protein (**frameshift mutation**).
