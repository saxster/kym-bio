Chapter 8 left you at the top of a flight of stairs in Mumbai. Within seconds of your climb beginning, your diaphragm and intercostal muscles deepened your breathing, your heart's sinoatrial pacemaker raised its beat rate, arterioles supplying your leg muscles widened, and your blood glucose remained steady even as contracting muscle fibres spent ATP. Thirty trillion cells shifted gear together without colliding into chaos.

An orchestra cannot play in time unless signals pass between the players. Yet the human body does not rely on a single communication network. Touch a hot steel roasting pan (*tawa*) in the kitchen, and your biceps muscle jerks your hand back without waiting for a conscious decision—long before a hormone carried in the blood could travel from your neck to your arm. Hear a stray dog bark suddenly behind you on an evening pavement, and your heart keeps pounding for several minutes after the dog has trotted away—long after the first nerve impulses have ended. To coordinate actions that span milliseconds as well as hours, your body runs two complementary networks side by side: a point-to-point network of **high-speed electrical wiring** (the nervous system) and a blood-borne network of **chemical couriers** (the endocrine system). Even a rooted moong or oat seedling on your windowsill, with no brain, nerves or bloodstream at all, detects the direction of sunlight and gravity and bends its growing stem by redistributing a chemical messenger of its own.

## 9.1 Two ways to coordinate a 30-trillion-cell body: nerves and hormones

:::scene Mumbai, a pavement startle and a hot kitchen tawa

Compare two moments from an ordinary day. First, your fingertips accidentally brush the edge of a hot steel tawa on the stove. Only the muscles of that arm need to contract, and they need to act rapidly to limit further heat exposure. Second, as you walk past a parked autorickshaw at dusk, a dog barks inches from your leg. Now a single muscle twitch is not enough: your heart, lungs, pupils, liver and leg arterioles all need to shift into emergency readiness together and stay ready for several minutes.

Every coordination pathway in a living organism begins with a detectable change in the internal or external environment: a ==stimulus== (plural: **stimuli**), such as heat, light, sound, gravity or a change in blood glucose concentration. Specialised cells or nerve endings called **receptors** detect a particular stimulus. A **coordinator** (such as the brain, spinal cord or an endocrine gland) integrates the incoming information and sends a signal to an ==effector==—a **muscle** that contracts or a **gland** that secretes a chemical—producing the organism's **response**.

:::note Stimulus
A detectable change in the internal or external environment of an organism, such as light, temperature, touch, sound or chemical concentration.
:::

:::note Effector
A body part—specifically a **muscle** or a **gland**—that carries out a response when stimulated by a nerve impulse or a hormone.
:::

:::predict

### The mammalian nervous system: CNS and PNS

In mammals, the **nervous system** coordinates and regulates body functions by sending fast **electrical impulses** along specialised nerve cells called ==neurones== (Cambridge spelling). Anatomically, the mammalian nervous system is divided into two connected parts:

1. **The central nervous system (CNS):** consisting of the **brain** (protected inside the cranium of the skull) and the **spinal cord** (running inside the vertebral column). The CNS acts as the central coordinator, receiving impulses from receptors and sending impulses out to effectors.
2. **The peripheral nervous system (PNS):** consisting of all the **nerves outside the brain and spinal cord** (cranial nerves branching from the brain and spinal nerves branching from the spinal cord) that connect receptors and effectors across the body to the CNS.

:::note Neurone
A specialised nerve cell adapted to transmit electrical impulses rapidly from one part of the body to another along a thin membrane extension (axon or dendron).
:::

### The endocrine system: ductless glands, blood plasma and target receptors

Alongside the nervous system sits the **endocrine system**. Whereas salivary and digestive glands (Chapter 7) pour their secretions through tubes (*ducts*) onto an epithelial surface, an **endocrine gland** is ductless: its cells release chemical messengers directly into surrounding capillaries, where they dissolve in the **blood plasma** (Chapter 8) and circulate throughout the body.

A ==hormone== is a chemical substance, produced by a gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific **target organs**.

:::note Hormone
A chemical substance, produced by an endocrine gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific target organs.
:::

:::evidence A message that survived cutting the nerves | Bayliss and Starling, 1902
At the start of the twentieth century, physiologists following Ivan Pavlov's work assumed that when acidic chyme leaves the stomach and enters the duodenum (Chapter 7), the pancreas is told to secrete alkaline pancreatic juice solely through a nervous reflex. In January 1902 at University College London, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling tested that assumption in an experimental laboratory preparation on anaesthetised animals (a historical physiological procedure never repeated outside licensed laboratory research).

They severed all nerve connections to an isolated loop of upper small intestine while leaving its blood vessels intact. When they introduced dilute hydrochloric acid into the denervated intestinal loop, the pancreas still poured out pancreatic juice within minutes. Next, they scraped cells from the intestinal lining, ground them with dilute acid, filtered the liquid and injected it into a vein: the pancreas secreted juice immediately. Acid in the duodenum had caused intestinal lining cells to release a chemical courier (**secretin**) into the blood plasma. In 1905, Starling coined the word **hormone** (from Greek *hormōn*, “setting in motion”) for all such blood-borne chemical messengers.
:::

If blood plasma carries a hormone through every capillary bed from your scalp to your toes, why does a hormone alter only specific **target organs**? Recall Chapter 4's rule of three-dimensional protein folding: only cells of the target organ possess **receptor proteins** (on their cell membrane or inside the cell) whose binding pocket is **complementary in shape and charge** to that hormone molecule. Cells lacking the matching receptor protein remain unaffected as the hormone washes past in the tissue fluid.

For Cambridge IGCSE 0610 (Topic 14.3), you should be able to identify these major endocrine glands on a body map and state the hormones they secrete:

- **Pituitary gland** (at the base of the brain): secretes several hormones (such as reproductive hormones FSH and LH, and ADH in kidney water balance, Chapter 10) and regulates other endocrine glands.
- **Adrenal glands** (one sitting like a cap atop each kidney): secrete **adrenaline**.
- **Pancreas** (below the stomach, between the duodenum and spleen): contains endocrine islets that secrete **insulin** and (Supplement) **glucagon** into the blood to regulate blood glucose concentration (in addition to its exocrine digestive enzymes in Chapter 7).
- **Ovaries** (in females, in the lower abdomen): secrete **oestrogen** (regulating female secondary sexual characteristics and the menstrual cycle).
- **Testes** (in males, in the scrotum): secrete **testosterone** (regulating male secondary sexual characteristics and sperm production).

### Adrenaline and the ‘fight or flight’ response

When the dog barks beside you on the pavement, your brain detects the sudden sound and visual threat and sends rapid sympathetic nerve impulses down the spinal cord directly to the **adrenal glands**. Within seconds, the adrenal glands release **adrenaline** into the blood plasma. Adrenaline prepares the body for vigorous physical action—fighting or running away (**‘fight or flight’**)—through four coordinated effects required by 0610 Topic 14.3:

1. **Increased heart rate:** Adrenaline binds to receptors on the heart's sinoatrial node pacemaker and cardiac muscle (Chapter 8), increasing both heart rate and stroke volume so oxygen and glucose are pumped faster to skeletal muscles.
2. **Increased breathing rate:** Breathing rate and depth rise, increasing alveolar ventilation (Chapter 8) so more oxygen enters the blood and more carbon dioxide is removed.
3. **Increased pupil diameter:** The pupils dilate (widen), admitting more light to the retina so visual sensitivity in dim surroundings increases.
4. **Increased blood glucose concentration (Supplement metabolic control):** Adrenaline binds to receptors on **liver cells**, stimulating enzymes that break down stored **glycogen** into **glucose** and release it into the blood plasma. Together with the increased heart rate, this boosts the supply of glucose and oxygen to respiring muscle cells for rapid **aerobic respiration** (Chapter 6).

The nervous system routes electrical impulses along specific neurones from the CNS (brain and spinal cord) via the PNS to a single effector; endocrine glands release hormones into blood plasma so any target organ with complementary receptor proteins responds.

## 9.2 Fast electrical wiring: neurones, the reflex arc, and the synapse

:::scene Plymouth, 1939 & TIFR Colaba, Mumbai, 1976

Look closely at the wiring that pulls your hand off a hot pan. In Chapter 2 we saw why a cell cannot grow into a thick, metre-wide sphere without starving its interior of diffusion surface. Yet a single **motor neurone** running from the lower spinal cord in your back to the muscles of your big toe can be **one metre long**. It solves the geometry trap by keeping its conducting fibre—the **axon**—microscopically thin (typically 1 to 20 µm across), while wrapping that fibre in insulating layers so electrical impulses race along it at up to **70 to 120 m/s**.

### Three specialist neurones: sensory, relay and motor

In Cambridge 0610 diagrams and photomicrographs (Topic 14.1.4), you can tell the three classes of neurone apart immediately by the position of the **cell body** (containing the nucleus, cytoplasm, ribosomes and mitochondria) and the length of the fibres:

1. **Sensory neurone:** carries electrical impulses **from a receptor to the CNS**. Its receptor endings join a long fibre (**dendron**) leading towards the spinal cord, and its **cell body sits out on a side branch** (in a swelling called the dorsal root ganglion just outside the spinal cord), followed by an axon entering the CNS.
2. **Relay neurone** (also called a connector or interneurone): lies **entirely inside the CNS** (in the grey matter of the spinal cord or brain). It has many short branched **dendrites**, a central cell body, and a short unmyelinated axon that passes impulses from sensory neurones to motor neurones (and to ascending pathways heading to the brain).
3. **Motor neurone:** carries electrical impulses **from the CNS to an effector** (a muscle or gland). Its star-shaped **cell body sits at the start of the cell** inside the grey matter of the spinal cord or brain, collecting inputs through short **dendrites**, and sends a single **long axon** out to end in **motor end plates** (synaptic terminals) on muscle fibres.

Many long sensory and motor axons in vertebrates are wrapped in a fatty insulating layer called the ==myelin sheath==, formed by supporting **Schwann cells** that wind their lipid membranes tightly around the axon like tape around a wire. Narrow uninsulated gaps about 1 µm wide—the **nodes of Ranvier**—interrupt the myelin sheath every millimetre or so. Myelin greatly reduces membrane current leakage between nodes. Local current spreads beneath it, and the action potential is regenerated at the exposed nodes. This rapid node-to-node regeneration is called (**saltatory conduction**), boosting conduction speed from ~1–5 m/s in an unmyelinated fibre to ~70–120 m/s in a myelinated mammalian nerve.

:::note Myelin sheath
An insulating layer of fatty cell membranes (formed by Schwann cells in peripheral nerves) wrapped around an axon, with narrow gaps (nodes of Ranvier) that greatly speed up the conduction of electrical impulses.
:::

### The spinal reflex arc: automatic protection before conscious pain

When your fingers touch a hot steel tawa, the withdrawal of your arm is a ==reflex action==: an automatic, rapid response to a stimulus that does not require a conscious decision by the brain. The pathway of neurones through which the impulse travels is a ==reflex arc==, and this withdrawal example can be represented by the following chain (0610 14.1.5):

:::eq
**stimulus** → **receptor** → **sensory neurone** → **relay neurone** → **motor neurone** → **effector** → **response**
:::

Trace the hot-tawa withdrawal step by step:

1. **Stimulus & Receptor:** Heat from the hot pan stimulates temperature and pain **receptors** in the skin of your fingertips.
2. **Sensory neurone:** The receptor triggers an electrical impulse that travels along the **sensory neurone** up your arm and enters the back (dorsal root) of the **spinal cord**.
3. **Relay neurone (in CNS):** Inside the butterfly-shaped grey matter of the spinal cord, the sensory neurone passes the signal across a microscopic junction (**synapse**) to a **relay neurone**.
4. **Motor neurone:** The relay neurone passes the signal across a second synapse to a **motor neurone**, whose axon leaves the front (ventral root) of the spinal cord and runs down your arm.
5. **Effector & Response:** The impulse reaches the **effector**—your **biceps muscle**—causing its fibres to contract and pull your hand away from the pan (**response**).

The exact timing depends on the stimulus, sensory fibres, connections, muscle and measurement method. A temperature at the pan is not yet an impulse: heat must reach receptors and be converted into a signal.

### Pause 2: how a salty cell regenerates an electrical signal

In Book 2 (*The Moving World*, Chapter 12), we saw that a copper wire conducts electricity because metallic copper is already packed with mobile free electrons that drift along the wire when a battery applies a voltage. A neurone contains no metal and **zero free electrons** drifting along its cytoplasm. If you tried to send a passive household current down an uninsulated, 10 µm-wide tube of salty water wrapped in a leaky membrane, the current would leak out across the membrane and fizzle away within a couple of millimetres.

How, then, does a biological axon carry an unweakened electrical impulse over a full metre? In 1939 and 1952, **Alan Hodgkin** and **Andrew Huxley** solved this puzzle by threading a fine glass electrode inside the 0.8 mm **squid giant axon** at Plymouth (in historical laboratory experiments on marine nerve fibres), building on the membrane ion-pump chemistry that **Jens Christian Skou** (`p-skou`, Chapter 3) later identified in crab nerves:

1. **The resting battery (−70 mV):** When a neurone is resting, Skou's **sodium–potassium pump** (`Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase`) uses ATP from respiration to pump **sodium ions (`Na⁺`) out** of the cell and **potassium ions (`K⁺`) in** (3 `Na⁺` out for every 2 `K⁺` in). Because some `K⁺` ions diffuse back out through background leak channels leaving negative proteins behind inside, the resting cytoplasm sits at an electrical potential of about **−70 mV** compared with the fluid outside. The membrane is **polarised**, storing potential energy like a cocked spring.
2. **Threshold and depolarisation (+30 mV):** Embedded in the axon membrane are thousands of folded protein pores whose gates respond to voltage: **voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels**. If a stimulus or a neighbouring patch of membrane raises the inside voltage from −70 mV past a **threshold** of about **−55 mV**, these `Na⁺` channels snap open. Driven by both their high outside concentration and the negative inside voltage, **`Na⁺` ions rush IN** across the membrane, flipping the local inside voltage from −70 mV all the way to **+30 mV** (**depolarisation**).
3. **Repolarisation and the one-way refractory period (−75 mV → −70 mV):** Less than a millisecond after opening, the voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels automatically **inactivate** (plugging themselves shut), while **voltage-gated `K⁺` channels** swing open. Now **`K⁺` ions diffuse OUT** down their concentration gradient, bringing the inside voltage back down to −70 mV (**repolarisation**) and briefly overshooting to **−75 mV** (**hyperpolarisation**).
4. **The sideways domino wave:** While one patch of axon is at +30 mV inside, positive `Na⁺` ions spread sideways a tiny distance forward inside the cytoplasm, raising the *next* patch of membrane to −55 mV and triggering *its* `Na⁺` gates to open. Because the patch just behind has inactivated `Na⁺` gates (**refractory period**), the **action potential** travels forward along the membrane like a line of falling dominoes—regenerating the spike at successive exposed regions. The ion movement changes only a tiny fraction of the total ionic distribution. The pump maintains the gradients over time; channels produce the rapid spike. Unlike fallen dominoes, the membrane resets and can fire again.

The second choice. Each patch of membrane opens its voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels so `Na⁺` ions cross **sideways** (inward across the 7 nm lipid membrane), and a tiny local spread of that inward current triggers the next patch's channels to open. The wave travels down the axon, while the ions move across the boundary.
:::

### Obaid Siddiqi and the warm-paralysed fruit flies of TIFR Colaba

Hodgkin and Huxley's equations showed that a nerve spike depends on voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels, but how do we know that a specific gene builds that sodium gate? In the 1970s, **Obaid Siddiqi**—who founded India's first Molecular Biology Unit at the **Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR)** in **Colaba, Mumbai** in 1962—teamed up with **Seymour Benzer** at Caltech to test temperature-sensitive mutants of the fruit fly *Drosophila melanogaster*.

:::evidence Dissecting the action potential with temperature-sensitive flies | Suzuki 1971; Siddiqi & Benzer, 1976
In 1971, David Suzuki and colleagues discovered a fruit-fly mutant called **`para`** (*paralytic*). At 22 °C, *para* flies walk and fly normally; warm their glass vial to **29–32 °C**, and within five seconds they fall motionless; cool them back to 22 °C, and they immediately stand up and walk again.

In 1976 (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 73: 3253–3257), **Obaid Siddiqi and Seymour Benzer** recorded electrical signals from the neck nerve and flight muscles of *Drosophila* as temperature rose (a laboratory microelectrode procedure on insect tissue). In **`para`** flies warmed above ~30 °C, **action potentials along the nerve axon disappeared completely**, even though stimulating the flight muscle directly still caused normal contraction. In two other mutants (**`shi`** and **`com`**), the axon kept firing action potentials at high temperature, but the signal stopped at the **synapse**. Subsequent cloning of the **`para` gene** (Loughney, Kreber & Ganetzky, 1989) proved that *para* encodes the main protein of the **voltage-gated `Na⁺` channel**: a single amino-acid change makes the channel gate thermally unstable above 30 °C, switching off the axonal spike on command.
:::

:::five Action potential
? What is it?
A rapid, self-propagating wave of electrical depolarisation and repolarisation (−70 mV → +30 mV → −70 mV) that travels along the membrane of a neurone or muscle fibre; in school biology it is called an **electrical impulse**.
? Why is it called this?
*Potential* refers to electrical potential difference (voltage, measured in millivolts across the membrane); an *action* potential is the active spike that triggers a response, contrasted with the steady *resting* potential.
? What problem did it solve?
A thin tube of salty cytoplasm leaks passive current within millimetres; regenerating the voltage spike at every patch (or node of Ranvier) lets the signal travel a metre or more with zero loss of amplitude.
? Why does it work?
Skou's `Na⁺/K⁺` pump pre-loads the concentration gradients using ATP; voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels then let `Na⁺` rush in at threshold (−55 mV), and delayed `K⁺` channels let `K⁺` rush out to reset the membrane.
? How does it help?
It explains why nerve impulses have an all-or-nothing height, why myelin speeds conduction up to ~100 m/s, and why sodium-channel mutations (`para^ts`) or toxins (tetrodotoxin) silence nerves without destroying the resting cell.
?? How do we know?
Hodgkin and Huxley (1939, 1952) measured the overshoot and separated the `Na⁺` and `K⁺` currents in squid giant axons; Siddiqi and Benzer (1976) showed that mutating the `para` sodium-channel gene reversibly abolishes the spike above 30 °C.
?? What it isn't
A flow of free electrons down a metal wire, or a wave whose height grows bigger when a stimulus is stronger (stronger stimuli increase the **frequency** of impulses per second, not the height of each spike).
?? Where does it stop working?
An action potential cannot jump directly across the 20 nm fluid gap between two separate neurones at a chemical synapse; there, the electrical wave must hand its baton to a diffusing chemical neurotransmitter.
:::

:::careful
> “A stronger stimulus, like a hotter pan, makes each nerve impulse taller (more millivolts).”

Action potentials are **all-or-nothing**: once a stimulus depolarises the membrane past threshold (−55 mV), the voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels open fully and fire the same ~+30 mV spike every time. A stronger stimulus signals its intensity by firing **more impulses per second** (higher frequency) and by recruiting **more neurones**, never by sending a taller voltage spike.
:::

### Pause 3: the chemical junction between cells

Follow an electrical impulse to the very end of a sensory or relay neurone's axon. Here the axon swells into a tiny terminal bulb—the **presynaptic knob**—and stops. Between its cell membrane and the membrane of the next neurone lies a water-filled space about **20 nanometres (20 nm)** wide: the **synaptic gap** (or **synaptic cleft**). In Core IGCSE 0610 (14.1.7), the whole junction between two neurones is defined simply as a ==synapse==.

:::note Synapse
A junction between two neurones, where an electrical impulse in the first neurone triggers the release of a chemical neurotransmitter that diffuses across a microscopic gap to stimulate the next neurone.
:::

Why does the electrical impulse not simply jump the 20 nm gap like a spark across air? Because extracellular tissue fluid is an electrical conductor (salty water): the tiny local current leaving the narrow presynaptic tip leaks harmlessly into the surrounding fluid instead of depolarising the much larger membrane of the next cell. To cross the gap reliably—and to allow the nervous system to add, subtract and filter signals from many neurones at once—the junction switches from an electrical wave to a **chemical courier** for 20 nanometres.

### Inside the synapse: the four-step Supplement sequence (0610 14.1.8–14.1.9)

Electron micrographs taken in the 1950s and physiological recordings by **Otto Loewi** (1921) and **Bernard Katz** (1950–1970) revealed three essential structures at every chemical synapse (0610 14.1.8):

- Inside the **presynaptic knob**: numerous **mitochondria** (supplying ATP) and hundreds of tiny membrane-bound sacs called **vesicles**, each filled with thousands of ==neurotransmitter== molecules (such as acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine or glutamate).
- Between the cells: the ~20 nm fluid-filled **synaptic gap (cleft)**.
- On the **postsynaptic membrane** of the next neurone: folded **receptor proteins** whose binding pockets are complementary in shape to the neurotransmitter, coupled to ion channels.

:::note Neurotransmitter
A chemical messenger molecule stored in vesicles in a presynaptic neurone, released into the synaptic gap when an impulse arrives, and able to bind complementary receptor proteins on the next neurone.
:::

At the **excitatory chemical synapse drawn here**, transmission follows this **four-step sequence** (Cambridge 0610 Supplement 14.1.9):

1. **Vesicle release:** An arriving electrical impulse depolarises the presynaptic knob (opening voltage-gated calcium, `Ca²⁺`, channels) and **stimulates vesicles to move to the presynaptic membrane, fuse with it, and release neurotransmitter molecules into the synaptic gap** (by exocytosis).
2. **Diffusion across the gap:** The released **neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the synaptic gap** down their steep concentration gradient (from high concentration at the presynaptic membrane to low concentration at the postsynaptic membrane). Because the distance *x* is only ~20 nm, pure diffusion takes less than a microsecond (*t* ≈ *x*² ÷ 2*D* ≈ 0.5 µs), while release and receptor responses add further delay. The explorer uses chosen delays rather than a universal timing rule.
3. **Complementary binding:** The **neurotransmitter molecules bind with complementary receptor proteins** on the membrane of the next (postsynaptic) neurone—matching by 3D shape and charge just like an enzyme and substrate in Chapter 4.
4. **Stimulating the next impulse (and resetting):** At this excitatory example, binding opens ligand-gated channels carrying a net inward positive current; `Na⁺` ions enter and depolarise the membrane, so **an electrical impulse is stimulated in the next neurone** if threshold (−55 mV) is reached. Immediately afterwards, the neurotransmitter is broken down by a cleft enzyme (such as **acetylcholinesterase**) or pumped back into the presynaptic knob so the synapse resets and does not keep firing continuously.

### Why synapses enforce one-way traffic (0610 14.1.10)

If you stimulate the middle of an isolated axon with an electrode, action potentials can travel away from the electrode in both directions. Yet in a living nervous system, impulses in a reflex arc travel **in one direction only** (from receptor → sensory → relay → motor → effector, never backwards from muscle to skin). Why?

Look at the asymmetry of the synapse (0610 14.1.10):

- **The release machinery for this relay is on the presynaptic side.**
- **The receiving receptors for this relay are on the postsynaptic membrane.** Presynaptic receptors can also regulate transmitter release.

Stimulating the receiving cell does not reverse this release arrangement. That gives the drawn chemical relay its forward direction. Some synapses inhibit the receiving cell, making an impulse less likely; the sodium-entry example is not every synapse. Electrical synapses use direct connections and are a different mechanism.

:::five Chemical synapse
? What is it?
A microscopic ~20 nm junction where an electrical impulse in a presynaptic neurone releases chemical neurotransmitter molecules from vesicles to diffuse across the gap and bind complementary receptors on the postsynaptic neurone.
? Why is it called this?
Coined in 1897 by Charles Scott Sherrington (with classical scholar Arthur Woollgar Verrall) from Greek *synapsis* (“conjunction” or “clasping together”, from *syn-* together + *haptein* to fasten).
? What problem did it solve?
It gives this chemical relay a forward direction and allows a postsynaptic neurone to integrate excitatory and inhibitory inputs from hundreds of different presynaptic neurones before firing.
? Why does it work?
In this excitatory chemical relay, presynaptic release delivers transmitter to postsynaptic receptors. The release-and-receive arrangement gives the relay a direction. Receptors, clearance routes and effects vary among synapses.
? How does it help?
It explains why reflex arcs have a small (~0.5–1 ms per synapse) central delay, why impulses never travel backwards from effectors to receptors, and how medicines, anaesthetics and neurotoxins can block or boost specific neural pathways.
?? How do we know?
Otto Loewi (1921) transferred bathing fluid (*Vagusstoff*, acetylcholine) from a nerve-stimulated frog heart to slow a second heart; 1950s electron microscopy visualised the 20 nm cleft and presynaptic vesicles; Bernard Katz proved packeted (quantal) release.
?? What it isn't
An electrical spark jumping across empty air, or a permanent lock: neurotransmitters bind reversibly and must be rapidly cleared by enzymes or reuptake transporters so the synapse can fire again.
?? Where does it stop working?
Prolonged high-frequency stimulation can temporarily deplete ready-to-release presynaptic vesicles (synaptic fatigue), and toxins that inhibit the cleanup enzyme leave the postsynaptic membrane locked in continuous depolarisation.
:::

Its measured time includes visual processing, response selection and finger movement; it is not a measurement of one spinal pathway. (Never attempt to test a withdrawal reflex using heat or sharp objects.)

## 9.3 Sense organs and the human eye: catching and focusing light

:::scene Mumbai, stepping from midday marine glare into a shaded lobby

Step out of midday sunlight along Marine Drive into a dimly lit building hallway. At first the hallway looks nearly black; within seconds your **pupils** widen automatically, and over the next few minutes your **rod** cells take over from your **cone** cells so you can make out the stairs in monochrome. Glance down at your watch (25 cm away) and then out through the doorway at a ship on the horizon (kilometres away): without your having to turn a focusing ring with your fingers, a circular muscle inside your eye adjusts the curvature of your **lens** so both images land sharply on your **retina**. Meanwhile, on a windowsill beside the stairs, a potted moong seedling with no eyes, nerves or muscles has bent its growing stem steadily towards the sunlit glass.

### Sense organs and the architecture of the mammalian eye (0610 14.2.1–14.2.3)

A ==sense organ== is a group of receptor cells responding to a specific stimulus, together with the tissues that deliver or focus that stimulus (0610 14.2.1):

- **Eye:** photoreceptor cells (rods and cones in the retina) respond to **light**.
- **Ear:** mechanoreceptor hair cells in the cochlea respond to **sound** (and vestibular receptors respond to gravity/head motion).
- **Skin:** separate receptors respond to **touch/pressure** and to **temperature** changes.
- **Tongue and nose:** chemoreceptors respond to dissolved and airborne **chemicals** (taste and smell).

:::note Sense organ
A group of receptor cells responding to specific stimuli: light, sound, touch, temperature and chemicals.
:::

In Book 2 (*The Moving World*, Chapter 10, Section 10.5), we traced the physics of the human eye as a converging optical system that projects a real, inverted image onto the back of the eyeball. Now let us connect that physics to the ten biological structures required by Cambridge 0610 Topic 14.2.2–14.2.3 and 14.2.7–14.2.9:

1. **Cornea:** the transparent, curved front window of the outer sclera coat. It **refracts (bends) light rays** as they enter the eye—performing a large fraction of the eye's total focusing because the jump in refractive index from air (*n* = 1.00) into the cornea (*n* ≈ 1.38) is the largest speed change along the optical path.
2. **Iris:** the coloured muscular disc behind the cornea. Containing antagonistic **circular** and **radial** smooth muscles, it **controls how much light enters the pupil**.
3. **Pupil:** the central hole (aperture) in the iris through which light passes to reach the lens.
4. **Lens:** a transparent, flexible, biconvex protein disc behind the iris that **focuses (fine-tunes) light onto the retina** by changing its curvature (**accommodation**).
5. **Ciliary muscles** and **suspensory ligaments:** a circular sphincter ring of smooth muscle (**ciliary muscle**) surrounding the lens, linked to the lens rim by radially arranged fibres (**suspensory ligaments**) that adjust lens curvature.
6. **Retina:** the innermost lining at the back of the eye, which **contains light receptor cells (rods and cones)**, some sensitive to light of different colours (wavelengths), and converts light energy into electrical impulses.
7. **Fovea** (yellow spot): a small shallow pit in the **centre of the retina** directly inline with the visual axis, packed densely with **cones only** (no rods), where vision is sharpest in bright light.
8. **Optic nerve:** the bundle of over a million sensory neurone axons leaving the back of the eyeball that **carries electrical impulses from the retina to the brain**.
9. **Blind spot:** the point on the retina where the axons of the **optic nerve leave the eye**; because there are **no rods or cones** at this exit point, light falling on the blind spot is not detected.

### Two kinds of photoreceptor: rods and cones (0610 14.2.7–14.2.9)

Why do vibrant colours disappear under moonlight while faint outlines remain visible—and why can you see a dim star better by looking slightly to one side of it than by staring straight at it? Because the human retina divides light detection between two specialised photoreceptor cell types:

- **Rods** (~120 million per eye): contain the visual pigment rhodopsin, which has **greater sensitivity to dim light**. Rods provide **night vision** in shades of grey (monochrome, because all rods have the same wavelength sensitivity) and are **distributed across the retina outside the fovea** (absent from the centre of the fovea and absent from the blind spot). Looking slightly to the side of a dim star moves its image off your rod-free fovea onto the rod-rich peripheral retina.
- **Cones** (~6 million per eye): require brighter light to fire and provide **high-detail colour vision**. Humans possess **three different kinds of cones** that absorb light of different overlapping wavelength ranges—**red (long-wavelength, ~564 nm)**, **green (medium-wavelength, ~534 nm)**, and **blue (short-wavelength, ~420 nm)**—and cones are **concentrated tightly at the fovea** (while sparser across the peripheral retina and absent at the blind spot).

:::fig c9_eye_anatomy_receptors() | Cross-section of the human eye (top, 0610 14.2.2–14.2.3) and comparison of rods and cones across the retina (bottom, 14.2.7–14.2.9).

### Two muscular reflexes inside the eye: the pupil reflex and accommodation (0610 14.2.4–14.2.6)

The eye adjusts continuously to two changing variables: **light intensity** (via the **pupil reflex**) and **object distance** (via **accommodation**).

#### 1. The pupil reflex: circular versus radial muscles in the iris (0610 14.2.4–14.2.5)
The **pupil reflex** is a cranial reflex arc (`retina photoreceptors → sensory neurones in optic nerve → relay neurones in midbrain → motor neurones → iris muscles`) that protects the retina from bleaching or damage in bright light and maximises light capture in dim light. Because a muscle fibre can only pull by contracting and cannot push itself longer, the iris uses an **antagonistic pair** of smooth muscles:

- **In bright light:** the **circular muscles contract** (tightening the ring around the pupil) and the **radial muscles relax** → the **pupil constricts** (becomes smaller/narrower, ~2 mm), reducing light entering the eye to protect the retina.
- **In dim light:** the **radial muscles contract** (pulling the iris rim outward like wheel spokes) and the **circular muscles relax** → the **pupil dilates** (becomes wider, ~7 mm), admitting more light to stimulate the rods.

:::predict

:::

#### 2. Accommodation: how a contracting ring slackens the suspensory ligaments (0610 14.2.6)
Light rays arriving from a **distant object** (more than 6 m away) are nearly parallel and need less bending  to meet on the retina, whereas light rays diverging steeply from a **near object** (such as a book at 25 cm) need **stronger refraction** . Because the cornea's curvature is fixed, the eye adjusts its focal length by changing the thickness of its elastic **lens**—a process called **accommodation** (Book 2, Section 10.5).

To get the muscle mechanics right every time on an exam, picture the **ciliary muscle** in three dimensions: it is a **circular sphincter ring** surrounding the lens, connected to the lens equator by radiating **suspensory ligaments**. When any circular ring of muscle **contracts**, its diameter gets **smaller** (moving inward *towards* the lens in the centre), which **releases tension** on the spokes inside it:

- **Viewing a NEAR object (diverging rays require MORE refraction):**
  1. **Ciliary muscles contract** (the muscle ring narrows inward towards the lens).
  2. **Suspensory ligaments slacken** (loosen; tension falls).
  3. The naturally elastic **lens becomes thicker / more convex** (fatter and more curved).
  4. Light rays are **refracted more strongly**, bringing the near object to a sharp focus on the retina.
- **Viewing a DISTANT object (parallel rays require LESS refraction):**
  1. **Ciliary muscles relax** (restoring tension in the zonular fibres around the lens).
  2. **Suspensory ligaments are pulled tight (taut)**.
  3. The **lens is pulled thinner / less convex** (flatter).
  4. Light rays are **refracted less**, focusing parallel rays on the retina.

It is the exact opposite—and that is why looking at a distant horizon rests your eyes, whereas reading small print close up makes your ciliary muscles tired. Because the ciliary muscle is a **circular ring** around the lens, **contracting** it makes the ring *smaller*, which **slackens** the suspensory ligaments so the elastic lens bulges thicker for **near** vision. **Relaxing** the ciliary muscle restores tension in the surrounding fibres, pulling the suspensory ligaments **tight** to flatten the lens for **distant** vision.

## 9.4 Slow chemical steering in plants: phototropism, gravitropism, and auxin

:::scene Calcutta, 1901, Utrecht, 1926 & a potted seedling on a Mumbai windowsill

Plants have no brain, no spinal cord and no muscles. Yet as **Jagadish Chandra Bose** showed in Calcutta at the turn of the twentieth century with his optical-lever **crescograph** (magnifying plant growth ten thousand times) and fine galvanometers, plants are far from passive: wounded or touched leaves such as *Mimosa pudica* (*chui-mui*) can transmit rapid **electrical action potentials** along their vascular bundles to trigger turgor folding within a second, while growing shoots and roots steer their architecture over hours by regulating cell elongation.

:::story p-bose

In Cambridge 0610 Topic 14.5, a **tropic response (tropism)** is a growth movement of a plant part in response to the direction of an external stimulus:

- ==Phototropism== is a response in which parts of a plant grow **towards or away from the direction of a light source**. Plant **shoots** show **positive phototropism** (growing towards light so leaves intercept sunlight for photosynthesis, Chapter 5), whereas most **roots** show either no response or **negative phototropism** (growing away from light).
- ==Gravitropism== (formerly called geotropism) is a response in which parts of a plant grow **towards or away from gravity**. Plant **roots** show **positive gravitropism** (growing downward towards gravity into soil for anchorage, water and mineral ions, Chapter 3), whereas **shoots** show **negative gravitropism** (growing upward away from gravity even in dark soil before emerging into light).

:::note Phototropism
A growth response in which parts of a plant grow towards (positive phototropism, as in shoots) or away from (negative phototropism) the direction of a light source.
:::

:::note Gravitropism
A growth response in which parts of a plant grow towards (positive gravitropism, as in roots) or away from (negative gravitropism, as in shoots) the pull of gravity.
:::

:::predict

- It grows straight upward because there is no unilateral light in darkness
- It bends to the left (towards the agar block) because auxin pulls the tip towards itself

:::

:::evidence From Darwin's canary grass to Went's agar blocks | 1880, 1913 and 1926
How does a young grass shoot sheath (**coleoptile**) detect light at its tip and bend several millimetres lower down in its zone of elongation? Three classic experiments traced the mechanism:

1. **Charles Darwin and Francis Darwin (1880, *The Power of Movement in Plants*):** Illuminated canary-grass and oat coleoptiles from one side (**unilateral light**). Intact coleoptiles bent towards the light. If the top 1 to 2 mm **tip was cut off**, or covered with a tiny **opaque foil cap**, the coleoptile did not bend—whereas burying the lower stem in black sand while leaving the tip exposed still produced bending. Therefore, the **shoot tip detects unilateral light** and sends a signal downward to the elongation zone.
2. **Peter Boysen-Jensen (1913):** Cut off the coleoptile tip and replaced it on top of a water-permeable block of **gelatin**: the shoot still bent towards unilateral light. When he inserted an impermeable **mica flake** halfway into the stem on the **shaded side**, bending stopped; inserting the mica flake on the **illuminated side** did not stop bending. Therefore, the downward signal is a **water-soluble chemical** travelling down the **shaded side**.
3. **Frits Warmolt Went (1926):** Placed excised oat coleoptile tips on tiny blocks of **agar jelly** for an hour so the chemical diffused into the agar, then placed an agar block **off-centre** on a decapitated coleoptile stump in **complete darkness**. The stump bent *away* from the side carrying the agar block—proving that the chemical, which Went named ==auxin== (chemically indole-3-acetic acid, IAA), **stimulates cell elongation in shoots**. When a tip was lit from one side over a split agar block, about **two-thirds (~65–68%) of the auxin collected in the shaded-side block** and only one-third in the lit-side block, with no loss of total auxin: unilateral light causes auxin to redistribute laterally to the shaded side rather than destroying it.
:::

:::note Auxin
A plant growth hormone (such as indole-3-acetic acid) produced in the shoot tip that diffuses downward and regulates cell elongation during phototropism and gravitropism.
:::

For **Cambridge IGCSE 0610 Supplement (14.5.4–14.5.5)**, state the four-step explanation of how **auxin** controls shoot growth in phototropism and gravitropism:

1. **Auxin is made in the shoot tip** (where cells are actively dividing).
2. **Auxin moves from the shoot tip** into the zone of cell elongation just behind it. Directed transport involves membrane transport proteins as well as local diffusion. The school account calls this movement diffusion.
3. **Auxin is unequally distributed in response to light and gravity:**
   - Under **unilateral light** (light from one side), auxin moves laterally and accumulates on the **shaded side** of the shoot.
   - In a **horizontal shoot**, gravity causes auxin to accumulate on the **lower side** of the shoot.
4. **In shoots, auxin stimulates cell elongation** (by activating proton pumps that loosen the cellulose cell wall so turgor pressure stretches the cells longer):
   - In unilateral light, cells on the **shaded side elongate faster** than cells on the illuminated side, bending the shoot **towards the light** (**positive phototropism**).
   - In a horizontal shoot in darkness, cells on the **lower side elongate faster** than cells on the upper side, bending the shoot **upward away from gravity** (**negative gravitropism**).

*(Why does a horizontal root bend downward while its shoot bends upward? In a horizontal root, auxin also accumulates on the **lower side**, but root cells have a different sensitivity: high auxin concentration **inhibits** cell elongation in roots, so the upper side elongates faster and turns the root tip **downward towards gravity**—**positive gravitropism**.)*

:::five Tropisms and auxin
? What is it?
A tropism is a directional plant growth response towards or away from light (**phototropism**) or gravity (**gravitropism**), driven by unequal distribution of the plant hormone **auxin** across the growing zone.
? Why is it called this?
*Tropism* comes from Greek *tropē* (a turning); *auxin* comes from Greek *auxein* (to grow or increase).
? What problem did it solve?
Without eyes, nerves or muscles, a germinating seedling buried in dark soil at any random angle must send its shoot upward into light for photosynthesis and its root downward into wet soil for anchorage and mineral uptake.
? Why does it work?
Auxin made in the shoot tip diffuses downward and redistributes to the **shaded side** (under one-sided light) or **lower side** (under gravity); because auxin stimulates cell elongation in shoots, the side with more auxin grows longer and curves the stem.
? How does it help?
It explains Darwin's, Boysen-Jensen's and Went's coleoptile experiments, how rotating a plant about a specified axis can average the direction of a stimulus over time (without removing gravity), and why synthetic auxins are used as selective weedkillers.
?? How do we know?
Went (1926) captured auxin from coleoptile tips in agar blocks, proved an off-centre block bends a dark stump by stimulating elongation, and measured ~2:1 shaded-to-lit auxin redistribution under unilateral light.
?? What it isn't
A plant “wanting” or “stretching” consciously towards the window, or sunlight warming the lit side to grow faster (if the lit side grew faster, the shoot would bend *away* from the window).
?? Where does it stop working?
Auxin-driven cell elongation takes 20 to 120 minutes and is irreversible growth; for sub-second leaf folding in *Mimosa pudica*, the plant instead uses Bose's rapid electrical action potentials and reversible water/turgor shifts at the leaf base.
:::



:::careful
> “Sunlight destroys auxin on the sunny side of a shoot, or warms the sunny side so it grows towards the window.”

Two classic traps sit in that sentence. First, if the sunny side elongated faster, the shoot would bend *away* from the window; a shoot bends towards light only because the **shaded side elongates faster**. Second, Frits Went's split-agar measurements showed that total auxin leaving a coleoptile tip stays the same in unilateral light as in darkness: light triggers **unequal lateral distribution** of auxin towards the shaded side rather than destroying it.

## The pufferfish molecule that silences sodium gates

:::case The pufferfish molecule that silences sodium gates
In 1964, the neurophysiologists Toshio Narahashi, John W. Moore and William R. Scott used a voltage clamp on giant axons to solve a famous marine mystery: how **tetrodotoxin (TTX)**—a small positively charged guanidinium molecule produced by marine bacteria and accumulated in the liver and ovaries of pufferfish (*Takifugu*)—causes rapid skeletal-muscle paralysis at nanomolar concentrations. *(This is a molecular reasoning case from laboratory neurophysiology, never a substance for experiment.)*

Three experimental clues sit in the laboratory file when nanomolar TTX is added to the saline bathing a motor nerve–muscle preparation:

1. **Clue 1 (Resting membrane check):** The axon's **resting potential stays steady at −70 mV**, and when the membrane is held depolarised under voltage clamp, outward **potassium (`K⁺`) current** through voltage-gated `K⁺` channels flows at its normal size.
2. **Clue 2 (Axonal spike check):** Stimulating the motor axon electrically fails to produce the inward **sodium (`Na⁺`) current**: the +30 mV action potential vanishes completely, just as warming to 32 °C silenced Obaid Siddiqi's `para^ts` fruit-fly axon.
3. **Clue 3 (Synapse check):** Applying acetylcholine directly onto the muscle's postsynaptic membrane still produces a **local endplate depolarisation**. This does not establish normal propagated muscle action potentials or whole-muscle contraction.

Using these three clues, answer three questions:
- Which specific membrane protein does tetrodotoxin block, and why does the resting potential remain at −70 mV?
- Why does action-potential-evoked acetylcholine release fail when the motor nerve is stimulated in the presence of TTX, while the postsynaptic response to directly supplied acetylcholine remains?
- How does TTX blockade differ from curare blockade (Section 9.2)?
???
- **Target protein & resting potential:** Tetrodotoxin plugs the outer pore of **voltage-gated sodium (`Na⁺`) channels** (its positively charged guanidinium group binds the selectivity mouth of the `Na⁺` pore while its bulky cage blocks hydrated `Na⁺` ions from entering). Because TTX does not block **potassium (`K⁺`) leak channels**, voltage-gated `K⁺` channels or Skou's **`Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase` pump**, the resting potassium gradient and **−70 mV resting potential** remain intact.
- **Why the synapse stays silent:** With voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels blocked along the axon, no action potential can propagate down the motor neurone to reach the **presynaptic knob**. Because the presynaptic membrane never depolarises, Step 1 of synaptic transmission (vesicle fusion and exocytosis) is never triggered, Clue 3 tests the receiving receptors, not presynaptic vesicle function. TTX can also affect skeletal-muscle sodium channels; a local endplate response is different from a propagated muscle spike. Spontaneous transmitter release is not excluded by failure of evoked release.
- **TTX versus curare:** **TTX** stops **axonal conduction** by blocking **voltage-gated `Na⁺` channels** *before* the impulse reaches the synapse (preventing neurotransmitter release), whereas **curare** leaves axonal conduction and presynaptic neurotransmitter release intact but blocks the **postsynaptic receptor proteins** (Step 3) across the synaptic gap.
