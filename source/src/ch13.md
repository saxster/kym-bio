Picture two imagined medical puzzles unfolding on the same rainy afternoon at a teaching hospital in Parel, Mumbai.

Upstairs in the maternity ward, two healthy baby boys were born during a brief generator switchover before their ankle identity bands were clipped on. **Baby 1** has **Blood Group O**; **Baby 2** has **Blood Group A**. The ward register lists the two sets of parents: **Couple 1 (the Desais)** are a **Group AB mother** and a **Group O father**, while **Couple 2 (the Kulkarnis)** are a **Group A mother** and a **Group B father**. The on-call geneticist sketches two small four-box grids on a notepad. Can she use the blood groups to work out which assignment fits? For this puzzle, assume ordinary ABO inheritance, accurate blood typing, and that these are the only two possible families. A real hospital would confirm identities with its records and DNA identity testing.

Downstairs in the paediatric clinic, a second couple—both of whom have **completely normal trichromatic colour vision**—are astonished to learn that their six-year-old son has **red–green colour vision deficiency**, while their eight-year-old daughter sees every red and green traffic light on Dr Babasaheb Ambedkar Road without difficulty. How can a trait that neither parent shows appear in their son? And why did the boy's maternal grandfather have the exact same condition, even though the boy's mother has normal colour vision?

In Chapter 12 you saw how **Walther Flemming** traced chromosome threads through **mitosis (`12.2`)**, and how **meiosis (`12.4`)** halves the chromosome count in reproductive organs from **diploid (`2n = 46`, arranged in `23` chromosome pairs)** to **haploid (`n = 23` single chromosomes)** in sperm and egg cells so **fertilisation** restores `2n = 46` in every new zygote. That chromosome dance gives us the master key to Chapter 13:

1. **Two copies of every gene (`13.1`):** Because every diploid body cell carries **two sets of homologous chromosomes**—one maternal homologue inherited from your mother's ovum and one paternal homologue inherited from your father's sperm—you carry **two copies (`alleles`) of every autosomal gene**. What happens when those two copies carry slightly different DNA base sequences (Chapter 11), and how did an Augustinian friar in Brno show, in work published in 1866, that alleles behave like discrete, unblending packets rather than mixed paint?
2. **The `2 × 2` Punnett ledger and family pedigrees (`13.2`):** How does meiotic segregation (`2n → n`) combine with random fertilisation (`n + n → 2n`) to generate the famous **`3 : 1`**, **`1 : 2 : 1`**, and **`1 : 1`** ratios of monohybrid crosses, diagnostic test crosses, and clinical pedigree charts?
3. **Multiple alleles and codominance (`13.3`):** What happens when neither allele masks the other—as with **`Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ`** in the three-allele human **ABO blood group** system on Chromosome `9`?
4. **Sex chromosomes (`XX` vs `XY`) and sex-linked inheritance (`13.4`):** How does the twenty-third chromosome pair give an expected **`1 : 1` ratio of `XX` to `XY` zygotes** in a simple model, and why do recessive alleles located on the **`X` chromosome**—such as **red–green colour vision deficiency** and **haemophilia**—strike males far more often than females?

## 13.1 The monk in the pea patch: why traits don't blend, and how genes come in alleles

:::scene St Thomas's Abbey, Brno (Moravia), 1856–1866

Walk into the walled kitchen garden of St Thomas's Abbey in Brno in the spring of 1856, and you would find a thirty-three-year-old Augustinian friar named **Johann Gregor Mendel** kneeling beside rows of climbing garden peas (*Pisum sativum*) with a pair of jeweller's forceps, a camel-hair paintbrush, and a ruled ledger book.

Before Mendel began his eight-year pea investigation, scientists had no agreed explanation of inheritance. One tempting model was **blending inheritance**. According to the blending view, the hereditary material of the mother and the father mixed irreversibly inside the offspring like a pot of red paint stirred into a pot of white paint to make pink paint. Once mixed, red and white could never be separated again.

Trained in experimental physics and combinatorial probability at the University of Vienna, Mendel investigated how parental characters reappear in later generations. Garden peas offered a way to test whether inherited instructions blend or remain distinct:

- **Strict pollination control:** A pea flower's reproductive organs (pollen-making anthers and ovule-holding carpel) are sealed inside a boat-shaped keel petal, so pea plants naturally **self-pollinate**. By snipping off a flower bud's immature anthers with forceps (**emasculation**) before its pollen ripened and dusting the stigma with pollen from a chosen partner plant, **Mendel** could control the exact parentage of every seed.
- **Sharp, either-or characters:** After two years (`1854–1856`) of testing thirty-four pea varieties to confirm which lines **bred true** (producing identical offspring generation after generation when self-pollinated), he selected **seven distinct, non-overlapping characters**—such as **round versus wrinkled seeds**, **yellow versus green seed cotyledons**, **purple versus white flowers**, and **tall (`1.8–2.1 m`) versus dwarf (`0.3–0.45 m`) stems**.
- **Large sample sizes:** A single pea cross yields dozens of dry seeds that can be counted like beads in a tray, allowing thousands of independent fertilisations to be scored in a few seasons.

:::predict
Suppose **Mendel** crosses a **pure-breeding round-seeded pea plant** with a **pure-breeding wrinkled-seeded pea plant** to produce first-generation (**`F₁`**) hybrid seeds, and then allows those `F₁` plants to self-pollinate to produce second-generation (**`F₂`**) seeds. If the **paint-pot blending hypothesis** were true, what should the `F₁` and `F₂` seeds look like—and what did **Mendel** actually observe?
- Blending predicts 100% round F₁ seeds and 75% round : 25% wrinkled F₂ seeds; Mendel observed 100% half-wrinkled F₁ and F₂ seeds
- Blending predicts semi-wrinkled intermediate F₁ seeds that stay semi-wrinkled forever in F₂; Mendel observed 100% round F₁ seeds, and in F₂ the wrinkled trait returned completely unblemished in roughly 25% of the seeds (a 3 : 1 ratio of round to wrinkled)
- Blending predicts 50% round and 50% wrinkled seeds in both F₁ and F₂; Mendel observed 100% wrinkled seeds in F₁
???
The second choice. If maternal and paternal instructions blended like paint, crossing round with wrinkled would produce intermediate, mildly dimpled `F₁` seeds, and self-pollinating those `F₁` plants could never recover a purely wrinkled seed. Instead, **Mendel** observed that **100% of the `F₁` seeds were completely smooth and round**. Even more strikingly, when those round `F₁` plants self-pollinated to form `F₂` seeds, **roughly one quarter (`25.3%`) of the `F₂` seeds were completely wrinkled**, indistinguishable from the original wrinkled grandparent. The wrinkled instruction had lain hidden inside the `F₁` plants for an entire generation without being diluted away.
:::

### Genes, loci, and alleles on homologous chromosomes

Why did **Mendel**'s pea traits come in pairs (`RR`, `Rr`, `rr`) inside every parent plant, separate to single copies (`R` or `r`) inside sperm and eggs, and rejoin in pairs at fertilisation? Because **Mendel** was tracking the behaviour of **homologous chromosomes** forty years before **William Bateson** coined the word *genetics*—and decades before **Rosalind Franklin** helped reveal DNA's structure and **Har Gobind Khorana** helped decipher its triplet code (Chapter 11).

Let us lock in the exact Cambridge definitions that connect Chapter 11 (DNA and proteins) and Chapter 12 (meiosis and chromosomes) to Chapter 13:

- ==Inheritance== is the **transmission of genetic information from generation to generation** (**0610 `17.4.1 Core`**).
- A ==gene== is a **length of DNA that codes for a protein** (**0610 `17.1.2 Core`**). Every gene sits at a fixed physical address—called its ==locus== (plural *loci*)—along a specific chromosome.
- An ==allele== is an **alternative form of a gene** (**0610 `17.1.3 Core`**). All alleles of the same gene occupy the **exact same locus** on homologous chromosomes and govern the same feature, but they differ slightly in their DNA base sequence (`A`, `T`, `C`, `G`).
- Because a **diploid (`2n`)** cell contains **two sets of homologous chromosomes**—one maternal homologue and one paternal homologue in each pair—we track **two inherited allele copies for each autosomal gene** (one on each homologue). Before division, DNA replication makes a sister copy of each; it does not add another parental version. During **meiosis (`2n → n`)**, homologous chromosomes separate into different **haploid (`n`) gametes**, so **each gamete carries only one allele** of each gene.

When we compare the two alleles at a single locus inside a diploid organism, three combinations are possible:

| Term | Definition (`0610 17.4`) | Pea Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Homozygous** | Having **two identical alleles** of a particular gene (`RR` or `rr`). Two identical homozygous individuals that breed together will be **pure-breeding** (`0610 17.4.6–17.4.7`). | **`RR`** (homozygous dominant, pure round) or **`rr`** (homozygous recessive, pure wrinkled) |
| **Heterozygous** | Having **two different alleles** of a particular gene (`Rr`). A heterozygous individual is **not pure-breeding** (`0610 17.4.8–17.4.9`). | **`Rr`** (`F₁` hybrid; round seed phenotype, but passes `r` to `50%` of its gametes) |
| **Genotype vs Phenotype** | **Genotype** is the **genetic make-up of an organism in terms of the alleles present** (`RR`, `Rr`, `rr`); **phenotype** is the **observable features of an organism** (`0610 17.4.4–17.4.5`). | Genotypes **`RR`** and **`Rr`** both produce the **round seed** phenotype; **`rr`** produces **wrinkled** |

:::evidence Brno, Moravia, 1856–1866: Johann Gregor Mendel's 19,959-outcome F₂ ledger and F₃ progeny verification | Quantitative breeding proof
In his 1866 monograph *Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden* (*Experiments on Plant Hybrids*), **Mendel** reported the exact counts from his `35 m × 7 m` abbey plot across all seven pea characters:

1. **Uniformity in the `F₁` generation:** Regardless of whether the round-seeded parent supplied the egg inside an ovule or sperm delivered by pollen—a **reciprocal cross**—100% of the `F₁` hybrid offspring displayed only one of the two parental traits (e.g. **round seeds**, **yellow cotyledons**, **purple flowers**, **tall stems**). **Mendel** named the trait that appeared in `100%` of `F₁` hybrids **dominant**, and the trait that vanished in `F₁` **recessive** (from the Latin *recedere*, "to step back").
2. **The `3 : 1` split across `19,959` `F₂` outcomes:** When the `F₁` hybrids self-pollinated, the recessive trait reappeared across all seven characters in ratios close to the expected **`3 : 1`** (`75%` dominant : `25%` recessive):
    - **Seed shape (`7,324` seeds):** `5,474` round to `1,850` wrinkled = **`2.96 : 1`** (`74.74%` : `25.26%`).
    - **Seed cotyledon colour (`8,023` seeds):** `6,022` yellow to `2,001` green = **`3.01 : 1`** (`75.06%` : `24.94%`).
    - **Flower / seed-coat colour (`929` plants):** `705` purple to `224` white = **`3.15 : 1`** (`75.89%` : `24.11%`).
    - **Ripe pod shape (`1,181` plants):** `882` inflated to `299` constricted = **`2.95 : 1`**.
    - **Unripe pod colour (`580` plants):** `428` green to `152` yellow = **`2.82 : 1`**.
    - **Flower position (`858` plants):** `651` axial to `207` terminal = **`3.14 : 1`**.
    - **Stem length (`1,064` plants):** `787` tall (`1.8–2.1 m`) to `277` dwarf (`0.3–0.45 m`) = **`2.84 : 1`**.
    - **Combined ledger total (`19,959` outcomes):** **`14,949` dominant (`74.90%`) to `5,010` recessive (`25.10%`) = `2.98 : 1`**.
3. **The `F₃` progeny test proving the hidden `1 : 2 : 1` ratio:** **Mendel** did not stop at `F₂`. He planted `565` round `F₂` seeds and let each grown plant self-pollinate to form `F₃` seeds. If all round `F₂` seeds were genetically identical, all `565` should behave alike. Instead, **`193` plants (`34.2%`) bred true** (producing only round seeds), while **`372` plants (`65.8%`) segregated `3 : 1` again** just like `F₁` hybrids—a **`1 : 1.93` ratio** (`1 : 2` theoretical). Combined with the `25%` pure wrinkled `F₂` seeds, this proved that the `3 : 1` visible ratio is actually a **`1 : 2 : 1` genetic ratio** (`¼` pure dominant : `½` hybrid : `¼` pure recessive).

### Why is one allele dominant and the other recessive? The enzyme bridge

Students often picture a **dominant allele** as a "bully" that physically attacks or deletes the **recessive allele** on the partner chromosome. Look back at Chapter 4 (enzymes as folded 3D protein catalysts, whose polypeptide conformation was mapped by **G. N. Ramachandran**) and Chapter 11 (how DNA base sequences are transcribed into mRNA and translated by ribosomes into enzymes). The two homologous chromosomes in a nucleus do not fight each other at all; an allele can still be copied into RNA even when its protein does not produce the visible trait. For the pea genes below, one working copy can supply enough useful protein.

Here is the real molecular reason why **`R` (round)** is dominant and **`r` (wrinkled)** is recessive—discovered when Cathie Martin, Alison Smith, and Madan Bhattacharyya sequenced **Mendel**'s seed-shape gene in 1990:

1. **The dominant allele (`R`) codes for a functional enzyme:** On pea Chromosome `5`, the `R` allele is an intact DNA sequence coding for **starch-branching enzyme isoform I (`SBEI`)**. Inside a developing pea seed, `SBEI` adds branches to growing glucose chains, helping make the branched starch **amylopectin**.
2. **The recessive allele (`r`) codes for a non-functional enzyme:** In wrinkled peas, the `r` allele contains an `800`-base-pair (`0.8 kb`) transposon-like DNA insertion right inside the coding sequence. The insertion disrupts the normal RNA message and prevents production of functional `SBEI`. Other enzymes can still make some starch, so wrinkled peas are not starch-free.
3. **Why `rr` seeds wrinkle (osmosis from Chapter 3):** In a homozygous recessive (`rr`) seed with **no functional `SBEI`**, soluble sugars, especially **sucrose**, pile up in the cytoplasm. Those dissolved sugars lower the seed's **water potential**, drawing extra water into the young seed by **osmosis** and swelling it up. Later, as the ripe pod dries out in the sun, the swollen `rr` seed loses that extra water and its stretched seed coat collapses into deep **wrinkles**.
4. **Why `Rr` heterozygotes are completely round (haplosufficiency):** In a heterozygous (`Rr`) seed, the `r` allele produces no working enzyme, but the single `R` allele on the homologous chromosome still transcribes normal mRNA and makes **enough active `SBEI` enzyme** for the round-seed phenotype. This is called **haplosufficiency**: one working copy is sufficient. Counting working copies does not tell us an exact enzyme percentage; protein amounts and reaction rates also depend on how cells regulate the gene. Consequently, `Rr` seeds have the **exact same round phenotype** as `RR` seeds.

A similar principle helps explain **Mendel**'s other pea traits: in flower colour (`B` vs `b`), `B` codes for a working transcription factor that switches on purple **anthocyanin** pigment synthesis, whereas `b` carries a single-base splice mutation (`G → A`) that produces no pigment (**white petals**); in stem height (`T` vs `t`), `T` codes for active **gibberellin `3β-hydroxylase`** enzyme that builds growth hormone `GA₁` for tall internodes (`1.8–2.1 m`), whereas `t` carries an amino-acid substitution (`Ala²²⁹ → Thr²²⁹`) near the active site that greatly reduces, but does not eliminate, enzyme activity and hormone production, leaving `tt` plants dwarf (`0.3–0.45 m`).

- A ==dominant allele== is an **allele that is expressed if it is present in the genotype** (whether in one copy `Rr` or two copies `RR`; **`0610 17.4.2 Core`**).
- A ==recessive allele== is an **allele that is only expressed when there is no dominant allele of the gene present in the genotype** (only in the homozygous recessive state `rr`; **`0610 17.4.3 Core`**).

:::note
**Letter choice in genetic diagrams:** Always use the **uppercase letter of the dominant trait** for the dominant allele (e.g. `R` for round, `B` for purple/blue-violet, `T` for tall) and the **matching lowercase letter** for the recessive allele (`r` for wrinkled, `b` for white, `t` for dwarf)—never two different letters like `R` for round and `W` for wrinkled. Where uppercase and lowercase look similar in handwriting (`C/c`, `S/s`, `W/w`, `P/p`), write curved or serifed uppercase letters or choose letters with unmistakable shapes (`A/a`, `B/b`, `D/d`, `R/r`, `T/t`).

:::five
? What is the difference between a gene and an allele?
A **gene** is a specific length of DNA at a fixed chromosome address (locus) that codes for a protein (such as the starch-branching enzyme gene in peas or the beta-globin gene in humans). An **allele** is one of the alternative versions of that gene (such as the functional `R` allele versus the non-functional `r` allele), differing slightly in its DNA base sequence.
? Why does a diploid organism have two alleles for each gene, while a gamete has only one?
A diploid (`2n`) organism inherited two complete sets of homologous chromosomes—one set from its mother's egg and one set from its father's sperm—so it has two copies of every chromosome and two alleles for every autosomal gene. In animals, **meiosis (`2n → n`)** separates homologous chromosome pairs during sperm or egg production. Each gamete receives one chromosome from each pair and one allele per autosomal gene. In peas, meiosis starts the process that separates alleles; the egg lies inside an ovule, and pollen produces and delivers sperm. An ovule is not an egg, and a pollen grain is not a sperm cell.
? Why is a heterozygous `Rr` pea seed just as round as a homozygous `RR` pea seed?
Because the `R` allele codes for a working starch-branching enzyme (`SBEI`). Even though the `r` allele in an `Rr` seed makes no working enzyme, the single `R` copy makes enough working enzyme to help build branched starch and keep the seed smooth and round.
? What does it mean to say that two homozygous individuals are pure-breeding, whereas a heterozygous individual is not?
A homozygous individual (`RR` or `rr`) carries two identical alleles, so 100% of its gametes carry that same allele; crossing two identical homozygotes (`RR × RR` or `rr × rr`) produces offspring that are 100% identical to the parents generation after generation (**pure-breeding**). A heterozygous individual (`Rr`) produces `50%` `R` gametes and `50%` `r` gametes, so self-crossing (`Rr × Rr`) splits the offspring into round (`RR`, `Rr`) and wrinkled (`rr`) seeds (**not pure-breeding**).
? Can two organisms have the exact same phenotype but different genotypes?
Yes. In simple dominance, a homozygous dominant pea (`RR`) and a heterozygous pea (`Rr`) have **different genotypes** (`RR` versus `Rr`), but both have the **exact same phenotype** (round seed) because a single `R` allele is expressed whenever it is present.
?? How did Johann Gregor Mendel prove that the round seeds in his F₂ generation were actually a 1 : 2 mixture of two different genotypes (`RR` and `Rr`)?
He planted `565` round `F₂` seeds and let each grown plant self-pollinate to produce `F₃` seeds. One third (`193` plants) bred true and produced only round seeds (proving their `F₂` genotype was `RR`), whereas two thirds (`372` plants) segregated in a `3 : 1` ratio of round to wrinkled seeds (proving their `F₂` genotype was heterozygous `Rr`).
?? Why did Carl Nägeli's suggestion to test hawkweed (*Hieracium*) fail to reproduce Mendel's 3 : 1 ratios, and how does E. K. Janaki Ammal's cytogenetics work (Chapter 12) explain why?
Mendel's `3 : 1` ratio requires sexual reproduction—specifically **meiosis** separating alleles into haploid gametes followed by **fertilisation** fusing sperm and egg nuclei. Hawkweed (*Hieracium*) frequently forms seeds asexually without meiosis or fertilisation (**apomixis**), producing maternal clones just like the mitotic sugarcane stem cuttings studied by **E. K. Janaki Ammal**.
?? If a recessive allele (`r`) is not expressed in the phenotype of an `Rr` F₁ hybrid, why doesn't it weaken or disappear over ten generations of heterozygotes?
Because an allele is a covalent sequence of DNA nucleotides (`A`, `T`, `C`, `G`) on a chromosome. Even when the `r` allele makes a non-functional protein, **semi-conservative DNA replication** copies the `r` chromosome base-for-base before every cell division with the same one-in-a-billion proofreading accuracy as the `R` chromosome, passing it intact into `50%` of the gametes during meiosis.
:::

:::careful
Two frequent traps to avoid when using `dominant` and `recessive`:

1. **"Dominant" does NOT mean "more common in the population" or "stronger/healthier":** Dominance only describes which allele is expressed inside a **heterozygote (`Aa`)**. Whether an allele is common or rare in a population depends on natural selection and history (Chapter 14), not on whether it is dominant. For example, in humans, some forms of **polydactyly** (extra fingers or toes) and **Huntington's disease** can be caused by **dominant alleles**, yet those alleles are uncommon. Conversely, **Blood Group O (`IᴼIᴼ`)** is recessive, yet it is the single most common blood group in many human populations.
2. **Never write "a dominant gene" or "a recessive gene":** Everyone has the *same genes* (the seed-shape gene, the flower-colour gene, the beta-globin gene). Always write **"a dominant allele"** or **"a recessive allele"**.

## 13.2 The Punnett ledger: monohybrid crosses, test crosses, and family pedigrees

:::scene University of Cambridge, 1900–1905, and Tata Memorial Centre, Parel, Mumbai, 1950s

When **Mendel**'s 1866 paper was rediscovered in the spring of 1900, Cambridge biologist **William Bateson** read it on the train to London to address the Royal Horticultural Society and realised that biology had found its atomic theory. Over the next five years in Cambridge, **William Bateson** coined the terms **genetics**, **allele**, **homozygote**, and **heterozygote**, while his collaborator **Reginald Punnett** introduced a simple visual matrix—the **`2 × 2` Punnett square**—that turns meiotic chromosome segregation and random fertilisation into a foolproof accounting grid.

Every **monohybrid cross** (a genetic cross tracking **one gene locus** with two alleles) follows the same **six-line genetic diagram protocol**:

1. **Parental phenotypes:** Write the observable feature of each parent (e.g. `Purple flower × Purple flower`).
2. **Parental genotypes (`2n`):** Write **two allele letters** for each diploid parent (e.g. `Bb × Bb`).
3. **Gametes (`n`):** Because **meiosis halves the chromosome number (`2n → n`)** by separating homologous chromosomes, each haploid gamete receives **only one allele letter**. Always draw a circle around each gamete allele—or write `(B)` and `(b)`—to show it is a separate haploid cell (`50%` `B` and `50%` `b` from a `Bb` parent).
4. **`2 × 2` Punnett square:** Place the two maternal gametes along the left side and the two paternal gametes along the top. Fill each of the `4` interior boxes by combining **one maternal gamete (`n`) + one paternal gamete (`n`) → one diploid zygote (`2n`)**, writing the uppercase allele first (`Bb`, never `bB`). Each box represents one equally likely combination, with probability **`1/4` (`25%` or `0.25`)**. The boxes are alternatives for one fertilisation; separate fertilisations are independent.
5. **Offspring genotypes and phenotypes:** Read the `4` boxes (`1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb`) and translate each genotype into its observable phenotype (`3 Purple : 1 White`).
6. **Expected ratios / probabilities:** Express both the **genotype ratio (`1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb`, or `25% : 50% : 25%`)** and the **phenotype ratio (`3 dominant : 1 recessive`, or `75% : 25%`)** in simplest whole numbers.

### The diagnostic test cross: unmasking a hidden recessive allele

Look closely at the `3 : 1` outcome of `Bb × Bb`. The white-flowered offspring (`25%`) have an unmistakable genotype: because white is recessive, every white-flowered pea plant **must be homozygous recessive (`bb`)**.

What about a purple-flowered pea plant (`B_`)? By sight alone, you **cannot tell** whether its genotype is **homozygous dominant (`BB`)** or **heterozygous (`Bb`)**, because both genotypes make enough anthocyanin enzyme to colour the petals deep purple. How can a plant breeder or geneticist discover which genotype a dominant-phenotype individual carries?

:::predict
A plant breeder in Pune has a prize **purple-flowered pea plant (`B_`)** and wants to know whether it is **pure-breeding (`BB`)** or a **heterozygous carrier (`Bb`)** of the white-flower allele. Which partner should she cross the mystery purple plant with to reveal a hidden recessive allele in the next generation—and what offspring ratios distinguish `BB` from `Bb`?
- Cross the mystery plant with a homozygous recessive white-flowered tester plant (`bb`, a **test cross**); if the mystery plant is `BB`, 100% of the offspring will be purple (`Bb`), whereas if the mystery plant is `Bb`, the offspring will split in a **1 : 1 ratio** (`50%` purple `Bb` : `50%` white `bb`)
- Cross the mystery plant with a homozygous dominant purple plant (`BB`); if it is `Bb`, 25% of the offspring will have white flowers
- Cross the mystery plant with a known heterozygote (`Bb`); only a `3 : 1` ratio can reveal a hidden allele
???
The first choice. Crossing with `BB` (the second choice) is useless because `BB` gives a dominant `B` allele to every single gamete, making `100%` of the offspring purple regardless of whether the mystery plant is `BB` or `Bb`. By crossing the mystery plant (`B_`) with a **homozygous recessive tester (`bb`)**—a procedure now called a ==test cross==, of the kind Mendel used—every tester gamete contributes only the recessive allele `(b)`. Therefore the offspring phenotypes directly display the gametes of the mystery parent:

- **If the mystery parent is homozygous dominant (`BB × bb`):** All gametes from the mystery parent are `(B)`, so **100% of the offspring are heterozygous (`Bb`) and purple-flowered** (`0%` white).
- **If the mystery parent is heterozygous (`Bb × bb`):** Half of its gametes are `(B)` and half are `(b)`, producing **`50%` purple (`Bb`) and `50%` white (`bb`) offspring—an expected `1 : 1` ratio**. Even a **single white-flowered (`bb`) offspring** proves beyond doubt that the purple parent carried a hidden `b` allele. An all-purple sample supports `BB` but does not prove it: a `Bb` parent can also produce only purple offspring by chance. For four offspring, that chance is `(1/2)⁴ = 1/16`.
:::

:::evidence Brno, 1866 & Cambridge, 1905: Mendel's test-cross verifications and Reginald Punnett's gamete matrix | Experimental & algebraic proof
In his 1866 paper, **Mendel** used test crosses (`Rr × rr` and `Yy × yy`) to test the prediction that `F₁` hybrids produce dominant and recessive gametes in an expected **`1 : 1` (`50% : 50%`) ratio** in both egg and sperm cells:

1. **Reciprocal test-cross counts:** Crossing `F₁` hybrid plants (`Rr Yy`) with pure recessive plants (`rr yy`)—first using the hybrid as the female seed parent and then as the male pollen parent—yielded numbers close to `1 : 1` at each locus, supporting the conclusion that female meiosis and male meiosis segregate alleles with identical `50% : 50%` fairness.
2. **Summary of the four fundamental monohybrid crosses:** Here are four useful patterns for a single gene with complete dominance. `AA × AA` gives all `AA`, and `aa × aa` gives all `aa`.

| Parental Cross | Offspring Genotypes | Offspring Phenotypes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. `AA × aa`** (Pure dom × Pure rec) | **`100% Aa`** (all heterozygous) | **`100%` dominant** : `0%` recessive |
| **2. `Aa × Aa`** (Heterozygote × Heterozygote) | **`1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa`** (`25% : 50% : 25%`) | **`3` dominant : `1` recessive** (`75% : 25%`) |
| **3. `Aa × aa`** (Heterozygote test cross) | **`1 Aa : 1 aa`** (`50% : 50%`) | **`1` dominant : `1` recessive** (`50% : 50%`) |
| **4. `AA × Aa`** (Homozygous dom × Heterozygote) | **`1 AA : 1 Aa`** (`50% : 50%`) | **`100%` dominant** (`50%` carriers) |

### Reading family trees: two powerful pedigree clues

With pea plants or fruit flies, geneticists can perform controlled test crosses and count hundreds of offspring. With human families, we cannot design experimental crosses, and most families have only a few children. Instead, human geneticists—like **L. D. Sanghvi** at the Tata Memorial Centre in Mumbai—trace inheritance across three or four generations using a **pedigree diagram** (**0610 `17.4.11 Core`**):

- **Squares (`□` / `■`)** represent **males**; **circles (`○` / `●`)** represent **females**.
- **Unshaded symbols (`□`, `○`)** represent **unaffected** individuals; **shaded symbols (`■`, `●`)** represent **affected** individuals showing the trait or condition.
- A **horizontal marriage/mating line** connects two parents; a **vertical line** drops to a horizontal sibling bracket listing their children in birth order from left to right across numbered generations (`I`, `II`, `III`).
- An unaffected individual who carries one hidden copy of a recessive allele (`Aa`) is called a **heterozygous carrier**.

Whenever you are handed an unfamiliar pedigree chart in a clinic or an examination, look immediately for a **parent–child triad** where the child's phenotype differs from **both** parents. For the next pedigrees, assume **one autosomal gene, no new mutations, and that everyone with the condition-causing genotype shows the condition**. Within that model, two powerful clues let us deduce the parents' genotypes. Real clinicians also consider new variants, age of onset, and variants that do not always cause visible symptoms.

1. **Pedigree Detective Rule 1 (`Unaffected × Unaffected → Affected child` proves RECESSIVE):**
    - Suppose two **unaffected parents** (`I-1` and `I-2`, unshaded) have an **affected child** (`II-2`, shaded).
    - **Deduction:** The condition **must be caused by a recessive allele (`a`)**, the affected child **must be homozygous recessive (`aa`)**, and **both unaffected parents must be heterozygous carriers (`Aa`)**.
    - **Why?** The affected child (`aa`) had to inherit one `a` allele from each parent. Because both parents are unaffected, each parent must also have a dominant normal allele (`A`) masking `a`. If the condition were dominant, an affected child (`A_`) would have had to inherit `A` from at least one parent, making that parent affected too. Thus this recessive allele can pass silently through an unaffected carrier. Outside our model, a dominant condition can also appear to skip a generation if someone carrying its allele does not show symptoms.
2. **Pedigree Detective Rule 2 (`Affected × Affected → Unaffected child` proves DOMINANT):**
    - Suppose two **affected parents** (`I-1` and `I-2`, shaded) have an **unaffected child** (`II-2`, unshaded).
    - **Deduction:** The condition **must be caused by a dominant allele (`A`)**, the unaffected child **must be homozygous recessive (`aa`)**, and **both affected parents must be heterozygous (`Aa`)**.
    - **Why?** If the condition were recessive, two affected parents would both be `aa × aa` and could pass only `a` alleles, making `100%` of their children affected (`aa`). Having even one unaffected child (`aa`) proves both affected parents carried a hidden recessive normal allele (`a`).

**The Two-Coin & Two-Dal Mendel Ledger (Mumbai Kitchen Investigation)**

*Educational adaptation disclosure:* This safe household probability model is a non-trialled educational adaptation designed to demonstrate how independent gamete segregation (`50% : 50%`) generates `1 : 2 : 1` genotype ratios, `3 : 1` phenotype ratios, `1 : 1` test crosses, and convergence from small (`N = 16`) to larger (`N = 64`) samples. It uses only dry kitchen items or coins (`0` chemicals, `0` heat, `0` biological fluids).

**What you need:** Two identical Indian coins (`₹1`, `₹2`, or `₹5`, where **Heads = dominant allele `R`** and **Tails = recessive allele `r`**)—or two small bowls each holding `20` pale **kabuli chana (chickpeas = `R`)** and `20` dark **kala chana (dark chickpeas = `r`)** mixed thoroughly—plus a notebook and pen.

**Part A — Simulating a monohybrid `Rr × Rr` cross (`N = 16` vs `N = 64`):**

1. **Small-family run (`16` fertilisations):** Toss both coins together (or draw one bean blindfolded from Bowl 1 and one from Bowl 2, returning the beans and remixing after each draw) **16 times**. Coin 1 represents the **maternal egg (`R` or `r`)**; Coin 2 represents the **paternal sperm (`R` or `r`)**. Use the coins if the two kinds of chickpea feel different when you draw them. Tally each outcome in four columns: **`RR` (Heads–Heads, Round)**, **`Rr` (Heads–Tails or Tails–Heads, Round)**, **`rr` (Tails–Tails, Wrinkled)**, and **Total Round (`RR + Rr`) vs Wrinkled (`rr`)**.
2. **Check the small-sample bounce:** With only `16` trials, your theoretical expectation is `4 RR : 8 Rr : 4 rr` (`12` Round : `4` Wrinkled = `3 : 1`), but random luck often gives `10 : 6` (`1.67 : 1`) or `14 : 2` (`7 : 1`). That is why a single pea pod or a human family of four children rarely shows an exact `3 : 1` split.
3. **Full `64`-trial convergence run:** Continue tossing for `48` more trials to reach **`N = 64` total fertilisations** (expecting `16 RR : 32 Rr : 16 rr`, or `48` Round : `16` Wrinkled). Compare the combined `RR + Rr` percentage with **`75%`**. Did it move closer? It need not do so in every run; larger samples have smaller *typical* percentage fluctuations. There are two routes to `Rr` (`Heads–Tails` + `Tails–Heads`), so its expected probability (`50%`) is twice that of `RR` or `rr` (`25%` each).

**Part B — Simulating a `1 : 1` diagnostic test cross (`Rr × rr`):**

1. Lay Coin 2 flat on the table showing **Tails (`r`)** permanently—this is your **homozygous recessive tester (`rr`)**, which can only ever supply `(r)` gametes.
2. Toss only Coin 1 (`Rr` parent) **32 times**. Every Heads gives **`Rr` (Round)**; every Tails gives **`rr` (Wrinkled)**. Compare your result with the expected **`50% Rr : 50% rr` (`1 : 1`)**. Thirty-two tosses need not split exactly in half; record what happened rather than changing the counts to fit.

:::note
**Why does `Aa` occur twice in `Aa × Aa`?** When you toss two coins, there is only **one** way to get `AA` (Maternal `A` + Paternal `A`) and only **one** way to get `aa` (Maternal `a` + Paternal `a`), but there are **two distinct ways** to get `Aa`: either Maternal `A` + Paternal `a`, or Maternal `a` + Paternal `A`. That two-way symmetry is why the middle term of `(0.5 A + 0.5 a)² = 0.25 AA + 0.50 Aa + 0.25 aa` is always `2` (`1 : 2 : 1`).

:::five
? Why do we draw only ONE allele letter inside each gamete circle in a genetic diagram?
Because sperm and egg cells are **haploid (`n`)**. In animals, **meiosis (`2n → n`)** produces the cells from which they develop. In peas, meiosis leads through haploid stages to eggs inside ovules and sperm produced by pollen. During Meiosis I, the two homologous chromosomes carrying the parent's two alleles separate into different daughter cells, so each gamete receives only one chromosome and therefore only one allele of the gene.
? Why do we write a `1 : 2 : 1` genotype ratio as a `3 : 1` phenotype ratio when `A` is dominant over `a`?
In `Aa × Aa`, `1/4` (`25%`) of the offspring are homozygous dominant (`AA`) and `2/4` (`50%`) are heterozygous (`Aa`). Because `A` is dominant, both `AA` and `Aa` express the exact same dominant phenotype (`25% + 50% = 75%`, or `3/4`), leaving `1/4` (`25%`) homozygous recessive (`aa`) with the recessive phenotype (`3 : 1`).
? How does a test cross tell you whether a tall pea plant (`T_`) is `TT` or `Tt`?
You cross the tall plant (`T_`) with a dwarf plant that is homozygous recessive (`tt`). Since the `tt` tester passes only `t` gametes, if the tall parent is `TT` all offspring receive `T` and are `100%` tall (`Tt`); if the tall parent is `Tt`, half the offspring receive `t` from both parents and are dwarf (`tt`), giving a `1 : 1` ratio of tall to dwarf.
? In a family pedigree, how can you tell immediately that a genetic condition is recessive?
Within our single-gene, no-new-mutation model, look for **two unaffected parents** (`□` and `○`) who have an **affected child** (`■` or `●`). Because the condition was hidden in both parents and appeared in their child (`aa`), the allele must be recessive and both parents must be heterozygous carriers (`Aa`).
? If two carrier parents (`Aa × Aa`) already have three children—two unaffected and one affected (`aa`)—what is the probability that their fourth child will be affected?
Still **exactly `25%` (`1 in 4`, or `0.25`)**. Chromosomes and gametes have no memory of earlier pregnancies; every new fertilisation is an independent `2 × 2` Punnett event with the same `1/4` chance of `aa`.
?? Suppose two unaffected carrier parents (`Aa × Aa`) have a daughter who is **already born and known to be unaffected**. What is the probability that she is a heterozygous carrier (`Aa`), and why is the answer `2/3` rather than `1/2`?
Before birth, the four equally likely Punnett boxes are `1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa` (`25%` each). Once the daughter is born and observed to be **unaffected**, the `aa` box is ruled out (`0%`). Out of the **three remaining equally likely boxes (`1 AA` and `2 Aa`)**, two boxes are `Aa`, so her probability of being a carrier is **`2/3` (`66.7%`)**.
?? In a pedigree where two affected parents have an unaffected daughter, could the condition be recessive? Explain why or why not.
Not in our single-gene, fully expressed model. If the condition were recessive, both affected parents would have to be homozygous recessive (`aa × aa`). Since neither parent would possess a normal `A` allele, `100%` of their gametes would be `(a)` and `100%` of their children would be affected (`aa`). Having an unaffected child (`aa`) proves the condition is **dominant** and both parents are heterozygous (`Aa`).
?? Why did Johann Gregor Mendel count 7,324 seeds to get 2.96 : 1, whereas a single pea pod of 8 seeds might show 5 round : 3 wrinkled (1.67 : 1)?
Each fertilisation is an independent random sampling event with a `75%` chance of round and `25%` chance of wrinkled. In a small sample of `8` seeds, random chance causes large percentage swings (sampling error); by the law of large numbers, averaging across thousands of independent fertilisations (`7,324` seeds) cancels out random fluctuations and converges on the theoretical `3.00 : 1` expectation.
:::

:::careful
Watch out for three classic Punnett and pedigree mistakes:

1. **Gametes carry 1 allele, never 2:** In a monohybrid cross of `Bb × Bb`, the gametes are **`(B)` and `(b)`**—never `(BB)` or `(Bb)`. Putting two letters of the same gene into one gamete means meiosis failed to separate homologous chromosomes.
2. **Probabilities do not "use up" squares across siblings:** A `1 in 4` (`25%`) chance of an `aa` child in `Aa × Aa` does **not** mean that if the first child is `aa`, the next three children are guaranteed to be unaffected. Every pregnancy draws fresh gametes from the `2 × 2` grid with the same `25%` probability.
3. **Always write ratios in simplest whole numbers:** Write **`3 : 1`** (not `75% : 25%` or `12 : 4` when a ratio is asked for) and **`1 : 1`** (not `2 : 2`), and always label which phenotype belongs to which number (e.g. **`3 round : 1 wrinkled`**).

## 13.3 When neither allele yields: codominance and the ABO blood group puzzle

:::scene Institute of Pathological Anatomy, Vienna, 1900–1901, and Indian Cancer Research Centre, Parel, Mumbai, 1950s

In **Mendel**'s seven pea characters, one allele was always completely dominant over the other: a heterozygous `Rr` seed looked identical to an `RR` seed. Soon after 1900, however, geneticists discovered traits where **neither allele masks the other** inside a heterozygote.

In 1900–1901 in Vienna, physician **Karl Landsteiner** solved the mystery of why blood transfusions between some people succeeded while others caused fatal clumping (**agglutination**) of red blood cells. As you learned in Chapter 8 (`8.4`) and Chapter 10 (`10.4`), the cell membranes of human red blood cells carry carbohydrate identity tags (**antigens**), while blood plasma carries Y-shaped **antibodies** against whichever foreign ABO antigens the person lacks. In 1924, mathematician Felix Bernstein showed that the inheritance of the four ordinary ABO blood groups—**A**, **B**, **AB**, and **O**—fits **three alleles at one locus**. Researchers mapped this locus to **Chromosome `9`** decades later, and in the 1950s **L. D. Sanghvi** mapped the frequencies and family pedigrees of those alleles across Mumbai and western India.

### Multiple alleles and codominance at the Chromosome 9 ABO locus

We will use the standard three-allele ABO model here. Rare variants and changes in other genes can alter blood typing; for example, the **Bombay phenotype** lacks the H-antigen precursor needed to display A or B. This is why a real identity investigation uses more evidence than an ABO grid.

When a single gene locus has three or more alternative alleles circulating in a population (such as `Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, and `Iᴼ`, where `I` stands for *isoagglutinogen*, Landsteiner's term for a blood-group antigen), geneticists call it a **multiple-allele system**. Remember the diploid chromosome rule from Section 13.1: even though **three alleles (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`) exist in the human population**, any individual person has only **two copies of Chromosome `9`** (`2n`), so **each person inherits at most two ABO alleles** (and each haploid gamete carries **only one**).

In 1990, Fumi-ichiro Yamamoto and colleagues sequenced the `ABO` gene on Chromosome `9` (`9q34.2`) and revealed the exact enzyme chemistry of the three alleles:

1. **Allele `Iᴬ` (codes for active enzyme `GTA`):** Instructs ribosomes to build **`α-1,3-N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase` (`GTA`)**, an enzyme that attaches the sugar **N-acetylgalactosamine (`GalNAc`)** onto the precursor **H-antigen** oligosaccharide stalks on red blood cell membranes, creating **Antigen A**.
2. **Allele `Iᴮ` (codes for active enzyme `GTB`):** Differs from `Iᴬ` at just **four amino-acid positions** (codons `176, 235, 266, 268`). Those four substitutions reshape the enzyme's active site (Chapter 4) so it binds and attaches a different sugar—**D-galactose (`D-Gal`)**—onto H-antigen stalks, creating **Antigen B**.
3. **A common `Iᴼ` allele (loss-of-function frameshift `ΔG261`):** Carries a **single-base deletion (`G` deleted at nucleotide `261`)** that shifts the mRNA triplet reading frame (Chapter 11) and hits an early stop codon, producing a truncated protein with **zero transferase activity (`0%`)**. The H-antigen stalks remain unmodified (neither Antigen A nor Antigen B is added).

Now ask: what happens when a child inherits **`Iᴬ` on one Chromosome `9` and `Iᴮ` on the partner Chromosome `9` (`IᴬIᴮ`)**? Neither enzyme blocks the other. Inside the developing red blood cell precursor in the bone marrow, **both `GTA` and `GTB` enzymes work simultaneously**, attaching **Antigen A (`GalNAc`)** to some membrane stalks and **Antigen B (`D-Gal`)** to neighbouring stalks on the **exact same red blood cell**.

- ==Codominance== is a pattern of inheritance in which **both alleles in a heterozygous organism are expressed independently and simultaneously in the phenotype** (**0610 `17.4.15 Supplement`**).
- Because both `Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ` are expressed side-by-side without blending, **`Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ` are codominant with each other** (`IᴬIᴮ` = **Blood Group AB**).
- At the same time, **both `Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ` are completely dominant over the recessive `Iᴼ` allele**: a single active copy of `Iᴬ` in **`IᴬIᴼ`** attaches enough Antigen A to give **Blood Group A** (identical in routine blood typing to **`IᴬIᴬ`**), and a single active copy of `Iᴮ` in **`IᴮIᴼ`** attaches enough Antigen B to give **Blood Group B** (identical to **`IᴮIᴮ`**). In this model, only **`IᴼIᴼ`** (no active ABO transferase copies) produces **Blood Group O**.

| **Group A** | **`IᴬIᴬ`** (homozygous) or **`IᴬIᴼ`** (heterozygous) | **Antigen A** on RBCs; **Anti-B antibodies** in plasma |
| **Group B** | **`IᴮIᴮ`** (homozygous) or **`IᴮIᴼ`** (heterozygous) | **Antigen B** on RBCs; **Anti-A antibodies** in plasma |

Four siblings in the same family can legally and biologically have **all four human ABO blood groups (`1 AB : 1 A : 1 B : 1 O`)**.

:::evidence Vienna, 1901, Göttingen, 1924 & Seattle, 1990: Landsteiner's agglutination groups, Bernstein's 3-allele model, and Yamamoto's ABO sequence | Serological & molecular proof
Three milestones established the ABO system as biology's benchmark for multiple alleles and codominance:

1. **Karl Landsteiner (`1901`):** Mixing washed red blood cells from laboratory colleagues with separated blood serum proved that humans fall into discrete agglutination groups (`A`, `B`, `O`, and `AB` added by Decastello and Sturli in `1902`).
2. **Felix Bernstein (`1924`):** Analysing family blood-group pedigrees across thousands of parents and children proved that `A`, `B`, and `O` are not inherited at two separate gene loci (`A/a` and `B/b`), under a simple independent two-locus model. That model predicts `AABB × aabb → all AaBb`, all expressing both antigens in the first generation. Instead, because **`IᴬIᴮ × IᴼIᴼ` parents produce exclusively Group A (`IᴬIᴼ`, `50%`) and Group B (`IᴮIᴼ`, `50%`) children—never Group AB (`0%`) and never Group O (`0%`)**—these family results support three alleles (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`) occupying the **exact same locus** on a single chromosome pair.
3. **Fumi-ichiro Yamamoto *et al.* (`1990`, *Nature* `345`: 229–233):** Cloning the cDNA of the `Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, and `Iᴼ` alleles on Chromosome `9` confirmed the four amino-acid substitutions between `GTA` (`Iᴬ`) and `GTB` (`Iᴮ`) and the `ΔG261` single-base deletion in `Iᴼ`.

### Why codominance gives a `1 : 2 : 1` phenotype ratio instead of `3 : 1`

What happens when two **codominant heterozygotes** breed together—such as two **Group AB parents (`IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ`)**, or two **roan cattle (`CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ`)** where `Cᴿ` codes for red hairs and `Cᵂ` codes for white hairs so the heterozygote `CᴿCᵂ` grows both pure red hairs and pure white hairs side-by-side in a roan coat?

Trace the `2 × 2` Punnett grid for **`IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ`**:

- **Parental phenotypes:** Group AB mother × Group AB father
- **Parental genotypes:** `IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ`
- **Gametes:** `(Iᴬ)` and `(Iᴮ)` × `(Iᴬ)` and `(Iᴮ)`
- **Offspring genotypes (`1 : 2 : 1`):** `25% IᴬIᴬ` : `50% IᴬIᴮ` : `25% IᴮIᴮ`
- **Offspring phenotypes (`1 : 2 : 1`):** **`25%` Group A (`1`) : `50%` Group AB (`2`) : `25%` Group B (`1`)** (`0%` Group O).

Notice the key difference from **Mendel**'s `3 : 1` pea crosses: in **simple dominance (`Rr × Rr`)**, the `RR` (`25%`) and `Rr` (`50%`) boxes collapse into a single phenotype (`75%` round, `3 : 1`). In **codominance (`IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ` or `CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ`)**, the heterozygote (`IᴬIᴮ` or `CᴿCᵂ`) has its **own distinct third phenotype** where both alleles are visibly expressed, so the **phenotype ratio is `1 : 2 : 1`—matching the `1 : 2 : 1` genotype ratio directly**. (Recall from Chapter 11 that at the molecular protein level, **sickle-cell trait `HbᴬHbˢ`** also shows codominance: red blood cells contain **both** normal `HbA` and sickle `HbS` haemoglobin molecules.)

:::fig c13_dominance_vs_codominance() | Side-by-side comparison of Simple Dominance (Rr × Rr → 1 : 2 : 1 genotype ratio collapses into a 3 : 1 phenotype ratio because Rr and RR look identical) versus Codominance (IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ or CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ → 1 : 2 : 1 phenotype ratio directly mirrors the 1 : 2 : 1 genotype ratio because the heterozygote expresses both alleles simultaneously without blending).

**Superscript notation for codominance (`0610 17.4.15`):** Because uppercase-versus-lowercase (`R` vs `r`) implies that one allele is dominant over the other, Cambridge `0610` uses a **common base letter for the gene** with **uppercase superscripts** for codominant alleles—such as **`Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ`** for ABO blood groups (with **`Iᴼ`** for the recessive allele at the same locus), **`Cᴿ` and `Cᵂ`** for coat/petal colour, and **`Hbᴬ` and `Hbˢ`** for beta-globin alleles.

:::five
? What is codominance?
**Codominance** is a pattern of inheritance in which **both alleles in a heterozygous organism are expressed simultaneously and independently in the phenotype**, so neither allele masks the other (for example, `IᴬIᴮ` produces both Antigen A and Antigen B on red blood cells, giving Blood Group AB).
? Why does each human inherit only two ABO alleles even though three alleles (`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, `Iᴼ`) exist in the population?
Because the `ABO` gene sits at a single locus on **Chromosome `9`**, and every diploid (`2n`) human inherits **only two copies of Chromosome `9`**—one from their mother's ovum (`n`) and one from their father's sperm (`n`).
? Why is Blood Group AB an example of codominance, whereas Blood Group A (`IᴬIᴼ`) is an example of simple dominance?
In **`IᴬIᴮ` (Group AB)**, both `Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ` code for active sugar-transferring enzymes (`GTA` and `GTB`), so both Antigen A and Antigen B are built side-by-side on the red blood cell membrane (**codominance**). In **`IᴬIᴼ` (Group A)**, `Iᴼ` codes for a non-functional enzyme while the single `Iᴬ` copy builds enough Antigen A to give full Group A blood, so `Iᴬ` is **dominant** over recessive `Iᴼ`.
? Why can't a parent with Blood Group AB (`IᴬIᴮ`) ever have a biological child with Blood Group O (`IᴼIᴼ`) in standard Mendelian inheritance?
A child with **Blood Group O (`IᴼIᴼ`)** must inherit one `Iᴼ` allele from their mother and one `Iᴼ` allele from their father. Because a **Group AB parent has the genotype `IᴬIᴮ`**, their gametes carry either `(Iᴬ)` or `(Iᴮ)`—they do not possess an `Iᴼ` allele to pass on.
? Why does crossing two codominant heterozygotes (`IᴬIᴮ × IᴬIᴮ` or `CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ`) give a `1 : 2 : 1` phenotype ratio instead of `3 : 1`?
In `3 : 1` simple dominance, the homozygous dominant (`25%`) and heterozygous (`50%`) genotypes look identical and merge into `75%`. In codominance, the heterozygote (`IᴬIᴮ` or `CᴿCᵂ`, `50%`) expresses **both** alleles and looks distinct from both homozygotes (`IᴬIᴬ` `25%` and `IᴮIᴮ` `25%`), so all three genotypes are visibly distinguishable in a **`1 : 2 : 1` phenotype ratio**.
?? Why isn't codominance a return to the nineteenth-century paint-pot blending hypothesis?
Two reasons: (1) At the cellular and molecular level, the two alleles in a codominant heterozygote do not blend into a lukewarm compromise molecule—in `IᴬIᴮ` cells, discrete **Antigen A (`GalNAc`)** molecules and discrete **Antigen B (`D-Gal`)** molecules sit side-by-side on the membrane, and in roan cattle (`CᴿCᵂ`), individual hairs are either `100%` red or `100%` white. (2) When two `IᴬIᴮ` parents have children, pure **Group A (`IᴬIᴬ`, `25%`)** and pure **Group B (`IᴮIᴮ`, `25%`)** segregate out unblemished in the very next generation.
?? Can two parents who both have Blood Group A ever have a child with Blood Group O? Show the Punnett logic.
Yes, if **both Group A parents are heterozygous (`IᴬIᴼ × IᴬIᴼ`)**. Each parent produces `50%` `(Iᴬ)` gametes and `50%` `(Iᴼ)` gametes. Their `2 × 2` Punnett grid yields `25% IᴬIᴬ` (Group A), `50% IᴬIᴼ` (Group A), and **`25% IᴼIᴼ` (Group O)**—a `3 Group A : 1 Group O` ratio.
?? If a mother is Group B and her baby is Group O (`IᴼIᴼ`), what is the mother's exact genotype, and which blood groups can be ruled out for the biological father?
Because the baby is `IᴼIᴼ`, the baby inherited one `Iᴼ` allele from the mother and one `Iᴼ` allele from the father. Therefore the Group B mother **must be heterozygous `IᴮIᴼ`**. The biological father must also carry at least one `Iᴼ` allele (`IᴬIᴼ`, `IᴮIᴼ`, or `IᴼIᴼ`), which rules out **Blood Group AB (`IᴬIᴮ`)** (as well as homozygous `IᴬIᴬ` and `IᴮIᴮ`).
:::

1. **Keep the base letter `I` on all three ABO alleles:** Always write **`Iᴬ`, `Iᴮ`, and `Iᴼ`**—never mix `Iᴬ` and `Iᴮ` with a bare lowercase `o` or `i` in Cambridge `0610` exams (`17.4.17`).

## 13.4 The twenty-third pair: XX, XY, and sex-linked inheritance

:::scene Bryn Mawr College, Pennsylvania, 1905, and the Fly Room, Columbia University, New York, 1910

In Sections `13.1` to `13.3`, every gene we tracked—pea seed shape (`R/r`), stem height (`T/t`), and human `ABO` blood group on Chromosome `9` (`Iᴬ/Iᴮ/Iᴼ`)—behaved identically in males and females. That is because **22 of the 23 homologous chromosome pairs** in a human diploid cell (`2n = 46`) are **autosomes** (`Chromosomes 1` to `22`), which are present in two matched copies of equal length and equal gene content in both sexes.

What about the **twenty-third pair**—the **sex chromosomes** first linked to sex determination by **Nettie Stevens** in 1905?

1. **Human females are `XX` (`44` autosomes + `XX` = `46`):** In a female diploid cell, Pair `23` consists of **two large, homologous `X` chromosomes**. When a woman's ovaries produce haploid egg cells (**ova**, `n = 23`) by meiosis, every single ovum receives `22` autosomes plus **one `X` chromosome (`100%` `X` ova)**.
2. **Human males are `XY` (`44` autosomes + `XY` = `46`):** In a male diploid cell, Pair `23` consists of **one large `X` chromosome** and **one much smaller `Y` chromosome**. When a man's testes produce haploid **sperm cells (`n = 23`)** by meiosis, the `X` and `Y` chromosomes separate into different cells: the expected proportions are **`50%` of sperm carrying an `X` chromosome (`22 + X`) and `50%` carrying a `Y` chromosome (`22 + Y`)**.

Place whole `X` and `Y` chromosomes into a `2 × 2` Punnett square (`XX × XY`):

- **Maternal gametes (ova):** `(X)` and `(X)`
- **Paternal gametes (sperm):** `(X)` and `(Y)`
- **Zygotes (`2 × 2`):** Two boxes are **`XX` (`50%` female)** and two boxes are **`XY` (`50%` male)**—an expected **`1 : 1` ratio** in this model (**`0893 8Bp.01/9Bp.01`**, **`0610 17.1 & 17.4`**).

Most females are `XX` and most males are `XY`; variations in sex chromosomes and development also occur. Actual birth ratios need not be exactly `1 : 1`. For the following crosses we use the typical `XX`/`XY` model and equal fertilisation probabilities.

Notice two immediate consequences of this model:

- **Sperm chromosome sets the zygote's genetic sex:** Because every ovum supplies an `X`, whether the zygote is `XX` (female) or `XY` (male) depends on whether an `X`-bearing sperm or a `Y`-bearing sperm fertilises the ovum.
- **Who gives which sex chromosome to sons versus daughters:** A mother passes one of her two **`X` chromosomes to every child** (both daughters and sons). A father passes his single **`X` chromosome to `100%` of his daughters (`XX`)** and his **`Y` chromosome to `100%` of his sons (`XY`)**—a father **never** passes an `X` chromosome to a son (`0%`).

:::fig c13_xx_xy_sex_determination() | Comparison of the human X chromosome (~156 Mb, ~850–900 protein-coding genes) and Y chromosome (~57 Mb, <100 protein-coding genes including SRY), alongside the whole-chromosome 2 × 2 Punnett square (XX × XY → 50% XX female : 50% XY male = 1 : 1 ratio) and Nettie Stevens's 1905 discovery.

### Why X-linked recessive traits strike males far more often than females

Look closely at the physical size difference in Figure `13.7` between the **`X` chromosome** and the **`Y` chromosome**:

- The human **`X` chromosome** is a long rod of roughly **`156` million base pairs (`~156 Mb`)** carrying **`~850–900` protein-coding genes**—most of which have nothing to do with sex determination at all, but instead build everyday body proteins such as **red-sensitive and green-sensitive retinal cone opsin photopigments** (Chapter 9, Section `9.4`) and **blood-clotting proteins Factors VIII and IX** (Chapter 8, Section `8.4`, and Chapter 10, Section `10.4`).
- The human **`Y` chromosome** is a short rod of roughly **`57` million base pairs (`~57 Mb`)** carrying **fewer than `100` protein-coding genes** (most notably the **`SRY`** gene that triggers testis development in an embryo). Crucially, the short `Y` chromosome **lacks the non-homologous portion of the `X` chromosome** and therefore carries **zero alleles** for red–green colour vision or clotting Factor VIII/IX.

That structural asymmetry between `X` and `Y` creates a distinct pattern of inheritance discovered by **Thomas Hunt Morgan** in his 1910 fruit-fly crosses:

- A ==sex-linked characteristic== is a **characteristic in which the gene responsible is located on a sex chromosome and that this makes it more common in one sex than in the other** (**0610 `17.4.16 Supplement`**).
- Because the allele sits on the `X` chromosome and **not** on the `Y` chromosome, we write the allele as a **superscript on `X` only** (e.g. **`Xᴮ`** for normal colour vision and **`Xᵇ`** for red–green colour vision deficiency; or **`Xᴴ`** for normal blood clotting factor and **`Xʰ`** for **haemophilia**, where soluble plasma fibrinogen cannot be rapidly converted into an insoluble fibrin mesh to seal wounds). The **`Y` chromosome is always written bare (`Y`)**—never with a superscript (`Yᵇ` is wrong because `Y` lacks that gene locus).

Compare five genotypes in our simple model of an **X-linked recessive condition** such as red–green colour vision deficiency (`Xᴮ` = normal opsin, `Xᵇ` = altered opsin) or haemophilia (`Xᴴ` = normal clotting factor, `Xʰ` = non-functional clotting factor):

| Sex & Genotype | Phenotype & Clinical Status | Why (`X` vs `Y` Gene Dosage) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Female `XᴮXᴮ`** (or `XᴴXᴴ`) | **Normal** female (homozygous dominant) | Two functional copies on her two `X` chromosomes |
| **Female `XᴮXᵇ`** (or `XᴴXʰ`) | **Carrier** female (heterozygous; often unaffected) | Usually enough cells express the working allele for normal function; haemophilia carriers can have bleeding symptoms |
| **Female `XᵇXᵇ`** (or `XʰXʰ`) | **Affected** female (homozygous recessive; **rare**) | In this inherited two-copy example, the altered allele comes from both parents |
| **Male `XᴮY`** (or `XᴴY`) | **Normal** male (hemizygous dominant) | His single `X` chromosome carries the functional `Xᴮ` allele |
| **Male `XᵇY`** (or `XʰY`) | **Affected** male (hemizygous recessive; **much more common**) | His single `X` carries `Xᵇ`, and his short `Y` chromosome has **no second locus** to mask it |

The diagram is a useful model, not a complete clinical diagnosis. **Some women with one altered `F8` or `F9` allele have bleeding symptoms or haemophilia.** Early in development, one X chromosome in each body cell is largely switched off (**X-inactivation**); the balance of cells using each X can differ. `F8` and `F9` are separate genes, and the notation below represents either one at a time.

Notice two inheritance patterns in the model:

1. **Why males cannot be unaffected carriers of an X-linked recessive allele:** A carrier is an individual who has a recessive allele masked by a dominant allele on a second homologous chromosome (`XᴮXᵇ`). Because an `XY` male has **only one `X` chromosome** (he is **hemizygous**), any recessive allele (`Xᵇ` or `Xʰ`) on his single `X` chromosome is immediately expressed in his phenotype.
2. **Why X-linked recessive conditions are much more common in males than in females:** A male has only one X chromosome, so one altered copy can cause the condition. In the simple two-copy model, a female needs an altered copy on each X. Red–green colour vision deficiency is therefore much more common in males, but its frequency varies between populations. Figures such as about `1 in 12` men and `1 in 200` women describe populations of Northern European ancestry, not a universal frequency for India or the world.

:::evidence Bryn Mawr, 1905 & Columbia University, 1910: Nettie Stevens's mealworm XY karyotypes and Thomas Hunt Morgan's white-eyed Drosophila | Cytogenetic & breeding proof
Two classic experiments anchored sex determination and sex linkage to physical chromosomes:

1. **Nettie Stevens (`1905`, *Studies in Spermatogenesis*):** Comparing stained metaphase chromosomes in the yellow mealworm beetle (*Tenebrio molitor*), **Nettie Stevens** showed that somatic female cells contain `20` large chromosomes (`XX`) and produce uniform `10`-chromosome eggs, whereas somatic male cells contain `19` large chromosomes plus `1` diminutive chromosome (`XY`) and produce two equal classes of sperm (`50% X` and `50% Y`), proving that sex is inherited as a `1 : 1` Mendelian chromosome cross at fertilisation.
2. **Thomas Hunt Morgan (`1910`, *Science* `32`: 120–122):** Crossing a spontaneous **white-eyed male fruit fly (`XʷY`)** with his **red-eyed sisters** produced `1,237` red-eyed `F₁` offspring **and three white-eyed males** in Morgan's original report. An idealised cross with a homozygous red-eyed female (`X⁺X⁺`) predicts all red-eyed `F₁` offspring; this reconstruction should not erase those three recorded exceptions. Interbreeding those `F₁` flies yielded `3,470` red-eyed and `782` white-eyed `F₂` flies—and **100% of the white-eyed `F₂` flies were male (`XʷY`)**. When **Thomas Hunt Morgan** then crossed a carrier female (`X⁺Xʷ`) with a white-eyed male (`XʷY`), **`50%` of the daughters (`XʷXʷ`) and `50%` of the sons (`XʷY`) had white eyes**, proving that the eye-colour gene resides physically on the `X` chromosome.

:::five
? What are the sex chromosomes in a human female and in a human male, and why does our model give `50% XX` and `50% XY` (`1 : 1`)?
A human female has **`XX`** sex chromosomes and produces `100%` `(X)` egg cells; a human male has **`XY`** sex chromosomes and produces **`50%` `(X)` sperm and `50%` `(Y)` sperm**. At fertilisation, an `(X)` egg has an equal `50%` chance of fusing with an `(X)` sperm (`XX`, girl) or a `(Y)` sperm (`XY`, boy), giving a `1 : 1` ratio.
? What is a sex-linked characteristic?
A **sex-linked characteristic** is a feature controlled by a gene located on a **sex chromosome** (usually on the non-homologous region of the large **`X` chromosome**, with no corresponding locus on the shorter `Y` chromosome), making the characteristic more common in one sex than in the other.
? Why can't a male (`XY`) be an unaffected carrier of red–green colour blindness (`Xᵇ`) or haemophilia (`Xʰ`)?
Because a male has **only one `X` chromosome** (`XY`) and the short `Y` chromosome does not carry the colour-vision or blood-clotting gene. If his single `X` chromosome carries the recessive allele (`XᵇY` or `XʰY`), there is no second `X` chromosome with a dominant allele to mask it, so he shows the condition.
? Why can't a father with haemophilia (`XʰY`) or red–green colour blindness (`XᵇY`) pass the condition directly to his son?
Because a father always gives his son his **`Y` chromosome** (which makes the child `XY` male), never his `X` chromosome. The son's single `X` chromosome always comes from his **mother's ovum**.
? Why is it critical to check whether an X-linked question asks for the probability "among sons" versus "among all children"?
In `XᴮXᵇ × XᴮY`, the `2 × 2` Punnett grid has **`4` boxes total (`2` daughters and `2` sons)**, and **only `1` box (`XᵇY`) is affected**. If the question asks for the probability **among all children**, the denominator is `4` (`1/4 = 25%`). If the question asks for the probability **that a son is affected** (among sons only), the denominator is the `2` male boxes (`1/2 = 50%`).
?? Assume no new mutation occurred. A boy has haemophilia (`XʰY`), yet both his mother and his father have completely normal blood clotting. State the exact genotypes of the boy, his father, and his mother, and explain how the boy inherited `Xʰ`.
- **Boy's genotype:** **`XʰY`** (affected male).
- **Father's genotype:** **`XᴴY`** (normal male; if he had `Xʰ`, he would have haemophilia himself, and he gave the boy his `Y` chromosome anyway).
- **Mother's genotype:** **`XᴴXʰ`** (unaffected heterozygous carrier female). Her `Xᴴ` allele produces enough clotting Factor VIII/IX for her own blood to clot normally, while meiosis placed her hidden `Xʰ` allele into the ovum (`50%` chance) that formed her son.
?? If a colour-blind man (`XᵇY`) and a homozygous normal-vision woman (`XᴮXᴮ`) have children, none of their sons or daughters are colour-blind—yet their daughter later has a colour-blind son with a normal-vision husband (`XᴮY`). Trace the `Xᵇ` allele across all three generations.
1. **Generation I (`XᴮXᴮ × XᵇY`):** The affected grandfather (`XᵇY`) passes his `(Xᵇ)` sperm to **`100%` of his daughters**, making his daughter an **unaffected carrier (`XᴮXᵇ`)** in Generation II.
2. **Generation II → III (`XᴮXᵇ × XᴮY`):** When that carrier daughter (`XᴮXᵇ`) has children with a normal-vision father (`XᴮY`), she passes `(Xᵇ)` to `50%` of her ova, giving each of her sons (Generation III) a **`50%` chance of inheriting `XᵇY`** and being colour-blind like his maternal grandfather.
?? Why do defects in the X-linked `Xᴴ/Xʰ` gene cause prolonged bleeding in Chapter 10's blood clotting pathway, whereas defects in the X-linked `Xᴮ/Xᵇ` gene alter colour vision in Chapter 9's retina?
Different loci on the `X` chromosome code for completely different proteins via **selective gene expression (`12.3`)**: cells in the liver express the separate **`F8` and `F9` genes** to supply clotting proteins. Factor VIII is a helper protein (a **cofactor**) for activated Factor IX in an upstream clotting reaction. These reactions help make **thrombin**, the enzyme that converts **soluble fibrinogen → insoluble fibrin fibres** at wounds (`10.4`), whereas foveal **cone photoreceptors (`9.4`)** express the **red/green opsin genes (represented together by `Xᴮ` in this simple diagram)** to detect red and green wavelengths of light.

Three essential exam rules for sex-linked genetic diagrams (`0610 17.4.19–17.4.20`):

1. **Always write the `X` and `Y` chromosomes in genotypes and gametes:** For sex-linked traits, never write bare letters like `Bb` or `Hh` as if the gene were autosomal. Always write **`XᴮXᵇ`**, **`XᴮY`**, **`XᵇY`** (or **`XᴴXʰ`**, **`XᴴY`**, **`XʰY`**), and write gametes as **`(Xᴮ)`**, **`(Xᵇ)`**, and **`(Y)`**.
2. **Never attach an X-linked superscript to `Y`:** Write **`XᴮY`** and **`XᵇY`**—never `XᴮYᵇ` or `XYᵇ`. The `Y` chromosome lacks the non-homologous X-linked locus.
3. **Check the denominator ("of all children" vs "of sons" vs "of daughters"):** Always write both numbers clearly in your answer (e.g. *"25% of all children, which is 50% of the sons"*), so your answer is unambiguous under any mark scheme.

1. **Case A — Maternity ward ABO proof (`13.1–13.3`):**
    - **Genotypes of Couple 1 (Desai):** Mother is Group AB = **`IᴬIᴮ`** (codominant heterozygote); Father is Group O = **`IᴼIᴼ`** (homozygous recessive).
    - **Punnett proof for Couple 1 (`IᴬIᴮ × IᴼIᴼ`):** The mother's ova carry `(Iᴬ)` (`50%`) or `(Iᴮ)` (`50%`)—never `(Iᴼ)`. The father's sperm carry only `(Iᴼ)` (`100%`). Therefore `100%` of Couple 1's biological children must be either **`IᴬIᴼ` (Group A, `50%`)** or **`IᴮIᴼ` (Group B, `50%`)**. At the molecular level on Chromosome `9`, every child of Couple 1 inherits either a functional `GTA` allele (`Iᴬ`, adding GalNAc = Antigen A) or a functional `GTB` allele (`Iᴮ`, adding D-Gal = Antigen B) from the mother paired with a non-functional `Iᴼ` allele from the father (the common `ΔG261` deletion is one example), so **Group O (`IᴼIᴼ`, `0%`) and Group AB (`IᴬIᴮ`, `0%`) are impossible for Couple 1**.
    - **Genotypes & Punnett proof for Couple 2 (Kulkarni, `IᴬIᴼ × IᴮIᴼ`):** Because both Kulkarnis had a Group O (`IᴼIᴼ`) parent, the Group A mother is **`IᴬIᴼ`** and the Group B father is **`IᴮIᴼ`**.     - **Father:** **`XᴮY`** (normal hemizygous male). He gave his son his **`(Y)` chromosome** (which carries no colour-vision locus), so the son could **not** inherit `Xᵇ` from his father.
    - **Six-year-old son:** **`XᵇY`** (affected male), having inherited `(Xᵇ)` from his carrier mother's ovum and `(Y)` from his father's sperm.
    - **Eight-year-old daughter:** Either **`XᴮXᴮ` (`50%` chance among daughters, homozygous normal)** or **`XᴮXᵇ` (`50%` chance among daughters, unaffected carrier)**, because she definitely received `(Xᴮ)` from her father's X-sperm and had an equal chance of receiving `(Xᴮ)` or `(Xᵇ)` from her mother's ovum.

3. **Next-pregnancy probabilities & autosomal comparison (`13.1–13.4`):**
    - **(a) Probability of a boy (`XY`):** **`50%` (`1 : 1`)**, independent of the two older children, because paternal meiosis produces `50%` `(X)` sperm and `50%` `(Y)` sperm.
    - **(b) X-linked recessive probability (`XᴮXᵇ × XᴮY`):** Among **sons (`XY`)**, the probability of red–green colour vision deficiency (`XᵇY`) is **`50%` (`1 in 2` sons)**; among **daughters (`XX`)**, the probability of being affected is **`0%`** (`50%` are `XᴮXᵇ` carriers); among **all children**, the probability of an affected child is **`25%` (`1 in 4`)**, and `100%` of affected children in this cross are male.
