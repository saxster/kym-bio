In Chapter 0 we saw that every living organism on Earth today—from a bacterium in a drop of puddle water to a banyan tree—comes from an existing living parent. That raises an immediate, sharper question. What *is* "being alive"?

Ask ten people on the street how to tell whether something is alive, and they will hand you a familiar checklist: living things move, breathe, eat, grow, and respond when you poke them. Yet nature embarrasses a surface checklist. A candle flame moves, grows, consumes wax fuel, takes in oxygen, gives out carbon dioxide and heat, and can even ignite a second candle—yet a flame is not alive. Meanwhile, a dry green *moong* bean sitting in a glass jar in your kitchen is as hard, cool, and still as a pebble—yet it is alive. Why? Cracking that paradox takes us straight to the physical foundations of biology: water, coupled free energy, molecular machines, and an inherited code.

## 1.1 The first rains in June

:::scene Sanjay Gandhi National Park, Mumbai, early June

Walk up the basalt trail towards Kanheri Caves in the last week of May, before the monsoon arrives. The hillsides look lifeless. The grass is brittle straw; the earth is baked into cracked brown brick; the rock pools are bone-dry dust. If an alien landed on those hills in late May, it might conclude that almost nothing on the ground was alive.

After the first heavy rains, brown hillsides turn green. Shoots emerge and pools refill. Different organisms return in different ways: seeds germinate, some tiny aquatic animals hatch from resistant resting eggs, and frogs breed in the new water. Tadpoles develop from frog eggs, not from dry mud cysts. There is no single timetable or survival mechanism for the whole hillside.

Where was the next generation of grass hiding through the dry weeks? Much of it waited inside seeds. Pick up a dry green *moong* bean (*Vigna radiata*), a chickpea, or a mustard seed. A viable seed contains a living embryo, stored nutrients, and cellular machinery that can resume development when conditions permit.

As many desiccation-tolerant seeds dry, their cell contents become extremely viscous and form a sugar-rich biological **glass**, a physical transition called *vitrification*. Molecular movement is severely restricted, and metabolism slows enormously. Not every motion or chemical change stops: dry seeds can still deteriorate over time. Water content, temperature, and the seed's composition govern this state. A seed that looks unchanged is not guaranteed to remain viable forever.

Adding liquid water softens this crowded glassy interior and allows the reactions required for germination to resume. Water uptake is called **imbibition**. Water enters through permeable regions of the seed coat; a single pore is not a universal explanation for all seeds. Once hydrated, the embryo oxidises stored nutrients in cellular respiration, supplying ATP for growth even in complete darkness.

## 1.2 The flame and the bean

Now that we have watched a dry bean wake up, let us set it side by side with our rival suspect: a burning candle flame.

Look at why the candle flame fools a hasty checklist. A candle is made of solid hydrocarbon wax—chains of carbon and hydrogen atoms storing chemical potential energy, much like the starch and lipid reserves inside a seed. Touch a match to the wick:

- **Fuel consumption:** The flame draws melted wax up the wick and oxidises it.
- **Gas exchange:** It consumes oxygen (O₂) from the room air and releases carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water vapour (H₂O)—the same gases a sprouting bean or a running human exchanges.
- **Response and spread:** It bends in a draught of air, grows taller if fed more wick, and ignites a second candle on contact.

Why, then, is the candle flame *not* alive, while the bean is?

To see the physical difference, step down to the nanometre scale—where chemistry and thermodynamics meet cell biology.

In the candle flame, vaporised wax reacts with oxygen through an uncoupled chain of reactions called **combustion**. Chemical potential energy transfers straight to the surroundings as random thermal motion (heat) and light. The flame has no membrane-bounded cells, no inherited instruction code, and no molecular machinery that couples fuel oxidation to cellular maintenance and construction. Multi-step chemistry alone is not life.

Now zoom inside a single cell of your hydrated *moong* bean at 28 °C. The bean cell also reacts glucose with oxygen to form CO₂ and H₂O. Whether a mole of glucose burns in an open flame or oxidises across a cell's pathways under the same start and end states, the total available free-energy change (Δ G°′ ≈ -2,870 kJ/mol under standard biochemical conditions) obeys the same thermodynamic conservation laws you met in [*The Moving World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-physics/).

The decisive difference is **how** that reaction proceeds and **what it is physically coupled to**:

:::fig c1_seed_vs_flame() | Both systems transform fuel. In the cell, enzyme-controlled pathways couple some energy transfers to ATP supply and cellular construction, while heat is also released. The flame lacks a cellular system of maintenance and inheritance. The staircase and 3D assembly are conceptual pictures, not literal molecular pathways.
:::live seed_flame3d

In the bean cell, folded protein catalysts called **enzymes** lower activation barriers so oxidation proceeds step by step at 28 °C. Those reaction steps physically link nutrient breakdown to the phosphorylation of ADP into ATP, and ATP reactions in turn drive active transport, repair, and polymer synthesis. That physical linkage between an energy-releasing reaction and an energy-requiring reaction on a shared enzyme or complex is called **coupling**. Warming a cell with a flame cannot substitute for coupled chemical intermediates.

A growing organ can gain structure while the whole seed in darkness loses net dry mass.

## 1.3 Paying for repairs, and SubbaRow’s ₹10 coin

:::scene Boston & Bhimavaram, 1929

A living cell builds ordered macromolecular structures while releasing heat and increasing the entropy of its surroundings. That obeys the second law of thermodynamics: the total entropy of an **isolated system** (cell plus environment) never decreases. Because a cell is an **open system** that continuously exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings, it maintains internal order by exporting heat and higher-entropy waste molecules (CO₂, H₂O) to the outside world.

In his 1943 Dublin lectures (*What Is Life?*), the physicist Erwin Schrödinger asked how organisms avoid decaying into thermodynamic equilibrium. For our waking *moong* bean, the question is concrete: how do chemical reactions using stored nutrients pay for building cell walls, pumping ions, and repairing proteins?

All the enzyme-catalysed chemical reactions running inside a cell have a single collective name: ==metabolism== (from the Greek *metabolē*, "change"). Metabolism links two complementary streams of reactions:
- **Catabolic pathways (fuel breakdown):** Degrading nutrient molecules such as glucose in cellular respiration, which lowers free energy; and
- **Anabolic pathways (cellular construction):** Assembling amino acids into proteins, nucleotides into DNA, or glucose units into cellulose walls, which requires an input of free energy.

:::note Metabolism
All the enzyme-catalysed chemical reactions taking place inside a living cell or organism—both catabolic pathways that break down fuel molecules and anabolic pathways that build complex molecules for growth and repair.
:::

Breaking down fuel and building cellular parts must be physically connected. Across all known cellular life, a primary chemical link between them is a small nucleotide molecule: ==ATP==, **adenosine triphosphate**.

In 1929, Yellapragada SubbaRow and Cyrus Fiske at Harvard Medical School, and independently Karl Lohmann in Germany, isolated ATP from muscle extracts. SubbaRow had grown up in Bhimavaram in Andhra Pradesh and studied medicine in Madras before developing the colorimetric phosphorus assay that made tracking ATP possible. Identifying the molecule in 1929 was the first step; over the following decades, Fritz Lipmann and other biochemists established how enzymes use ATP as a shared cellular energy carrier.

:::story p-subbarow

:::note ATP (adenosine triphosphate)
A principal rechargeable nucleotide energy carrier in cells, composed of adenine, ribose, and three phosphate groups. Enzymes couple ATP reactions (such as phosphoryl transfer or hydrolysis to ADP and Pᵢ) to cellular work.
:::

An ATP molecule consists of an adenine base, a five-carbon ribose sugar, and a chain of three negatively charged phosphate groups. When an enzyme catalyses the reaction of ATP with water (**hydrolysis**), the terminal phosphate group splits off, leaving **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate) and inorganic phosphate (Pᵢ):

:::eq
**ATP + H₂O ⇌ ADP + Pᵢ (Δ G°′ ≈ -30.5 kJ/mol)**
- ATP (`given` charged donor): Adenosine with three crowded, negatively charged phosphate groups
- ADP + Pᵢ (`moves` better-solvated products): Released products stabilised by resonance and water hydration shells
- Δ G°′ (`stays` whole-reaction free energy): Standard transformed free-energy change of the complete coupled system (actual cellular Δ G depends on concentrations)
:::

:::fig c1_atp_cycle() | A schematic of ATP and one common reaction cycle, ATP + water → ADP + Pᵢ. Re-forming ATP requires an energy-coupled reaction. Charges contribute to the chemistry, but the whole reaction and its conditions determine the free-energy change. Other ATP-dependent reactions can transfer phosphate to another molecule instead.

### How enzymes couple ATP to mechanical and chemical work

Follow one concrete mechanism: a motor protein such as myosin or kinesin binds ATP in its active pocket. Its catalytic cycle links ATP binding, hydrolysis, and product release to discrete changes in the protein's 3D shape, stepping the motor along a filament. Hydrolysing ATP in a separate beaker beside the motor accomplishes nothing: the chemical reaction and the protein's conformational cycle must be physically coupled on the same machine.

In biosynthetic pathways, a different enzyme transfers ATP's terminal phosphate group directly onto a substrate molecule (**phosphorylation**), raising that intermediate's free energy so a subsequent bond-forming step proceeds readily. There is no single phosphate-transfer recipe used by every cellular machine.

Why does the overall ATP reaction release usable free energy? Breaking any covalent bond—including the P–O bond in ATP—*requires* an input of energy. However, the complete hydrolysis reaction forms stronger, better-solvated bonds with water, relieves electrostatic repulsion along the triphosphate tail, and increases resonance stabilisation in Pᵢ. Because the products sit at a lower total Gibbs free energy than the reactants, the overall reaction can drive coupled work.

Think of ATP as a rechargeable ₹10 coin circulating rapidly between catabolic "cash dispensers" (respiration and photosynthesis) and anabolic "workstations":

:::work
How can a tiny standing pool of ATP support massive daily cellular work? Use **illustrative adult-scale assumptions** (not a direct measurement of your body): a 50 g standing ATP pool and 40,000 g (40 kg) of ATP turned over per day.

**40,000 g/day ÷ 50 g pool = 800 pool-equivalents per day**

**1,440 minutes/day ÷ 800 cycles = 1.8 minutes per pool-equivalent**

These numbers describe average turnover relative to an assumed pool; individual ATP molecules in active muscle vs resting tissue turn over on very different schedules.
:::

:::five ATP

A small nucleotide molecule used as a rechargeable free-energy carrier across cellular reactions.
? Why is it called this?
Adenosine combines the base adenine and the sugar ribose; triphosphate names the chain of three phosphate groups attached to the ribose (5′ carbon).

It bridges energy-releasing catabolic reactions (such as glucose oxidation) to energy-requiring cellular work (transport, movement, and biosynthesis) via shared enzymatic intermediates.
? Why does it work?

? How does it help?

?? What it isn't
It is not a long-term fuel reserve (starch and fat store long-term energy), not the cell's only energy carrier (GTP, NADH, and ion gradients also carry energy), and not a bond that releases energy merely by snapping.
?? Where does it stop working?
ATP in water without its specific coupling enzymes and reaction partners only warms the solution slightly when hydrolysed; it cannot perform work on its own.
:::

:::careful
> "Snapping ATP's end bond releases energy by itself."

Breaking a chemical bond always *absorbs* energy. Usable free energy comes from the **whole coupled reaction**—including the new bonds formed with water, relief of negative charge crowding, product hydration, and coupling on the enzyme's active site. An uncoupled ATP hydrolysis reaction occurring in bulk solution cannot drive a separate reaction elsewhere.
:::

:::technical How Rotary ATP Synthase Recharges the Coin
Inside mitochondria and bacterial membranes, protons (H⁺) flowing down an electrochemical gradient spin the F₀ rotor ring and central γ-shaft of **F₀F₁ ATP synthase** like a molecular waterwheel. Each 360° turn of the asymmetric γ-shaft forces three catalytic β-subunits in the F₁ head through open, loose, and tight conformations, mechanically pressing ADP and Pᵢ together into 3 ATP molecules (Chapter 6).
:::

## 1.4 Seven processes in a living system

Once you see what a living organism actually is—**a cell-based system that couples free-energy transfers to maintain internal order and copy its genetic instructions**—you no longer have to memorise a school textbook's "seven characteristics of living things" as an arbitrary list. Each process solves a concrete physical failure mode that would otherwise stop the cellular machine. Not every process is visible at every instant: a dry seed pauses rapid growth, and an individual organism can live a full life without reproducing.

In Cambridge IGCSE and British schools, the seven processes are summarised by the initials **MRS GREN**. Trace how each one keeps the cellular system running:

:::fig c1_mrs_gren_engine() | The seven MRS GREN processes as a single connected engineering diagram of a living organism. Nutrition brings in matter and fuel; Respiration transfers chemical energy from nutrients into forms including ATP; Excretion removes toxic chemical wastes made by metabolism (CO₂, urea); Sensitivity and Movement let the organism find resources and dodge danger; Growth builds new cellular structure; and Reproduction passes the DNA blueprint across generations.

1. **Movement** (*repositioning the body or its parts*): ==Movement== is an action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place. Animals contract muscle fibres; plants grow shoots toward light and open or close stomatal guard cells.
2. **Respiration** (*oxidising fuel to recharge ATP*): ==Respiration== is the set of chemical reactions inside cells that break down nutrient molecules (such as glucose) and release energy for metabolism. Active plant, animal, fungal, and bacterial cells respire continuously; dry dormant seeds restrict metabolic rates drastically until hydrated.
3. **Sensitivity** (*detecting environmental changes*): ==Sensitivity== is the ability to detect or sense stimuli in the internal or external environment and make appropriate responses, from a bacterial receptor sensing sugar to your iris narrowing in bright noon sun.
4. **Growth** (*building permanent new dry mass*): ==Growth== is a permanent increase in size and dry mass by an increase in cell number or cell size (or both). Soaking a dry sponge or a seed increases *wet* mass reversibly; biological growth synthesises new cellular polymers from within.
5. **Reproduction** (*copying the heritable instructions*): ==Reproduction== is the processes that make more of the same kind of organism, transmitting replicated DNA across generations before the parent body wears out.
6. **Excretion** (*removing metabolic waste products*): ==Excretion== is the removal from organisms of the waste products of metabolism, toxic materials, and substances in excess of requirements—such as CO₂ from cellular respiration and **urea** from deaminating excess amino acids in the liver.
7. **Nutrition** (*acquiring carbon skeletons, ions, and energy*): **Plants** absorb inorganic molecules (CO₂, H₂O, mineral ions) and use light to synthesise organic molecules; **animals and fungi** take in ready-made organic compounds.

:::note Respiration (biological definition)
Cellular chemical reactions that break down nutrient molecules (such as glucose) and release energy for metabolism (recharging ATP). Never confuse cellular respiration with *breathing* (ventilation), which is the muscular movement of air in and out of lungs.
:::

:::note Excretion vs. egestion
Excretion is the removal of waste products formed by chemical reactions *inside* cells (such as CO₂ from respiration and urea from amino acid breakdown), plus excess water and salts. **Egestion** is passing out undigested food (such as cellulose fibre in faeces) that travelled through the gut lumen without ever crossing into a body cell.
:::

:::predict
After you eat a bowl of sweetcorn or leafy greens, tough cellulose plant fibres pass all the way through your digestive tract without being broken down and leave your body in faeces. In biology, does passing faeces count as **excretion**?

The second choice. Think of your digestive canal as a hollow tube running through the middle of your body. A cellulose fibre that enters your mouth and leaves in faeces never crossed the gut epithelium into your bloodstream or entered a single cell's metabolism. Passing undigested leftovers out of the gut tube is **egestion**. True **excretion** removes molecules produced by chemical reactions *inside* your cells (like CO₂ exhaled from your lungs, or urea filtered out of your blood into urine by your kidneys)—plus water and salts in excess.
:::

:::careful
> "Animals breathe and respire, while plants only photosynthesise."

Plants have no lungs, yet their living cells carry out **cellular respiration** twenty-four hours a day in both light and darkness to recharge ATP. In a sunlit leaf, photosynthesis and respiration run simultaneously; net gas exchange depends on their relative rates. In darkness, photosynthesis stops while cellular respiration continues.
:::

### Signpost: Quick Audit to Separate Excretion From Egestion

:::tip Quick Audit to Separate Excretion From Egestion

If it merely travelled down the hollow gut tube without ever crossing a cell membrane (like undigested cellulose fibre in faeces), passing it out is **egestion**.

## 1.5 The outlaw at the border

Our working physical account of cellular life has three pillars: living systems consist of one or more membrane-bounded **cells**, run an enzyme-coupled **metabolism** using carriers such as **ATP** for maintenance and synthesis, and replicate inherited **DNA** instructions.

Then a nanometre-scale outlaw walks up to the border checkpoint and challenges our neat boundary.

:::scene St Petersburg, 1892 & Delft, 1898

In 1892, the Russian botanist Dmitri Ivanovsky crushed diseased tobacco leaves blotched with yellow-and-green mosaic spots and forced the sap through a Chamberland porcelain filter whose pores trapped every known bacterium. The crystal-clear filtrate that dripped through remained completely infectious. Six years later, the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck repeated the test, demonstrated that the invisible agent multiplied only inside living, dividing plant tissue, and named it a *contagium vivum fluidum*—a ==virus== (from the Latin word for "slimy liquid" or "poison").

:::note Virus
An infectious biological particle consisting of a DNA or RNA genome enclosed inside a protein coat (*capsid*), and in some species a lipid envelope. Viruses lack ribosomes, cytoplasm, and independent ATP-generating metabolism; they replicate only by hijacking a living host cell.
:::

When the electron microscope was built in the 1930s, researchers finally resolved viral particles directly. Compare a living bacterial cell with two viral architectures in Figure 1.5:

:::fig c1_virus_border() | The border of life: a living Bacterial Cell (left, ~2,000 nm long) vs. two virus examples, a T4 bacteriophage and an enveloped coronavirus (right, drawn at different enlargements so their parts are visible). The bacterium is a cell supplied by nutrients and its surroundings with a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, ATP generators and a circular DNA chromosome. The viruses lack ribosomes and independent ATP production. An enveloped virus also has a lipid covering; these schematic examples are not a universal size or shape rule.

Inspect what a virus **carries** versus what it **lacks**:
- **What it carries:** A nucleic-acid genome (either **DNA** or **RNA**) packaged inside a geometric **protein capsid** (built from repeating protein subunits, such as a 20-faced icosahedron with triangulation number T = h² + hk + k²), and in enveloped viruses like influenza or SARS-CoV-2, an outer lipid bilayer studded with glycoprotein spikes.
- **What it lacks:** It has **no cytoplasm**, **no ribosomes** (no protein-synthesis machinery), and **no independent ATP-producing pathways**. An outer lipid envelope is not a self-maintaining cellular membrane system.

Outside a host cell, a virion does not respire, grow, or synthesise proteins, though its molecules can still undergo chemical damage or conformational changes. A virus is more than disembodied software: its capsid or envelope physically binds host surface receptors and delivers the genome across the membrane—by tail-sheath contraction in T4-like bacteriophages, or by membrane fusion and endocytosis in enveloped animal viruses.

Once inside a susceptible host cell, **host ribosomes** translate viral messenger RNA into viral proteins, while **polymerase enzymes** (supplied by the host or encoded by the virus, depending on the viral class) copy the viral genome. Ribosomes synthesise proteins; they never copy DNA or RNA genomes. New capsids self-assemble around the copied genomes and exit by cell lysis or membrane budding.

:::predict
A child develops a fever and runny nose caused by an influenza **virus**. Someone suggests taking an **antibiotic** such as amoxicillin (which blocks bacterial peptidoglycan cell-wall cross-linking; other antibiotics bind bacterial 70S ribosomes). Will the antibiotic cure the viral flu?
- Yes, because antibiotics kill all microscopic pathogens including viruses
- No, because a virus has no bacterial cell wall and no ribosomes of its own for the antibiotic to target; unnecessary antibiotic use selects for resistant bacteria
- Only if the virus lacks a lipid envelope
???
No. Antibiotics target specific bacterial structures and enzymes—such as bacterial cell-wall synthesis or bacterial ribosomes. Because a virus has no cell wall, no independent metabolism, and uses *your own human ribosomes* inside your cells to translate its proteins, an antibiotic has no viral target to attack. Taking antibiotics for a viral infection does not speed recovery and selects for antibiotic-resistant bacteria (Chapter 14).
:::

Because viruses are acellular and cannot carry out metabolic processes or reproduction without hijacking a host cell, **Cambridge IGCSE and standard biological classification place viruses outside the cellular domains and kingdoms of life**—operating on the border between supramolecular chemistry and living cells. Cellular organisms are classified by shared evolutionary ancestry and named using Carl Linnaeus's two-part (**binomial**) system, such as *Panthera pardus* for the Indian leopard.

:::warning Antibiotics Never Touch Viruses
Never take leftover antibiotics for a viral cold, flu, or dengue fever. Antibiotics target bacterial enzymes and 70S ribosomes; viruses lack both. Misusing antibiotics leaves the virus untouched while killing beneficial gut bacteria and selecting for drug-resistant bacterial strains.
:::

:::school
In Cambridge IGCSE Biology (Topic 1.2 & 1.3), apply two exact exam rules:
1. **Viruses belong to none of the Five Kingdoms** (*Prokaryote, Protoctist, Fungus, Plant, Animal*). If an exam diagram shows a particle with only a **protein coat (capsid)** and **genetic material (DNA or RNA)**, identify it as a **virus** and state that it is **non-living** because it lacks cellular structure and cannot respire or replicate outside a host cell.
2. In a **binomial** scientific name, the first word is the **Genus** (always **capitalised**, e.g. *Panthera*) and the second word is the **specific epithet** (always **lowercase**, e.g. *pardus*), printed in *italics* (or underlined when handwritten).
:::

## Case file: Looking at the evidence

:::case The Viking Mars soil puzzle (1976)
NASA's Viking landers tested Martian soil in 1976. In the Labelled Release experiment, adding aqueous nutrients tagged with radioactive carbon (¹⁴C) produced a rapid release of radioactive gas. Heating a duplicate soil sample strongly reduced that gas release. However, injecting a second dose of nutrients into the same sample later did not trigger a renewed burst of gas.

Those clues posed a sharp detective dilemma: living Martian soil microbes or reactive non-living soil chemistry? Heating kills microbes, yet heating also decomposes reactive inorganic oxidants. A failure to respond to a second feeding challenges a simple growing-microbe model, though its interpretation depends on the organism and conditions assumed.

Viking's organic-chemistry instruments added a further twist. Small chlorinated carbon compounds detected during heated analysis were initially dismissed as earthly cleaning-solvent contamination. Decades later, after NASA's Phoenix lander discovered reactive **perchlorate** salts in Martian soil in 2008, laboratory tests showed that heating organic matter with perchlorates destroys most organics while forming those exact chlorinated compounds. Neither result proves life, and Viking provided no clear evidence of living microorganisms on Mars.

Which observations in the Viking tests could fit *both* a biological and a non-biological explanation, and what independent lines of evidence would be required to establish cellular life?
???
Gas release upon adding nutrients and loss of activity after strong heating can occur in both biological metabolism and non-biological chemical oxidation (such as reactive soil superoxides or perchlorate-related chemistry). To establish cellular life rather than abiotic soil chemistry, investigators look for converging independent evidence: reproducible metabolic cycling across multiple feedings, intact cellular compartments, homochiral structural polymers (such as proteins or nucleic acids), and energy-coupling machinery rather than isolated chemical reactivity.
:::
