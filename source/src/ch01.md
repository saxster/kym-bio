In Chapter 0 we saw that every living creature on Earth today—from a bacterium in a drop of puddle water to a banyan tree—comes from an existing living parent. That raises an immediate, sharper question. What *is* "being alive"?

If you ask ten people on the street how to tell whether something is alive, they will give you a sensible-sounding list: living things move, breathe, eat, grow, and respond when you poke them. Yet nature loves to embarrass a hasty list. A candle flame moves, grows, consumes wax fuel, takes in oxygen, gives out carbon dioxide and heat, and can even "reproduce" by lighting a second candle—yet a flame is not alive. Meanwhile, a dry green *moong* bean sitting in a glass jar in your kitchen is as hard, cold and still as a pebble—yet it is alive. Why? Cracking that paradox takes us straight to the first principles of all biology: water, energy, molecular machines, and a digital code.

## 1.1 Forty-eight hours in June

:::scene Sanjay Gandhi National Park, Mumbai, early June

Walk up the basalt trail towards Kanheri Caves in the last week of May, before the monsoon arrives. The hillsides look dead. The grass is brittle straw; the earth is baked into cracked brown brick; the rock pools are bone-dry dust. If an alien landed on those hills in late May, it might conclude that almost nothing on the ground was alive.

Then, around the second week of June, the black Arabian Sea clouds break over Mumbai. Within **forty-eight hours** of that first heavy downpour, the brown rock turns emerald green. Mosses unfurl on stone walls, grass shoots pierce the mud, fern fronds uncurl, and temporary rainwater pools teem with swimming ostracods and tadpoles that hatched from dry mud cysts.

Where was all that life hiding on 31 May, when the hillside looked like baked brick?

It was waiting inside **dormant seeds, spores and cysts**—biological time capsules that had shut their water content down from the normal $75\text{–}85\%$ of a working cell to just $8\text{–}10\%$ by mass. You have thousands of these time capsules sitting in your kitchen right now: dry green *moong* beans (*Vigna radiata*), chickpeas (*chana*), and mustard seeds (*rai*).

Pick up a dry *moong* bean. At $10\%$ water content, the cytoplasm inside its cells is not a liquid at all—it has solidified into a dense, sugar-rich biological **glass** (biologists call this *vitrification*). Inside that glass, folded protein machines (enzymes) and genetic instructions sit locked in place, unable to drift or collide. Because molecules cannot move through solid glass to react, the dry seed uses almost zero oxygen (less than $0.05$ microlitres of $\text{O}_2$ per gram per hour)—so little that no ordinary school instrument can detect it.

:::predict
You weigh out $50\text{ g}$ of dry *moong* beans and submerge them in a bowl of tap water at Mumbai room temperature ($28^\circ\text{C}$) for eight hours, then drain the surface water and weigh the beans again. What happens to the mass of the beans and to the air inside a closed jar holding those soaked beans overnight?
- The beans stay near 50 g because their seed coats are waterproof; the air in the jar does not change
- The beans swell to more than 100 g as water rushes in, and the oxygen in the jar drops sharply while carbon dioxide and warmth rise—even in pitch darkness
- The beans absorb water, but they only use oxygen if you put the jar in sunlight so they can photosynthesise
???
The second choice! In eight hours, $50\text{ g}$ of dry *moong* beans absorb about $55\text{ g}$ of water through a tiny pore in the seed coat (the micropyle), swelling to roughly $105\text{ g}$—more than double their dry mass. The moment water melts the glassy cytoplasm back into a liquid soup, the waiting enzymes begin colliding with stored starch and sugar. Oxygen uptake leaps by more than **2,000 times**, carbon dioxide pours out, and a sensitive thermometer inside an insulated flask of sprouting beans rises by $1\text{–}2^\circ\text{C}$—all in complete darkness, before a single green leaf exists!
:::

## 1.2 The flame and the bean

Now that you have watched a dry bean wake up, let us put it side by side with our rival suspect: a burning candle flame.

Look at why the candle flame fools so many people at first glance. A candle is made of solid hydrocarbon wax—chains of carbon and hydrogen atoms storing chemical potential energy, very much like the starch and fat stored inside a seed. When you touch a match to the wick:

- The flame **consumes fuel** (melted wax drawn up the wick).
- It **takes in oxygen** ($\text{O}_2$) from the room air and **releases carbon dioxide** ($\text{CO}_2$) and water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)—the exact same gases a sprouting bean or a running human exchanges!
- It **moves and responds** to a draught of air, bending away from a breeze.
- It **grows** larger if you feed it more wick, and can **start new flames** if touched to another candle.

So why is the candle flame *not* alive, while the bean is?

To see the real difference, we have to zoom down to the nanometre scale—where chemistry and physics meet biology.

In the candle flame, wax molecules and oxygen molecules collide at $1,000^\circ\text{C}$ in a wild, uncontrolled smash called **combustion**. The covalent bonds in the wax break and re-form into $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ gases, releasing all their stored chemical energy in one violent burst as **random thermal motion** (heat and light). Every second the flame burns, it destroys structure and scatters atoms randomly into the room. Nothing in the flame uses that released energy to build a wall, repair a damaged part, or copy a blueprint. The moment the wax runs out, the flame vanishes.

Now zoom inside a single cell of your soaked *moong* bean at $28^\circ\text{C}$. The bean is also reacting sugar with oxygen to make $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$! If you burned a spoonful of glucose sugar in a flame and let a bean cell oxidise the exact same spoonful of glucose, the total energy released from start to finish would be identical (recall from Book 2, [*The Moving World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-physics/), Chapter 7, that energy is strictly conserved).

The difference is **how** the energy is released and **what it is coupled to**:

:::fig c1_seed_vs_flame() | The Flame vs. The Living Seed across three scales. Top (Macroscopic): both consume organic fuel and O₂ and release CO₂ and H₂O. Middle (Submicroscopic mechanism): in the flame, uncontrolled one-step combustion releases 100% of the chemical energy as random thermal jiggling (chaos). In the seed cell, folded enzymes unlock glucose in dozens of cool, controlled steps at 28 °C, capturing energy into rechargeable ATP batteries to build ordered cell walls, membranes and proteins. Bottom (Information): the flame has no blueprint; the seed executes and copies a 4-letter DNA code.
:::live seed_flame3d

In the bean cell, glucose is never allowed to burst into flame. Instead, teams of folded protein catalysts—**enzymes**—pass the glucose molecule down an assembly line of more than twenty small, gentle steps at room temperature. At key steps along that staircase, instead of letting the energy escape as useless heat, the cell catches it to recharge a tiny molecular battery—and then spends that battery to **link small molecules together into highly ordered 3D structures**: new cell membranes, cellulose walls, protein motors, and exact copies of its DNA instruction book.

:::predict
Suppose you seal a burning candle stub inside Glass Chamber 1, and a handful of wet, sprouting *moong* beans inside Glass Chamber 2 (kept in the dark so no photosynthesis happens). Both chambers start with the same amount of oxygen. After four hours, both have used up the same amount of oxygen and produced the same amount of $\text{CO}_2$ gas. What has happened to the solid matter inside each chamber?
- Both the candle and the beans have only lost solid structure, turning fuel into gas and ash
- The candle has only lost organized wax into scattered gas, whereas the beans have used part of their stored fuel to assemble *new* organized cells, roots, cell walls and DNA copies
- The beans have gained total mass out of nowhere because they are alive
???
The second choice! In Chamber 1, wax was purely broken down into scattered $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ gas. In Chamber 2, the bean burned *part* of its starch store into $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (so the total dry mass of the bean actually *decreases* slightly in the dark!), **but** it used the captured energy from that fuel to transform the rest of its stored molecules into millions of brand-new, intricately organized root and shoot cells.
:::

## 1.3 Rebellion against chaos, and SubbaRow’s ₹10 note

:::scene Boston & Bhimavaram, 1929

In Book 2 (*The Moving World*, Chapter 8), you met one of the deepest laws of physics: left to themselves, things get messier, never tidier. A hot cup of chai cools until its heat is spread evenly through the room; a drop of ink in water spreads until the water is pale grey; a sandcastle left on Juhu Beach crumbles into a flat heap. Physicists call this relentless drift towards random disorder **entropy**.

In 1943, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger stood before an audience in Dublin and asked how a living cell avoids crumbling into equilibrium with its surroundings. His answer was that a living thing is an **open system** that stays alive by continuously pulling in high-grade order (sunlight or food molecules) from its environment, using it to pay for internal repairs, and exporting the resulting heat and waste ($\text{CO}_2$, water, urea) back outside. The moment a cell stops doing that work—the moment its internal chemistry reaches quiet equilibrium with the room—it is dead.

All the hundreds of chemical reactions running inside a cell to keep this rebellion going have a single name: ==metabolism== (from the Greek *metabolē*, "change"). Metabolism has two halves that work like a demolition crew and a construction crew:
- **Breaking down fuel** (like oxidising glucose in cellular respiration) to release stored chemical energy; and
- **Building up parts** (like stitching amino acids into proteins, or glucose units into cellulose walls), which *costs* energy.

:::note Metabolism
All the enzyme-controlled chemical reactions taking place inside a living cell or organism—both those that break down fuel molecules to release energy and those that build up complex molecules for growth and repair.
:::

For decades, biologists were baffled by a simple mechanical question: **how does the demolition crew pay the construction crew?** If breaking down a sugar molecule in one corner of the cell just warmed up the water, that random heat could never tell a ribosome in another corner of the cell to link two amino acids together. There had to be a physical go-between—a single, portable chemical "coin" charged up by fuel breakdown and spent wherever the cell needs to do work.

In 1929, a thirty-four-year-old Indian physician and biochemist named **Yellapragada SubbaRow**—who had grown up in Bhimavaram in Andhra Pradesh, studied medicine in Madras, and arrived in Boston with almost no money—cracked the mystery together with Cyrus Fiske at Harvard Medical School (while Karl Lohmann independently found the same molecule in Germany). Working with extracts of muscle tissue, Fiske and SubbaRow isolated the molecule that powers almost every movement, nerve impulse and chemical construction job on Earth: ==ATP== (**adenosine triphosphate**).

:::story p-subbarow

:::note ATP (adenosine triphosphate)
The universal rechargeable energy carrier ("chemical currency") inside all living cells, discovered in 1929 by Yellapragada SubbaRow and Cyrus Fiske (and independently by Karl Lohmann). It carries three negatively charged phosphate groups; transferring the end phosphate powers cellular work.
:::

What does an ATP molecule actually look like, and why does it work like a spring-loaded battery?

Look at Figure 1.3. An ATP molecule has three parts: an adenine ring (the exact same 'A' letter used in DNA!), a five-carbon ribose sugar, and a tail of **three phosphate groups** ($\text{P–P–P}$) chained together in a row.

:::fig c1_atp_cycle() | Yellapragada SubbaRow's molecular battery: the ATP / ADP cycle. Left: ATP carries a tail of three negatively charged phosphate groups (P–P–P). Because like charges repel, those three negative charges push against one another like a compressed spring. Right: when a cellular machine (a protein builder, ion pump or muscle fibre) reacts ATP with water to snip off the third phosphate (leaving ADP + Pᵢ), the system drops to a lower potential energy and drives the machine's work. Cellular respiration then uses energy from food to clip the third phosphate back onto ADP.

Here is the physics secret inside that three-phosphate tail. At the normal pH of a cell, each of those three phosphate groups carries **negative electric charges** (`− − −`). Remember from chemistry and physics what like electric charges do when you force them to sit right next to each other: **they repel!** Forcing three negatively charged phosphate groups onto one short tail is like squeezing a stiff metal spring and latching it shut.

When a molecular machine in your cell—say, a protein pump in a nerve membrane or a ribosome building a protein—needs a push, it takes one ATP molecule, reacts the end phosphate with water, and transfers that third phosphate group onto the machine. The third phosphate pops off, leaving **ADP** (*adenosine diphosphate*, with two phosphates) plus one free phosphate ion ($\text{P}_i$). With the crowding of negative charges relieved and new, stronger bonds formed with the surrounding water molecules, the products sit at a much lower potential energy—and that energy drop drives the molecular machine through its stroke!

Think of glucose as a ₹2,000 bank note—too large to put into a vending machine—and ATP as a pocketful of ₹10 coins that every single machine in every cell on Earth is built to accept.

:::work
How fast do your cells spend and recharge their ATP coins?
1. Your entire human body holds only about **$50\text{ grams}$** of ATP at any one instant—roughly the mass of a single small hen's egg.
2. Yet an active person turns over between **$35\text{ kg}$ and $50\text{ kg}$ of ATP every single day**—nearly your own entire body weight!
3. Divide the daily usage by the standing pool:
   $$\frac{40,000\text{ g used per day}}{50\text{ g in the body}} = 800\text{ cycles per day}$$
Every single ATP molecule in your cells is discharged to $\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$ and recharged back to $\text{ATP}$ by cellular respiration about **800 times a day**—roughly once every two minutes!
:::

:::five ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)
? What is it?
A small nucleotide molecule—made of adenine, ribose sugar, and a tail of three negatively charged phosphate groups—that acts as the universal, rechargeable energy coin inside every living cell.
? Why is it called this?
*Adenosine* (adenine + ribose sugar) + *tri-* (three) + *phosphate* (phosphorus-oxygen groups, $\text{PO}_4^{3-}$). When it loses its third phosphate, it becomes *ADP* (adenosine *di*phosphate, two phosphates).
? What problem did it solve?
Burning food directly as heat would cook the cell and cannot drive specific molecular machines. Cells needed a single standard energy packet small enough to power individual enzymes, pumps and motors without wasting all the energy as heat.
? Why does it work?
Its three closely packed, negatively charged phosphate groups repel one another; reacting the terminal phosphate with water (or transferring it to a protein) relieves that repulsion and forms lower-energy, better-hydrated products ($\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$), coupling fuel breakdown to mechanical or chemical work.
? How does it help?
Every time your heart beats, a nerve fires in your eye, a firefly glows, or a *moong* root builds a new cell wall, ATP is the exact coin paying for the job.
?? How do we know?
In 1929, Cyrus Fiske and Yellapragada SubbaRow (and independently Karl Lohmann) extracted ATP from muscle and showed that muscle contraction and cellular synthesis stop immediately when ATP regeneration is blocked, and resume when ATP is supplied.
?? What it isn't
It is not a long-term fuel tank! Cells never store days' worth of ATP (it is too heavy and chemically reactive); they store long-term energy in compact glucose, starch and fat, and recharge a tiny 50 g pool of ATP second by second.
?? Where does it stop working?
Outside a cell or without liquid water and magnesium ions ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$), ATP slowly breaks down on its own; and be careful with the old textbook phrase "high-energy bond"—breaking any chemical bond *takes* energy, and the energy release comes from the whole hydrolysis reaction dropping to more stable products in water.
:::

:::careful
> "ATP stores energy inside a special 'high-energy bond', and simply snapping that bond releases a burst of energy like snapping a glow-stick."

Remember from Book 1 (*The Invisible World*, Chapter 7): breaking a chemical bond *always* requires putting energy in! What releases energy in the $\text{ATP} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$ reaction is that the three crowded negative charges (`− − −`) push apart and the separated products form much stronger, lower-energy bonds with the surrounding water molecules than the original bond took to break. The *net* reaction releases energy because the final state is at lower potential energy than the starting state.
:::

## 1.4 Seven jobs that keep the rebellion going

Once you understand what a living organism actually is—**a cell-based machine that uses energy to maintain internal order and copy its genetic instructions**—you no longer need to memorise a school textbook's list of "seven characteristics of living things" as arbitrary rules. Every single one of those seven jobs is an engineering requirement for keeping a self-copying machine running!

In Cambridge IGCSE and British schools, the seven processes are remembered by the initials **MRS GREN**. Let us see *why* each of the seven is unavoidable from first principles:

:::fig c1_mrs_gren_engine() | The seven MRS GREN processes as a single connected engineering diagram of a living organism. Nutrition brings in matter and fuel; Respiration unlocks that fuel into ATP; Excretion removes toxic chemical wastes made by metabolism (CO₂, urea); Sensitivity and Movement let the organism find resources and dodge danger; Growth builds new cellular structure; and Reproduction passes the DNA blueprint across generations.

1. **Nutrition** (*getting raw materials and fuel*): No machine can build new parts out of nothing or recharge ATP without an external energy source. **Plants** take in simple inorganic molecules ($\text{CO}_2$ gas from air, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and mineral ions from soil) and use sunlight to build their own organic food. **Animals and fungi** take in ready-made organic compounds by eating or absorbing other organisms.
2. **Respiration** (*unlocking fuel to recharge ATP*): Having starch or sugar in your cell is useless until you transfer its stored chemical energy into ATP coins. ==Respiration== is the set of chemical reactions inside cells that break down nutrient molecules (such as glucose) and release energy for metabolism. *Every* living cell—plant, animal, fungus and bacterium—respires continuously to stay alive.
3. **Excretion** (*throwing out toxic chemical exhaust*): Every chemical factory produces waste side-products that would poison the cell if allowed to build up. Aerobic respiration produces $\text{CO}_2$ (which turns blood acidic if not removed); breaking down excess amino acids in your liver produces toxic nitrogen waste (**urea**). ==Excretion== is the removal from organisms of the waste products of metabolism, toxic materials, and substances in excess of requirements.
4. **Sensitivity** (*detecting clues in the outside world*): A machine that cannot tell light from dark, hot from cold, or food from poison will quickly be destroyed. ==Sensitivity== is the ability to detect or sense stimuli in the internal or external environment and to make appropriate responses.
5. **Movement** (*changing position to act on those clues*): ==Movement== is an action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place. Animals walk, swim or fly using muscles; plants move more slowly, growing their shoots towards light and opening or closing their leaf pores (stomata).
6. **Growth** (*building more ordered structure*): ==Growth== is a permanent increase in size and dry mass by an increase in cell number or cell size (or both). Notice the words *permanent* and *dry mass*: blowing up a balloon or soaking a sponge in water makes it bigger, but once you dry the sponge out, no new cellular matter was built!
7. **Reproduction** (*backing up the blueprint before the machine wears out*): Every individual physical body eventually suffers too much damage or gets eaten. ==Reproduction== is the processes that make more of the same kind of organism, copying the DNA instruction manual into a fresh generation.

:::note Respiration (biological definition)
The chemical reactions in all living cells that break down nutrient molecules (such as glucose) and release energy for metabolism (recharging ATP). Never confuse it with *breathing*, which is just the muscular movement of air in and out of lungs.
:::

:::note Excretion vs. egestion
Excretion is the removal of waste products made by chemical reactions *inside* cells (such as $\text{CO}_2$ from respiration and urea from amino acid breakdown), plus excess water and salts. **Egestion** is passing out undigested food (such as plant cellulose fibre in faeces) that travelled through the gut tube without ever entering a body cell.
:::

:::predict
After you eat a bowl of sweetcorn or leafy greens, tough cellulose plant fibres pass all the way through your digestive tract without being broken down and leave your body in faeces. In biology, does passing faeces count as **excretion**?
- Yes, because faeces are waste leaving the body
- No—it is **egestion**, because those cellulose fibres never crossed into your body's cells or took part in any metabolic chemical reaction inside a cell
- Yes, for animals, though not for plants
???
The second choice! Think of your digestive canal as a hollow tunnel running right through the middle of your body. A cellulose fibre that enters your mouth and leaves in faeces never crossed the gut wall into your bloodstream or entered a single cell's metabolism. Passing undigested leftovers out of the gut tunnel is **egestion**. True **excretion** is getting rid of molecules that were produced by chemical reactions *inside* your cells (like $\text{CO}_2$ exhaled from your lungs, or urea filtered out of your blood into urine by your kidneys)—plus water and salts in excess.
:::

:::careful
> "Animals breathe and respire, while plants only photosynthesise."

In studies of twelve- to fourteen-year-olds, nearly two-thirds fall into this trap because everyday English uses the word "respiration" to mean breathing with lungs! Plants do not have lungs, so they do not *breathe*—but every living cell in a plant (especially root cells buried in pitch-dark soil!) must continuously recharge its ATP batteries 24 hours a day, day and night, by carrying out **cellular respiration**. In bright sunlight, a green leaf simply photosynthesises *faster* than it respires, so overall it takes in net $\text{CO}_2$ and gives out net $\text{O}_2$. At night, when photosynthesis stops, you can easily measure the plant taking in $\text{O}_2$ and releasing $\text{CO}_2$, just like you.
:::

:::school
Cambridge IGCSE 0610 Paper 1 and Paper 4 love two classic traps on Topic 1.1:
1. If an exam question asks *"Name the characteristic of living organisms shown when carbon dioxide leaves the lungs"* or *"when sweat/urine leaves the body"*, the answer is **excretion** (never egestion).
2. If asked to define **growth**, always include **"permanent increase in dry mass"** (weighing an organism after all water has been removed, since temporary water intake is not growth).
:::

:::check
A dry sponge dropped into a bucket of water swells to twice its size. A crystal of alum hung in a saturated alum solution slowly grows larger over a week. Using the biological definitions of growth and metabolism, explain why neither the sponge nor the alum crystal is carrying out biological growth.
???
The sponge only absorbs liquid water into existing spaces; its **dry mass** does not increase at all, and the expansion is reversible when dried. The alum crystal increases in dry mass as ions from the solution stack onto its outer surface (**accretion**), but it has no cells, no internal metabolism, no ATP, and no genetic code directing the synthesis of new molecules from within. Biological growth builds new cells and protoplasm from the inside using metabolic energy.
:::

## 1.5 The outlaw at the border

We have built a clean, sharp boundary around the living world: a living thing is made of one or more **cells**, runs an internal **metabolism** powered by **ATP** to fight entropy, and carries a **DNA** instruction book to **reproduce** and carry out the seven **MRS GREN** jobs.

And then a microscopic outlaw walks up to the border checkpoint and breaks our neat categories in half.

In 1892, the Russian botanist Dmitri Ivanovsky crushed up diseased tobacco leaves that were blotched with yellow-and-green spots (tobacco mosaic disease) and forced the sap through an ultra-fine porcelain filter whose pores were small enough to trap every known bacterium. To his astonishment, the crystal-clear liquid that dripped through the filter was still completely infectious! Six years later, the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck repeated the test, showed that the invisible agent multiplied *only* inside living, dividing plant cells, and named it a *contagium vivum fluidum*—a ==virus== (from the Latin word for "slimy liquid" or "poison").

:::note Virus
A microscopic infectious particle ($20\text{–}300\text{ nm}$ across), far smaller than a bacterium, consisting of a strand of genetic material (DNA or RNA) enclosed inside a protective protein coat (capsid). Viruses have no cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes or ATP metabolism of their own, and can replicate only by hijacking a living host cell.
:::

When the electron microscope was invented in the 1930s, scientists finally saw what a virus looks like. Compare a bacterium and a virus in Figure 1.5:

:::fig c1_virus_border() | The border of life: a living Bacterial Cell (left, ~2,000 nm long) vs. two Viruses—a T4 Bacteriophage and an Enveloped Coronavirus (right, ~100 nm across, drawn enlarged so their parts are visible). The bacterium is a complete self-powered factory with a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, ATP generators and a circular DNA chromosome. A virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no ATP; it is simply a protein box (capsid) carrying a strip of genetic code (RNA or DNA).

Look at what a virus **has**, and what it **lacks**:
- **What it has:** A short strip of genetic code (either **DNA** or **RNA**) locked inside a geometric **protein coat** (called a *capsid*, often shaped like a 20-sided icosahedron or a lunar lander), sometimes wrapped in a stolen patch of fatty membrane studded with spike proteins.
- **What it lacks:** It has **no cytoplasm**, **no ribosomes** (no protein printers), **no mitochondria or ATP-making enzymes**, and **no cell membrane** pumping ions.

Floating in the air or sitting on a doorknob, a virus particle (a *virion*) is as chemically inert as a grain of salt. It does not respire. It does not grow. It does not excrete. It uses zero ATP. Unlike a dormant *moong* bean, even if you drop a virus into warm water full of sugar, amino acids and oxygen, it does **nothing at all**—because it has no machinery of its own to read its code or burn fuel!

A virus is **biological software without hardware**—like a USB flash drive holding a computer program, lying on a desk without a computer.

Only when a virus bumps into the right living host cell—when its surface spike protein clicks into a matching receptor lock on the host cell's membrane—does the hijack begin. The virus injects its RNA or DNA code into the cell. The host cell's own ribosomes and ATP batteries cannot tell the viral code from the cell's own instructions: they dutifully read the viral recipe and print hundreds of new viral protein coats and copies of the viral genome, until the hijacked cell bursts or buds off a swarm of new viruses.

:::predict
A child comes down with a runny nose and fever caused by an influenza **virus**. Someone suggests taking an **antibiotic** (such as amoxicillin, which works by blocking the enzymes bacteria use to build their peptidoglycan cell walls, or by jamming bacterial ribosomes). Will the antibiotic cure the viral flu?
- Yes, because antibiotics kill all microscopic germs including viruses
- No, because a virus has no bacterial cell wall and no ribosomes of its own for the antibiotic to attack—and using antibiotics against viruses only breeds antibiotic-resistant bacteria in your body
- Yes, if you take a double dose for a week
???
No! Antibiotics target specific bacterial machinery—such as bacterial cell-wall synthesis or bacterial ribosomes. Because a virus has no cell wall, no metabolism, and uses *your own human ribosomes* inside your cells to make its proteins, an antibiotic has nothing to attack in a virus. Taking antibiotics for a viral cold or flu does zero harm to the virus while wiping out helpful bacteria in your gut and selecting for resistant superbugs (Chapter 14).
:::

Because viruses do not consist of cells and cannot carry out the MRS GREN processes on their own, **Cambridge IGCSE and most biologists classify viruses as non-living**—sitting right on the border between complex chemistry and cellular life. Everything that *is* cellular and alive on Earth is sorted into groups based on shared evolutionary ancestors and structural features—a system called **classification**, first organised with two-part Latin names (*binomial nomenclature*, such as *Homo sapiens* or *Panthera pardus* for the Mumbai leopard) by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in 1735, which we will map in full in Chapters 2 and 14.

:::school
In Cambridge IGCSE Biology (Topic 1.2 & 1.3), remember two exact exam rules:
1. **Viruses are not in any of the Five Kingdoms** (*Prokaryote, Protoctist, Fungus, Plant, Animal*). If an exam diagram shows a particle with only a **protein coat** and **genetic material (DNA/RNA)**, identify it as a **virus** and state that it is **non-living** because it cannot reproduce or carry out metabolic processes outside a host cell.

## Case file: Looking at the evidence

:::case The Viking Mars soil puzzle (1976)
In July 1976, NASA's *Viking 1* lander touched down on the rusty red plains of Mars, scooped up a pinch of dry Martian soil into a sealed test chamber, and dripped a drop of water containing radioactive carbon-14 nutrients ($^{14}\text{C}$-labelled simple organic compounds) onto the soil. Immediately, a burst of radioactive $^{14}\text{CO}_2$ gas was released from the wet soil! The scientists then took a second scoop of Martian soil and baked it at $160^\circ\text{C}$ for three hours (hot enough to denature any protein enzymes and kill any Earth microbes) before adding the nutrient drop: this time, almost no gas came off.

At first, the team thought they had discovered living microbes respiring in the Martian dirt! Then they ran two more tests:
1. When they injected a *second* drop of nutrients into the first soil sample a week later, no new burst of gas occurred (unlike Earth soil microbes, which would have multiplied and released *more* gas on the second feeding).
2. A mass spectrometer baking the Martian soil found **zero organic carbon molecules**—no cells, no membranes, no proteins, no DNA—only reactive chemical oxidants (such as perchlorates and superoxides formed by ultraviolet sunlight hitting the dry dust).

Use the ideas of this chapter (comparing the Flame, the Seed, and Enzymes) to explain:
(a) Why a purely chemical oxidant in the Martian soil could break down the nutrient drop into $\text{CO}_2$ gas on the first injection (like a chemical flame), and why baking the soil at $160^\circ\text{C}$ first could destroy a fragile chemical oxidant too; and
(b) Why the **second feeding test** (where multiplying Earth microbes would release *more* $\text{CO}_2$, while a one-shot chemical reactant gets used up on the first drop) was the decisive clue separating living metabolism from non-living chemistry.
???
(a) Just like a candle flame or hydrogen peroxide, reactive chemical oxidants in Martian dust can chemically oxidise organic nutrient molecules into $\text{CO}_2$ gas in a one-way reaction without any cells or ATP being present. Strong heating ($160^\circ\text{C}$) can thermally decompose unstable chemical superoxides in dust, mimicking the heat-denaturation of biological enzymes!
(b) A non-living chemical oxidant is **used up** when it reacts with the first drop of nutrients, so adding a second drop produces no further reaction. Living cells, by contrast, use the energy from the first meal (via ATP) to **grow and reproduce**, creating *more* cells and *more* enzymes—so a second meal given to living soil produces an even bigger burst of respiratory $\text{CO}_2$ than the first.
:::
