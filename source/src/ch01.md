In Chapter 0 we saw that every living creature on Earth today—from a bacterium in a drop of puddle water to a banyan tree—comes from an existing living parent. That raises an immediate, sharper question. What *is* "being alive"?

If you ask ten people on the street how to tell whether something is alive, they will give you a sensible-sounding list: living things move, breathe, eat, grow, and respond when you poke them. Yet nature loves to embarrass a hasty list. A candle flame moves, grows, consumes wax fuel, takes in oxygen, gives out carbon dioxide and heat, and can even "reproduce" by lighting a second candle—yet a flame is not alive. Meanwhile, a dry green *moong* bean sitting in a glass jar in your kitchen is as hard, cold and still as a pebble—yet it is alive. Why? Cracking that paradox takes us straight to the first principles of all biology: water, energy, molecular machines, and a digital code.

## 1.1 The first rains in June

:::scene Sanjay Gandhi National Park, Mumbai, early June

Walk up the basalt trail towards Kanheri Caves in the last week of May, before the monsoon arrives. The hillsides look dead. The grass is brittle straw; the earth is baked into cracked brown brick; the rock pools are bone-dry dust. If an alien landed on those hills in late May, it might conclude that almost nothing on the ground was alive.

After the first heavy rains, brown hillsides begin turning green. Shoots emerge and pools refill. Different organisms return in different ways: seeds germinate, some tiny aquatic animals hatch from resistant resting eggs, and frogs breed in the new water. Tadpoles develop from frog eggs, not from dry mud cysts. There is no single timetable or survival mechanism for the whole hillside.

Where was the next generation of grass hiding through the dry weeks? Much of it was inside seeds. Pick up a dry green *moong* bean (*Vigna radiata*), a chickpea or a mustard seed. A viable seed contains a living embryo, stored materials and machinery that can resume development when conditions permit.

As many desiccation-tolerant seeds dry, their cell contents become extremely viscous and can form a sugar-rich biological **glass**, a change called *vitrification*. Molecular movement is greatly restricted, and metabolism slows enormously. Not every motion or chemical change stops: dry seeds can still deteriorate. Water content, temperature and the seed's composition affect this state. A seed that looks unchanged is not guaranteed to remain viable forever.

Adding water softens this crowded interior and allows the reactions needed for germination to resume. Water uptake is called **imbibition**. Water can enter through permeable regions of the coat; a single pore is not a universal explanation for all seeds. Once hydrated, the embryo can use stored nutrients in respiration, supplying ATP for growth even in darkness.

## 1.2 The flame and the bean

Now that you have watched a dry bean wake up, let us put it side by side with our rival suspect: a burning candle flame.

Look at why the candle flame fools so many people at first glance. A candle is made of solid hydrocarbon wax—chains of carbon and hydrogen atoms storing chemical potential energy, very much like the starch and fat stored inside a seed. When you touch a match to the wick:

- The flame **consumes fuel** (melted wax drawn up the wick).
- It **takes in oxygen** ($\text{O}_2$) from the room air and **releases carbon dioxide** ($\text{CO}_2$) and water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)—the exact same gases a sprouting bean or a running human exchanges!
- It **moves and responds** to a draught of air, bending away from a breeze.
- It **grows** larger if you feed it more wick, and can **start new flames** if touched to another candle.

So why is the candle flame *not* alive, while the bean is?

To see the real difference, we have to zoom down to the nanometre scale—where chemistry and physics meet biology.

In the candle flame, vaporised wax reacts with oxygen through a network of reactions called **combustion**. Chemical changes transfer energy to the surroundings as heat and light. The flame has no cells, inherited instruction system or machinery that couples these reactions to cellular maintenance and reproduction. Many-step chemistry alone is not a test for life.

Now zoom inside a single cell of your soaked *moong* bean at $28^\circ\text{C}$. The bean is also reacting sugar with oxygen to make $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$! If you burned a spoonful of glucose sugar in a flame and let a bean cell oxidise the exact same spoonful of glucose, the total energy released from start to finish would be identical (recall from Book 2, [*The Moving World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-physics/), Chapter 7, that energy is strictly conserved).

The difference is **how** the energy is released and **what it is coupled to**:

:::fig c1_seed_vs_flame() | Both systems transform fuel. In the cell, enzyme-controlled pathways couple some energy transfers to ATP supply and cellular construction, while heat is also released. The flame lacks a cellular system of maintenance and inheritance. The staircase and 3D assembly are conceptual pictures, not literal molecular pathways.
:::live seed_flame3d

In the bean cell, **enzymes**, biological catalysts, help reactions proceed at ordinary cellular temperatures. The pathways connect nutrient breakdown to other reactions, including the formation of ATP. ATP-dependent reactions can in turn be linked to transport, repair and building cellular material. This physical connection between reactions is called **coupling**. Simply warming the cell would not provide the same organised chemical connections.

:::predict
A germinating seed in darkness develops a longer root while its total dry mass falls. Which account reconciles those observations?
- The growing root gains material transferred from reserves, while some reserve material leaves as respiration products
- Growth creates matter, so the dry-mass measurement must be wrong
- A longer root must mean the entire seed has gained dry mass
???
The first. Stored materials supply the developing root. Some fuel is also oxidised, and carbon dioxide leaves. A growing part can gain material while the whole seed loses dry mass. There is no claim here about equal gas quantities from wax and carbohydrate, or a fixed four-hour growth result.
:::

:::check
Could a non-living chemical reaction release heat and carbon dioxide? Which additional features distinguish the viable seed from the flame?
???
Yes. Gas and heat alone do not establish life. The seed contains a cellular system with inherited instructions and machinery capable of maintaining and developing it under suitable conditions. The flame lacks that system.
:::

## 1.3 Paying for repairs, and SubbaRow’s ₹10 coin

:::scene Boston & Bhimavaram, 1929

A cell can build an organised structure while releasing heat and changing its surroundings. That fits the second law of thermodynamics: the total entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. A cell is an **open system**, exchanging matter and energy with its surroundings. Looking only at its internal organisation leaves part of the account out.

In his 1943 Dublin lectures, Erwin Schrödinger asked how living things maintain organisation. The useful question for our bean is concrete: how do reactions using stored nutrients help build and maintain its cells? A temporarily inactive dry seed can retain the machinery to resume. Irreversible loss of that capacity is different from a pause in activity.

All the hundreds of chemical reactions running inside a cell in growth, maintenance and energy transfer have a single name: ==metabolism== (from the Greek *metabolē*, "change"). Metabolism has two halves that work like a demolition crew and a construction crew:
- **Breaking down fuel** (like oxidising glucose in cellular respiration) to release stored chemical energy; and
- **Building up parts** (like stitching amino acids into proteins, or glucose units into cellulose walls), which *costs* energy.

:::note Metabolism
All the enzyme-controlled chemical reactions taking place inside a living cell or organism—both those that break down fuel molecules to release energy and those that build up complex molecules for growth and repair.
:::

Breaking down fuel and building a cell must be physically connected. One important connection uses a small molecule: ==ATP==, **adenosine triphosphate**.

In 1929, Yellapragada SubbaRow and Cyrus Fiske at Harvard, and independently Karl Lohmann in Germany, identified ATP in muscle extracts. SubbaRow had grown up in Bhimavaram and studied medicine in Madras. Identifying the molecule was one discovery; establishing its widespread role in energy transfer took further experiments and later work by many researchers.

:::story p-subbarow

:::note ATP (adenosine triphosphate)
A principal rechargeable energy carrier in cells. Enzymes couple ATP reactions to other chemical changes and cellular work. ATP is not the only energy carrier.
:::

An ATP molecule contains adenine, a ribose sugar and three phosphate groups. After hydrolysis to **ADP**, adenosine diphosphate, two phosphates remain attached; the other product is inorganic phosphate, Pᵢ.

:::fig c1_atp_cycle() | A schematic of ATP and one common reaction cycle, ATP + water → ADP + Pᵢ. Re-forming ATP requires an energy-coupled reaction. Charges contribute to the chemistry, but the whole reaction and its conditions determine the free-energy change. Other ATP-dependent reactions can transfer phosphate to another molecule instead.

Follow one example: a motor protein binds ATP. Its reaction cycle includes ATP hydrolysis and changes in how the protein binds and changes shape. The linked cycle can produce movement. Hydrolysis in a separate beaker would not move that motor: the reactions and shape changes must be connected.

A different enzyme can transfer ATP's terminal phosphate directly to another molecule. That produces a phosphorylated molecule, rather than releasing free phosphate in the same step. There is no single phosphate-transfer recipe used by every cellular machine.

Why can these reactions help? Under suitable cellular conditions, the overall ATP hydrolysis reaction lowers **Gibbs free energy**, the quantity used to judge the capacity of a chemical change to drive coupled work. Charge repulsion, the stability of the products and their interactions with water all contribute. Breaking a bond takes energy; the complete reaction can still release usable energy because it forms new interactions as well.

Think of ATP as a widely accepted ₹10 coin: useful for many transactions, but not the only currency. Cells regenerate ATP from ADP and phosphate through energy-coupled reactions. Respiration supplies such energy; chloroplasts also make ATP using light-supported processes.

:::work
How does a small pool support much larger daily turnover? Use **illustrative adult-scale assumptions**, not measurements of your body: a 50 g ATP pool and 40,000 g turned over per day.

40,000 ÷ 50 = **800 pool-equivalents per day**.

1,440 minutes ÷ 800 = **1.8 minutes per pool-equivalent**.

These calculations describe average turnover relative to an assumed pool. They do not say every molecule follows the same timetable. Actual pools and turnover vary with tissues, body size and activity.
:::

:::five ATP
? What is it?

? Why is it called this?
Adenosine combines adenine and ribose; triphosphate means three phosphate groups. ADP has two attached phosphates.

A long-term fuel store, the only energy carrier, or a bond that gives out energy merely because it breaks.

:::

:::careful
> "Snapping ATP's end bond releases energy by itself."

Bond breaking requires energy. Consider the full reaction, including the new interactions formed and the surrounding conditions. Also identify the coupling: an ATP reaction occurring separately cannot automatically drive another reaction elsewhere.
:::

No. ATP's reaction must be coupled to the motor's binding and shape-changing cycle. Releasing heat nearby is not equivalent to supplying that organised reaction cycle.

## 1.4 Seven processes in a living system

Once you understand what a living organism actually is—**a cell-based machine that uses energy to maintain internal order and copy its genetic instructions**—you no longer need to memorise a school textbook's list of "seven characteristics of living things" as arbitrary rules. These processes describe living systems across their lives. They need not all be visible at once, and an individual can be alive without reproducing. A dry seed retains a capacity to resume development; it need not display rapid metabolism while dry.

In Cambridge IGCSE and British schools, the seven processes are remembered by the initials **MRS GREN**. Follow how these processes contribute to a living system:

:::fig c1_mrs_gren_engine() | The seven MRS GREN processes as a single connected engineering diagram of a living organism. Nutrition brings in matter and fuel; Respiration transfers chemical energy from nutrients into forms including ATP; Excretion removes toxic chemical wastes made by metabolism (CO₂, urea); Sensitivity and Movement let the organism find resources and dodge danger; Growth builds new cellular structure; and Reproduction passes the DNA blueprint across generations.

1. **Nutrition** (*getting raw materials and fuel*): No machine can build new parts out of nothing or recharge ATP without an external energy source. **Plants** take in simple inorganic molecules ($\text{CO}_2$ gas from air, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and mineral ions from soil) and use sunlight to build their own organic food. **Animals and fungi** take in ready-made organic compounds by eating or absorbing other organisms.
2. **Respiration** (*unlocking fuel to recharge ATP*): Stored nutrients can supply material for construction as well as chemical energy. Respiration transfers some of that energy into forms including ATP. ==Respiration== is the set of chemical reactions inside cells that break down nutrient molecules (such as glucose) and release energy for metabolism. Active plant, animal, fungal and bacterial cells use nutrient reactions to supply energy. Extremely dry, inactive seeds remind us that rates can be greatly reduced during dormancy.
3. **Excretion** (*throwing out toxic chemical exhaust*): Every chemical factory produces waste side-products that would poison the cell if allowed to build up. Aerobic respiration produces $\text{CO}_2$ (which turns blood acidic if not removed); breaking down excess amino acids in your liver produces toxic nitrogen waste (**urea**). ==Excretion== is the removal from organisms of the waste products of metabolism, toxic materials, and substances in excess of requirements.
4. **Sensitivity** (*detecting clues in the outside world*): A machine that cannot tell light from dark, hot from cold, or food from poison will quickly be destroyed. ==Sensitivity== is the ability to detect or sense stimuli in the internal or external environment and to make appropriate responses.
5. **Movement** (*changing position to act on those clues*): ==Movement== is an action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place. Animals walk, swim or fly using muscles; plants move more slowly, growing their shoots towards light and opening or closing their leaf pores (stomata).
6. **Growth** (*building more ordered structure*): ==Growth== is a permanent increase in size and dry mass by an increase in cell number or cell size (or both). Notice the words *permanent* and *dry mass*: blowing up a balloon or soaking a sponge in water makes it bigger, but once you dry the sponge out, no new cellular matter was built!
7. **Reproduction** (*backing up the blueprint before the machine wears out*): Every individual physical body eventually suffers too much damage or gets eaten. ==Reproduction== is the processes that make more of the same kind of organism, copying the DNA instruction manual into a fresh generation.

:::note Respiration (biological definition)
Cellular chemical reactions that break down nutrient molecules (such as glucose) and release energy for metabolism (recharging ATP). Never confuse it with *breathing*, which is just the muscular movement of air in and out of lungs.
:::

:::note Excretion vs. egestion
Excretion is the removal of waste products made by chemical reactions *inside* cells (such as $\text{CO}_2$ from respiration and urea from amino acid breakdown), plus excess water and salts. **Egestion** is passing out undigested food (such as plant cellulose fibre in faeces) that travelled through the gut tube without ever entering a body cell.
:::

:::predict
After you eat a bowl of sweetcorn or leafy greens, tough cellulose plant fibres pass all the way through your digestive tract without being broken down and leave your body in faeces. In biology, does passing faeces count as **excretion**?
- Yes, because faeces are waste leaving the body
- No—it is **egestion**, because those cellulose fibres never crossed into your body's cells or took part in any metabolic chemical reaction inside a cell
- Yes, for animals, though not for plants
???
The second choice. Think of your digestive canal as a hollow tunnel running right through the middle of your body. A cellulose fibre that enters your mouth and leaves in faeces never crossed the gut wall into your bloodstream or entered a single cell's metabolism. Passing undigested leftovers out of the gut tunnel is **egestion**. True **excretion** is getting rid of molecules that were produced by chemical reactions *inside* your cells (like $\text{CO}_2$ exhaled from your lungs, or urea filtered out of your blood into urine by your kidneys)—plus water and salts in excess.
:::

:::careful
> "Animals breathe and respire, while plants only photosynthesise."

Plants have no lungs, but their active cells carry out **cellular respiration** in both light and darkness. In a lit leaf, photosynthesis and respiration can occur together. Net gas exchange depends on their rates: bright light can produce net oxygen release; darkness removes the photosynthetic contribution. A dormant seed is a different state, with greatly restricted metabolic activity.
:::

:::school
For Cambridge IGCSE 0610 Topic 1.1:
1. If an exam question asks *"Name the characteristic of living organisms shown when carbon dioxide leaves the lungs"* or *"when sweat/urine leaves the body"*, the answer is **excretion** (never egestion).
2. If asked to define **growth**, always include **"permanent increase in dry mass"** (weighing an organism after all water has been removed, since temporary water intake is not growth).
:::

:::check
A dry sponge dropped into a bucket of water swells to twice its size. A crystal of alum hung in a saturated alum solution slowly grows larger over a week. Using the biological definitions of growth and metabolism, explain why neither the sponge nor the alum crystal is carrying out biological growth.
???
The sponge only absorbs liquid water into existing spaces; its **dry mass** does not increase at all, and the expansion is reversible when dried. The alum crystal increases in dry mass as ions from the solution stack onto its outer surface (**accretion**), but it has no cells, no internal metabolism, no ATP, and no genetic code directing the synthesis of new molecules from within. Biological growth builds new cells and protoplasm from the inside using metabolic energy.
:::

## 1.5 The outlaw at the border

Our working account of cellular life is this: living systems are made of one or more **cells**, use an internal **metabolism** with energy carriers such as **ATP** for maintenance and development, and inherit **DNA** instructions. The seven processes describe these systems across their lives.

And then a microscopic outlaw walks up to the border checkpoint and breaks our neat categories in half.

In 1892, the Russian botanist Dmitri Ivanovsky crushed up diseased tobacco leaves that were blotched with yellow-and-green spots (tobacco mosaic disease) and forced the sap through an ultra-fine porcelain filter whose pores were small enough to trap every known bacterium. To his astonishment, the crystal-clear liquid that dripped through the filter was still completely infectious! Six years later, the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck repeated the test, showed that the invisible agent multiplied in living plant tissue, and named it a *contagium vivum fluidum*—a ==virus== (from the Latin word for "slimy liquid" or "poison").

:::note Virus
An infectious biological particle containing a DNA or RNA genome and a protein coat. Many familiar viruses are tens to hundreds of nanometres across, but sizes vary. Some have a lipid envelope. Viruses lack ribosomes and an independent cellular metabolism; replication requires a suitable host cell.
:::

When the electron microscope was invented in the 1930s, scientists finally saw what a virus looks like. Compare a bacterium and a virus in Figure 1.5:

:::fig c1_virus_border() | The border of life: a living Bacterial Cell (left, ~2,000 nm long) vs. two virus examples, a T4 bacteriophage and an enveloped coronavirus (right, drawn at different enlargements so their parts are visible). The bacterium is a cell supplied by nutrients and its surroundings with a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, ATP generators and a circular DNA chromosome. The viruses lack ribosomes and independent ATP production. An enveloped virus also has a lipid covering; these schematic examples are not a universal size or shape rule.

Look at what a virus **has**, and what it **lacks**:
- **What it has:** A short strip of genetic code (either **DNA** or **RNA**) locked inside a geometric **protein coat** (called a *capsid*, often shaped like a 20-sided icosahedron or a lunar lander), sometimes wrapped in a stolen patch of fatty membrane studded with spike proteins.
- **What it lacks:** It has **no cytoplasm**, **no ribosomes** (no protein printers), **no independent ATP-producing metabolism**. A lipid envelope is not a complete cellular membrane system.

Outside a suitable host, a virus particle does not independently respire, grow or make proteins. Its molecules can still undergo chemical changes, so calling it chemically inert would go too far. Water and nutrients alone cannot supply the missing cellular machinery.

A virus depends on a host for replication, but it is more than software: its physical coat and, when present, envelope help deliver its genome. Different viruses enter in different ways; injection describes some bacteriophages, while other viruses use membrane fusion or uptake into the cell.

Inside a suitable cell, host ribosomes make viral **proteins** using RNA messages. Separate enzymes copy the viral **genome**; depending on the virus, these enzymes can be supplied by the host or encoded by the virus. New components assemble into particles, which may leave through cell rupture or budding. Ribosomes do not copy DNA or RNA genomes.

:::predict
A child comes down with a runny nose and fever caused by an influenza **virus**. Someone suggests taking an **antibiotic** (such as amoxicillin, which interferes with bacterial cell-wall construction; other antibiotics target bacterial ribosomes). Will the antibiotic cure the viral flu?
- Yes, because antibiotics kill all microscopic germs including viruses
- No, because a virus has no bacterial cell wall and no ribosomes of its own for the antibiotic to attack; unnecessary antibiotic use can select resistant bacteria
- Only if the virus has a fatty envelope
???
No. Antibiotics target specific bacterial machinery—such as bacterial cell-wall synthesis or bacterial ribosomes. Because a virus has no cell wall, no metabolism, and uses *your own human ribosomes* inside your cells to make its proteins, an antibiotic has nothing to attack in a virus. These antibiotics do not treat influenza. Unnecessary use can affect helpful bacteria and select resistant bacteria (Chapter 14).
:::

Because viruses do not consist of cells and cannot carry out the MRS GREN processes on their own, **Cambridge IGCSE and most biologists classify viruses as non-living**—sitting right on the border between complex chemistry and cellular life. Cellular organisms are classified using shared features and evolutionary relationships. Two-part scientific names, developed systematically by Carl Linnaeus, identify species: for example *Panthera pardus*, the leopard. Later chapters return to ancestry and classification.

:::school
In Cambridge IGCSE Biology (Topic 1.2 & 1.3), remember two exact exam rules:
1. **Viruses are not in any of the Five Kingdoms** (*Prokaryote, Protoctist, Fungus, Plant, Animal*). If an exam diagram shows a particle with only a **protein coat** and **genetic material (DNA/RNA)**, identify it as a **virus** and state that it is **non-living** because it cannot reproduce or carry out metabolic processes outside a host cell.
2. In a two-part scientific name (**binomial**), the first word is the **Genus** (always written with a **capital letter**, e.g. *Panthera*) and the second word is the **specific name** (always written with a **small letter**, e.g. *pardus*), printed in *italics* (or underlined when handwritten). The two words together identify the species.
:::

:::check
Compare three tiny objects: (1) a dry *moong* seed, (2) a rod-shaped bacterium (*E. coli*), and (3) a coronavirus particle. Which of the three contain ribosomes and can make their own ATP when given water and nutrients?
???
Only **(1) the *moong* seed** (whose cells contain ribosomes and mitochondria, with greatly restricted metabolism while dry; viable seeds can resume development when conditions permit) and **(2) the bacterium** (a single prokaryotic cell containing its own ribosomes and ATP-making enzymes). **(3) The coronavirus** has no ribosomes, no cytoplasm, and no ATP machinery even when placed in water and nutrients; it can only be copied by hijacking a living host cell.
:::

## Case file: Looking at the evidence

:::case The Viking Mars soil puzzle (1976)
NASA's Viking landers investigated Martian soil in 1976. In the labelled-release experiment, adding nutrients containing radioactive carbon produced labelled gas. Heating a comparison sample strongly reduced the response. A later nutrient addition did not produce a renewed burst like the first.

Those observations raised a difficult question: metabolism or non-living soil chemistry? Heating can damage organisms, but it can also change chemical reactants. A second feeding is informative, but its result depends on the organisms and conditions assumed. It cannot alone settle every possible biological explanation.

The organic-chemical analyses were also difficult to interpret. Small chlorinated organic compounds were initially treated as contamination. Later experiments suggested that perchlorates, discovered on Mars by Phoenix in 2008, could destroy organics during heated analysis and produce such compounds. That interpretation does not establish life either. Viking provided no clear evidence of living microorganisms.

Which observations could fit both explanations? What further independent evidence would make a biological interpretation stronger?
???
Gas release and sensitivity to heating can occur in either biological or non-biological systems. Failure to respond to another feeding challenges a simple growth model, but does not identify the cause by itself. Seek independent evidence: reproducible activity under varied conditions, chemical analysis that accounts for reactive soil and instrument effects, and signs of a coordinated biological system. Organic molecules alone can also have non-biological origins. The lesson is to compare explanations and combine evidence, rather than turn one suggestive response into a verdict.
:::
