The moong bean in Chapter 1 kept its machinery and its instructions through the dry months. Add water, and its cells can resume the work of building a root. Now look more closely at the workers. What is inside one cell, and why does a growing root make more cells instead of simply swelling one cell larger and larger?

An often-used estimate puts an adult human body at about **30 trillion human cells**. That is an estimate for a reference adult, not a count of you. The puzzle survives any change in the number: why divide a body into such small pieces? Start with something much easier to cut than a human body. An onion will do.

## 2.1 Inside the walled city

:::scene Mumbai, an onion on the kitchen board

Peel a white onion bulb. Beside it, put a green leaf. Both came from plants. If every plant cell follows the same school drawing, both should be packed with green bodies. Yet the thin skin inside the onion is almost transparent. Where did its green parts go?

A drawing of “the plant cell” is a useful collection of features. It can tempt you into treating every real cell as a copy of that drawing.

:::predict
A white onion bulb cell has no developed green bodies for capturing light. What else must be missing?
- Its mitochondria, because plants use light instead of respiration
- Its cell membrane, because the wall does that job
- Neither: it can have mitochondria and a membrane without chloroplasts
???
The third choice. An ordinary white bulb cell has mitochondria and a cell membrane. It can use stored nutrients to supply ATP without capturing light itself.
:::

:::evidence Two plant samples, different equipment | Repeatable microscopy
Compare a thin onion bulb epidermis with a thin green leaf under a school microscope. Cell outlines appear in both. In suitable living leaf material, green bodies can be seen inside the outlines; the white bulb epidermis has no developed chloroplasts. That observation challenges a universal “all plant cells are green inside” rule.

It does not tell you everything in either cell. Ordinary light microscopy cannot resolve individual ribosomes or the two molecular layers of a cell membrane. Finer structural observations require other methods, including electron microscopy. A structure missing from your view may be too small or too transparent to see. Our labelled cutaway combines knowledge from several methods; it is not a photograph of what one school microscope reveals.
:::

**Start at the boundary.** What keeps the inside separate, and what supports it?

The boundary that separates a cell's internal mixture from its surroundings is a double layer of oily molecules called lipids, with proteins embedded in it. Small molecules cross in different ways; some pass through the lipid layer, while others use protein pathways. This boundary controls what enters and leaves: the ==cell membrane==. In a plant, a supporting cellulose mesh lies outside it: the **cell wall**. A mesh that gives support and a boundary that controls passage do different jobs.

:::note Cell membrane
The thin boundary around a cell that controls movement of substances into and out of it. A plant's cell wall lies outside this membrane.
:::

Inside is a crowded mixture of water, dissolved substances, large molecules and working structures. In a cell with a nucleus, the region inside the outer membrane but outside the nucleus is the **cytoplasm**. In a bacterium, which has no nucleus, that name covers the interior within its membrane. Our drawing leaves gaps so you can see the parts; those gaps do not mean the real cell is an almost-empty bag.

**Follow the instructions.** Where is information kept, and how is it used?

Most ordinary animal and plant cells keep most of their DNA behind another membrane boundary. This compartment is the **nucleus**. Cells with this arrangement are called **eukaryotic**. Bacteria have DNA too, but no membrane-enclosed nucleus; they are **prokaryotic**. In a typical bacterial diagram, the main DNA forms a circle in the cytoplasm. Smaller extra DNA rings, when present, are **plasmids**. Bacterial walls are usually made with peptidoglycan, a tough mesh of sugar chains linked by short protein-like chains. Plant cellulose is a different material.

All three cell types have tiny structures that join amino acids into proteins using a molecular message copied from DNA: **ribosomes**. That message is made of a molecule called RNA; we will follow the copying in Chapter 11.

**Follow the energy supply.** Which structures help supply ATP, and which capture light?

Typical plant and animal cells also contain **mitochondria**, where important stages of aerobic respiration supply ATP from the chemical energy in nutrients. Green leaf cells additionally contain **chloroplasts**, which use light energy to build carbohydrates. Many mature plant cells have a large **vacuole** containing **cell sap**, a watery solution of substances such as sugars and salts; its water helps press the cell contents against the supporting wall.

:::five Nucleus and ribosomes
? What is it?
The nucleus holds most DNA in a typical plant or animal cell. Ribosomes use RNA messages to join amino acids into proteins.
? Why is it called this?
*Nucleus* comes from a Latin word for a kernel. *Ribosome* means a ribose-containing body: ribose is the sugar in RNA, which forms part of a ribosome.
? What problem did it solve?
The distinction connects stored information with construction: DNA instructions can be copied into messages, then used to assemble particular proteins.
? Why does it work?
RNA messages carry a sequence copied from DNA. At ribosomes, that sequence specifies the order of amino acids joined into a protein.
? How does it help?
You can distinguish storing instructions from using a message to build something. A bacterium has ribosomes and DNA despite lacking an enclosed nucleus.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy and molecular experiments locate DNA and ribosomes and establish how RNA messages guide protein assembly.
?? What it isn't
A cell nucleus is not the atomic nucleus from chemistry. A ribosome is a molecular assembly, not a tiny membrane-bounded room.
?? Where does it stop working?
Some specialised cells lose this equipment. A typical-cell inventory is not a list that every living cell must retain.
:::

:::five Mitochondria and chloroplasts
? What is it?
Mitochondria carry out important stages of aerobic respiration that supply ATP. Chloroplasts use light energy to build carbohydrates.
? Why is it called this?
*Mitochondrion* combines Greek words for thread and little granule. *Chloroplast* combines Greek roots meaning green and formed. These names describe appearance; they do not explain the chemistry.
? What problem did it solve?
The distinction separates building carbohydrates with light from using chemical energy in nutrients to supply ATP for cellular work.
? Why does it work?
Each contains membranes and protein machinery suited to particular reactions. The reactions transfer energy; neither structure creates it.
? How does it help?
It explains why green leaf cells have both structures, while ordinary white onion bulb cells can keep working without developed chloroplasts.
?? How do we know?
Structural microscopy identifies the compartments; chemical experiments connect their machinery with light capture and respiration.
?? What it isn't
“Power station” is a reminder of ATP supply, not an explanation by itself. Chloroplasts do not replace mitochondria in a green leaf cell.
?? Where does it stop working?
This is a typical-cell comparison. Bacteria perform their own metabolism without mitochondria or chloroplasts, and some specialised cells lose organelles.
:::

:::five Cell compartments
? What is it?
A cell has a boundary and internal machinery. Some cells also separate particular jobs into membrane-bounded spaces.
? Why is it called this?
A compartment is a separated space. Hooke used *cell*, meaning a small room, for the box-like spaces he saw in cork; those were remains of dead cells.
? What problem did it solve?
Separating spaces allows different chemical conditions and reactions within one cell, while the outer membrane separates the cell from its surroundings.
? Why does it work?
Membranes keep some substances apart and contain proteins that control passage and carry out reactions.
? How does it help?
You can explain a cell's equipment by the work it does, then compare real cells instead of copying one standard picture.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy reveals structures; other experiments establish their chemical contents and functions. One instrument does not reveal every part.
?? What it isn't
A cell is neither a flat drawing nor a little city with people deciding what to do. Molecules interact through physical and chemical processes.
?? Where does it stop working?
Typical diagrams have exceptions. Mature human red blood cells, which we meet below, have lost their nucleus and mitochondria.
:::

:::careful
> “Plant cells have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria.”

Ordinary green leaf cells have both. Chloroplasts build carbohydrates using light; mitochondria help supply ATP through respiration. Onion bulb and root cells can respire using nutrients without developed chloroplasts. Respiration continues in light and darkness.
:::

## 2.2 The stowaways that became power stations

:::scene Boston, 1967

You have just separated the nucleus from the rest of a cell in your mental picture. Now comes an awkward clue: some of the cell's DNA is elsewhere. Mitochondria have their own inherited DNA. Chloroplasts do too.

Lynn Margulis, publishing as Lynn Sagan, brought evidence for a remarkable explanation together in a 1967 paper. Perhaps these structures descended from bacteria that once lived inside other cells. The idea had earlier advocates; she made a powerful case for taking it seriously again.

An internal working structure such as a mitochondrion or chloroplast is called an **organelle**: a “little organ”. Before accepting the ancestry idea, ask what evidence could distinguish an internal bacterial descendant from a compartment assembled entirely by its host.

:::predict
Which finding would give the stronger reason to investigate bacterial ancestry?
- A mitochondrion looks roughly oval, like some bacteria
- Mitochondria have their own DNA and bacterial-related protein-making machinery, and gene comparisons connect them to bacteria
- Mitochondria are too small to see clearly with unaided eyes
???
The second choice. Many unrelated things are oval or tiny. Several independent inherited similarities provide a much stronger test of ancestry.
:::

:::evidence DNA outside the nucleus | Ris and Plaut, 1962
Hans Ris and Walter Plaut investigated the chloroplast of the single-celled green alga *Chlamydomonas*. They combined electron microscopy with chemical tests for DNA. Particular regions stained as DNA, and fine fibres in those regions were sensitive to a DNA-breaking enzyme, DNase.

The comparison mattered: a thread-like appearance alone could mislead, while the chemical response tested what the material was. DNA was present inside a chloroplast, outside the nucleus. That result did not by itself establish the whole history of the organelle. It supplied a testable clue, alongside evidence gathered by other researchers. Later comparisons of inherited sequences helped establish which organisms were related to which. Margulis's contribution was a synthesis of evidence and an evolutionary explanation, not one experiment that settled everything in 1967.
:::

Consider a cell containing another organism. If the internal organism survives and divides, its descendants can pass into later host cells. If its chemical activity benefits the host, and the host supplies useful materials, the association can persist across generations. Changes that make the partners more dependent on each other can accumulate. No partner has to foresee the outcome or make an agreement.

This is the kind of history supported for mitochondria and chloroplasts: ==endosymbiosis==. Their ancestors were bacteria. Across evolutionary time, many genes moved to the host's nucleus or were lost. Present-day organelles rely on proteins and other supplies from the surrounding cell; you cannot simply release one and expect it to resume independent bacterial life.

:::note Endosymbiosis
An association in which one organism lives inside another. The endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts explains their descent from internal bacterial partners.
:::

The clues fit together. Each organelle is surrounded by two membranes. Each retains DNA and ribosomes related to bacterial machinery. New mitochondria and chloroplasts arise by growth and division of existing ones. Most tellingly, comparisons of genes connect mitochondria to bacterial ancestry and chloroplasts to **cyanobacteria**, a group that carries out photosynthesis.

:::fig c2_stowaway_clues() | Independent clues support one ancestry explanation. The internal membrane folds of a mitochondrion and the membrane stacks of a chloroplast do different chemical jobs. These enlarged cutaways are reconstructions, not surviving photographs of ancient events.
:::live organelle_clues

Calling a mitochondrion a “power station” can help you remember its role after you understand the mechanism: reactions use chemical energy from nutrients to supply ATP for other work. It creates no energy. A chloroplast uses incoming light energy to build carbohydrates. An ordinary green plant cell carries descendants of both kinds of bacterial partner.

:::five Endosymbiosis
? What is it?
A lasting association with one organism inside another; in this case, bacterial partners became integrated organelles over evolutionary time.
? Why is it called this?
*Endo-* means within; *symbiosis* means living together.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why some compartments in our cells have their own inherited machinery and particularly close bacterial relatives.
? Why does it work?
Partners that persist and reproduce together can become dependent. Gene transfer and gene loss can make that dependence permanent.
? How does it help?
It connects present cell structure to evolutionary history and generates predictions about DNA, ribosomes and division.
?? How do we know?
Structural observations, chemical tests and comparisons of inherited sequences support the same explanation.
?? What it isn't
An intentional partnership, or a claim that every part of a cell began as a swallowed bacterium.
?? Where does it stop working?
It does not settle every detail of the first host or every step by which the association began.
:::

:::careful
> “Margulis proved that mitochondria are ordinary bacteria living in us today.”

Their ancestry is bacterial. Their present dependence on host-cell machinery makes them integrated parts of the cell. The evidence accumulated through many researchers; the 1967 paper was a major contribution to that investigation.
:::

:::

## 2.3 The geometry trap

:::scene Mumbai, cubes on the kitchen plate

Put a small potato cube beside a larger one. The larger cube has more outside surface. That seems helpful if its interior has to receive substances from the surrounding liquid. Perhaps a larger cell could feed itself more easily because its boundary is larger too.

There is a second quantity to count: how much interior that boundary serves. Keep shape and chemical demand per unit volume the same, and enlarge the cube. The outside and the inside do not grow at the same rate.

:::predict
A cube's side doubles from 1 cm to 2 cm. What happens to its surface and volume?
- Both double
- Surface becomes four times as large; volume becomes eight times as large
- Surface becomes eight times as large; volume becomes four times as large
???
The second choice. Each face has two dimensions to double; the interior has three. The bigger cube has more surface in total but less surface for each cubic centimetre of interior.
:::

:::evidence Count faces before trusting the picture | A repeatable geometric test
A cube has six square faces. A 1 cm face has area 1 cm², so the cube has 6 cm² of surface around 1 cm³ of volume. Double the side: each face becomes 4 cm², giving 24 cm² around 8 cm³. Tenfold side gives 600 cm² around 1000 cm³.

You can check the middle case by cutting a 2 cm cube into eight 1 cm cubes. The total volume stays 8 cm³. If you separate the pieces so liquid reaches every face, the exposed area becomes 8 × 6 = 48 cm². Cutting has exposed new surface. This arithmetic is exact for ideal cubes; it makes no claim about how far a particular dye travels into potato tissue.
:::

Divide surface area by volume to ask how much boundary there is for each unit of interior. This is the ==surface-area-to-volume ratio==, often written **SA/V**. A cube of side *a* has surface area 6a² and volume a³, so its ratio is 6/a. The ratio falls as the side grows.

:::note Surface-area-to-volume ratio
Surface area divided by volume. For the same shape, enlarging all lengths reduces the surface available per unit of interior.
:::

:::fig c2_geometry() | All three cubes use the same drawing scale. Tenfold side gives a hundredfold surface and a thousandfold volume. Centimetres make the geometry easy to inspect; these are model blocks, not actual cell dimensions.
:::live sav_cubes3d

Why should a cell care about that arithmetic? Its chemistry uses materials throughout its active interior. Substances entering through its outside must cross a limited boundary, then reach the places where reactions use them. If demand per unit volume stays similar, enlargement adds demand faster than exchange surface. The centre also lies farther from the outside. Small size helps with both constraints.

A thought experiment makes the difference visible. Choose a reached layer **2 mm** deep around every face of a cube. In a 1 cm cube, that leaves a 0.6 cm core: **78.4%** of the full volume lies in the reached layer. In a 10 cm cube, the same depth reaches only **11.5%**. Those percentages follow from the geometry. We have not measured a dye speed, and real diffusion produces gradual concentration changes rather than a perfectly sharp coloured boundary.

There is no universal rule that every cell must fit one tiny size. A long, thin cell can keep its interior close to a surface. Folded boundaries provide extra area. Internal transport can carry materials, and a large storage compartment can leave only a thin active layer. Geometry sets a constraint; structure changes how the constraint applies.

A large body still has a second supply problem. Its internal cells do not all touch outside air or food. Many small cells shorten routes within each cell, but transport systems must also bring supplies close to them and carry wastes away. In animals, circulation helps do that; in a plant, conducting tissues connect roots and leaves with other parts. Dividing a body into cells and supplying those cells are two connected jobs.

:::five Surface-area-to-volume ratio
? What is it?
A comparison of the area across which exchange can occur with the volume that may depend on that exchange.
? Why is it called this?
The name states the calculation: surface area divided by volume.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why making a cell larger can make exchange harder even while its total surface increases.
? Why does it work?
For similarly shaped objects, area grows with the square of length while volume grows with the cube.
? How does it help?
It connects small cells, thin extensions and folded exchange surfaces with the same geometry.
?? How do we know?
Count areas and volumes, then test exchange while controlling other factors such as material, temperature and time.
?? What it isn't
A rule that every large cell dies, or a measurement of a particular substance's diffusion rate.
?? Where does it stop working?
Ratio alone cannot predict exchange when permeability, chemical demand, shape or internal transport differs.
:::

:::careful
> “The large cube has more surface, so it must exchange substances more easily.”

It has more surface in total, but less per unit of volume. Compare both quantities. A dyed surface also does not show that every living cell inside the potato has received the same amount of a substance.
:::

## 2.4 Specialists in the city

:::scene Mumbai, a breath on a dusty afternoon

Air entering your breathing passages carries tiny particles. A sticky layer traps many of them. Under a microscope, the surface beneath that layer is busy: rows of tiny hairs beat and move the mucus towards your throat.

Now compare one of those cells with a red blood cell or a root hair cell. A single standard “cell” outline would hide the feature that explains each job. We have reached the next reason a body contains many cells: different arrangements of the shared machinery support different work.

:::predict
Two root cells contain similar amounts of active material and have equally permeable membranes. Which feature gives the stronger reason to predict faster absorption under the same surrounding conditions?
- A long, thin extension that increases exposed contact area and keeps routes short
- A compact sphere because reducing its surface always speeds absorption
- The same volume guarantees the same absorption rate, whatever the shape
???
The extension increases exposed contact area while keeping routes short. Equal volume does not guarantee equal absorption: shape changes the exchange surface and distances even when the amount of active material stays similar.
:::

:::evidence Follow the moving layer | Microscopy and comparison
In living airway tissue, microscopy shows hairs beating along the cell surface while the mucus above them moves. Compare the moving surface with the flexible discs seen in a blood sample and the extensions seen on young roots. Different structures recur in different places, associated with different activities.

A still image helps identify shape; watching living material supplies evidence of movement. Neither observation alone explains every molecular step. The model improves when we connect structure with a physical job: beating hairs exert forces on the surrounding layer; a thin disc shortens the distance across it; an extension adds contact surface. These are repeatable observations and comparisons, not a fictional experiment performed by one historical discoverer. Use prepared school material or documented microscopy; do not collect blood or culture airway samples at home.
:::

The beating hairs are **cilia**, and the cells carrying them are **ciliated epithelial cells**. Their coordinated, ATP-driven movements shift mucus in the trachea and bronchi. The mucus traps particles; the cilia move the layer.

A mature human **red blood cell** is a flexible disc with a dip on both broad faces. Its thin shape gives oxygen a short route across the cell, and haemoglobin inside binds oxygen for transport. It has lost its nucleus and mitochondria. It still obtains ATP through reactions that do not require mitochondria. “Every animal cell has a nucleus” was only a typical-cell shortcut.

A **root hair cell** extends part of its surface into the spaces around soil particles, increasing contact for absorption of water and mineral ions. A **palisade mesophyll cell** in a leaf contains many chloroplasts. Its position and column-like arrangement help expose photosynthetic machinery to light. It also has mitochondria. A **neurone** has long, narrow processes and branches that connect it with other cells; electrical impulses travel along its membrane. Length does not require the whole cell to become a thick sphere.

Reproductive cells specialise too. A **sperm cell** has a tail whose movement propels it through fluid. An **egg cell** has a much larger cytoplasm containing materials for early development. Both carry hereditary information. We will investigate how that information combines later in the book.

New cells are produced by division of existing cells. As a root grows, it makes more cells; those cells can then develop different structures. Cells with particular structures and functions are ==specialised cells==. They arise as cells develop different patterns of activity and structure; an individual cell does not decide which equipment to grow.

:::note Specialised cell
A cell whose structure suits a particular function, such as moving mucus, absorbing materials or transporting oxygen.
:::

:::fig c2_specialists() | Five different solutions to different physical jobs. The profiles have different enlargements. Below them, a plant example connects one specialised cell to the whole organism.

Cells working together in a shared function form a **tissue**. Different tissues working together form an **organ**; several organs can cooperate in an **organ system**, within the whole **organism**. Follow a palisade cell into palisade mesophyll tissue, then into the leaf, the shoot system and the bean plant. The leaf also contains other tissues, including protective and transport tissues. A tissue need not contain only identical cells.

:::five Specialised cells and organisation
? What is it?
Cells have structures suited to particular functions. Their coordinated work builds tissues, organs and organ systems within an organism.
? Why is it called this?
*Specialised* means suited to a particular kind of work. *Organ* comes from a word for an instrument or tool.
? What problem did it solve?
One cell shape cannot perform every job equally well. Different structures support exchange, transport, movement and communication.
? Why does it work?
Physical features change performance: extensions add area, thin regions shorten distances and beating hairs move a fluid layer.
? How does it help?
You can infer a possible job from structure and test it against observations, rather than learning disconnected cell pictures.
?? How do we know?
Microscopy reveals structures and activities; experiments test the relationship between a changed structure and its performance.
?? What it isn't
A claim that each cell chooses its role, or that an organ contains only one kind of tissue.
?? Where does it stop working?
An outline alone cannot identify every cell. Chemical composition and activity matter too.
:::

:::careful
> “A bigger animal is made by making all its cells bigger.”

Larger bodies generally contain more cells. Their different cell types also have different shapes and sizes. A long neurone is compatible with the geometry argument because it remains thin; a mature human red blood cell is compatible with cell theory despite having lost its nucleus.
:::

Return to the dough comparison in Investigation 2.3. Flattening shortened the route from the centre to the nearest surface without changing the amount of material. A red blood cell's thin regions give oxygen a short exchange distance. Its real performance also depends on its membrane and haemoglobin; the dough models only the geometry.

## The cell that seems to break the rule

:::case The pearl in the coastal mud
In 1997, researchers collected sediment from the continental shelf off Namibia. The bacterium described in 1999, *Thiomargarita namibiensis*, formed pale beads. Some cells reached **750 µm** across, large enough to see without a microscope.

Three clues sit in the file: it really is a bacterium; a huge nitrate-storing vacuole occupies much of its interior; most active cytoplasm forms a thin layer around that store. Does this overturn the geometry argument? Explain which distance and which volume matter.
???
It changes how the argument applies. Most of the large cell's volume is storage, so it is misleading to treat all of it as equally active cytoplasm. The thin active layer keeps much of the working machinery close to an exchange surface. Area, active volume, transport and chemical demand still matter. This coastal-sediment bacterium is a counterexample to “all bacteria are tiny”, not a suspension of geometry.
:::
