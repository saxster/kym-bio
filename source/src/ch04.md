Chapter 3 left us at a protein doorway. A water channel lets water through while excluding ions; a carrier picks up particular passengers. How can a molecule make such distinctions? It has no eyes, no list of names and no idea what the cell requires.

The answer begins on your lunch plate. Rice, dal and a little oil contain many of the same kinds of atoms, connected differently. Follow those connections into three dimensions and a new possibility appears: a molecule whose surface helps another molecule react. Our “million-speed locks” are a nickname for remarkable chemical helpers, not a promise that every enzyme multiplies every rate by a million. The next clue is how molecular connections create a useful surface.

## 4.1 Water's sticky geometry and carbon's four hands

:::scene Mumbai, rice, dal and a drop of oil

Stir sugar into water and it can disappear from sight. Add cooking oil and it gathers into droplets or a layer. Both are made of molecules. Why does water surround one so readily while separating from the other?

A tempting answer is that all tiny pieces should mix equally well. But smallness is only part of the story. The arrangement of electrical charge matters too.

:::predict
A water molecule has no overall electrical charge. Could it nevertheless attract another water molecule strongly in a particular orientation?
- No: neutral means there can be no electrical attraction
- Yes: its charge can be unevenly distributed even though the total is zero
- Only after an electron has moved completely from one water molecule to another
???
The second answer. Oxygen draws the shared electrons closer than hydrogen does. The oxygen end is partly negative and the hydrogen ends partly positive. The whole molecule remains neutral.
:::



### Why water surrounds sugar

Water is bent, and its shared electrons are unevenly distributed. This unevenness is ==polarity==. A partly positive hydrogen attached to oxygen can interact with a nearby oxygen on another molecule: a ==hydrogen bond==. These attractions keep forming and breaking as liquid water moves. They are different from the covalent O–H bonds holding each water molecule together.

:::note Polarity
An uneven distribution of electrical charge within a molecule or bond. A polar molecule can still have zero overall charge.
:::

:::note Hydrogen bond
An attraction involving a hydrogen covalently bonded to an electronegative atom, such as oxygen or nitrogen, and another suitable electronegative atom. In water, many such attractions join neighbouring molecules temporarily.
:::

Water can form useful interactions around ions and many polar molecules. Sugar molecules have several oxygen-containing groups, including O–H groups, that can form hydrogen bonds with surrounding water. Most of an oil molecule's surface consists of long carbon-and-hydrogen regions without those contacts. Mixing oil and water does not replace enough favourable interactions to make them mix uniformly. Oil is called **hydrophobic**, literally “water-fearing”; molecules feel no fear. Keep the chemical interactions, discard the emotion. Book 1, [*The Invisible World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-chem/), gives the electrical background.

:::fig c4_water_carbon() | Water's partial charges and carbon's bonding capacity. The methane drawing records connections; it is not a claim that methane is flat. Distances and sizes are illustrative.

:::five Water polarity and hydrogen bonding
? What is it?
Water has an uneven charge distribution. Its partly positive hydrogens can form hydrogen bonds with suitable atoms nearby.
? Why is it called this?
Polarity names different electrical ends, or poles. A hydrogen bond is named for the hydrogen involved in the attraction.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why neutral water molecules attract one another and interact differently with sugar and oil.
? Why does it work?
Shared electrons are closer to oxygen. Opposite partial charges attract when molecules approach in suitable arrangements.
? How does it help?
It connects a tiny molecule's structure with the watery environment in which proteins operate.
?? How do we know?
Molecular structure and measurements of water's properties support the model. Comparing substances tests more than saying “everything small mixes”.
?? What it isn't
A full positive or negative charge on the whole water molecule, or an O–H covalent bond being cut whenever water flows.
?? Where does it stop working?
Polarity alone does not calculate exactly how much of any substance dissolves; temperature and the full set of interactions matter.
:::

### Same units, different connections

Now inspect the other workhorse: carbon. A carbon atom commonly forms four covalent bonds, with shared electron pairs. A double bond counts as two of those four. Carbon can link to carbon, making chains, branches and rings. Its “four hands” mean bonding capacity. No atom reaches out deliberately, and no little hands grow from its surface.

One small sugar, **glucose**, has formula C₆H₁₂O₆. In water it is mostly ring-shaped. Link glucose-derived units repeatedly and you can make much larger molecules. A small unit used to build a chain is a ==monomer==; a molecule with many linked units is a ==polymer==. Joining changes chemical bonds; cells also require suitable reactions and energy coupling to build their molecules. This Lego set is chemistry, not bricks pushed together by a miniature builder.

:::note Monomer
A small molecular unit that can be joined with others to build a polymer.
:::

:::note Polymer
A large molecule containing many linked units derived from smaller molecules. The units may be alike or differ in their side groups or sequence.
:::

Plants store glucose in **starch**, which includes unbranched and branched chains. Animals store it in **glycogen**, with more frequent branching. **Cellulose** uses a different orientation of glucose links; its straight chains associate into strong fibres in plant walls. The different links also change which digestive enzymes can act. Cellulose is not merely starch with another name.

:::evidence Same units, different material | Structure and digestion
Purified starch and cellulose can both be broken down chemically to glucose. That shared building unit does not make their structures identical. Their links have different orientations, giving the chains different shapes and arrangements. Human digestive enzymes can break down starch; we do not make the cellulase needed to digest cellulose in the same way.

This is a useful double comparison. Chemical analysis identifies the smaller units, while structure and digestion distinguish how those units are joined. Simply finding “glucose building blocks” would miss the feature that decides whether a human digestive enzyme can act. The ingredient list alone does not specify the finished molecule, just as a list of letters does not specify a sentence.
:::

:::fig c4_building_units() | Compare architecture, not token counts. Real chains are much longer. The curved return symbols in the cellulose row mark alternating unit orientation; this is not an atom-by-atom formula. The triglyceride drawing represents only one kind of lipid.

:::five Monomers and polymers
? What is it?
Monomers are smaller building units; polymers contain many chemically linked units. Glucose-derived units build starch, glycogen and cellulose.
? Why is it called this?
Mono means one, poly means many, and the shared Greek word-part means part. The names count parts; they do not explain the links.
? What problem did it solve?
It connects small molecules that cells can assemble with large stores and supporting structures.
? Why does it work?
Covalent bonds join the units. Link orientation and branching alter the resulting material and which enzymes can act on it.
? How does it help?
It explains why the same glucose ingredients can make a food store or a tough cell wall.
?? How do we know?
Chemical breakdown identifies units, and structural analysis identifies their connections. Different enzyme responses provide another test.
?? What it isn't
A guarantee that all glucose-containing molecules have the same properties, or that every large biological molecule is this kind of chain.
?? Where does it stop working?
A building-unit list omits sequence, connections and three-dimensional arrangement. Those details often decide function.
:::

### Other building patterns: proteins and lipids

Proteins use another set of small units: **amino acids**. Each has an amino group and an acid group, with a side chain that distinguishes it from others. Cells commonly use twenty types in proteins, though there are rarer additions. Links called **peptide bonds** form an ordered chain, a **polypeptide**. A ==protein== consists of one or more such chains in a structure that affects what it does. We will follow its folding in Madras next.

:::note Protein
A biological molecule made from one or more amino-acid chains. Sequence, three-dimensional arrangement and chemical conditions affect its properties and function.
:::

:::five Amino acids and proteins
? What is it?
Amino acids are the units joined by peptide bonds into the chains of proteins. Their side chains differ chemically.
? Why is it called this?
Amino acid names the amino and acid groups. Protein comes from a Greek word meaning of first importance; that history is not its mechanism.
? What problem did it solve?
Different ordered chains can make many kinds of molecular tools from a manageable set of units.
? Why does it work?
Side chains differ in charge, size and interactions with water and with one another. Their order helps determine which arrangements are stable.
? How does it help?
It connects a food component with channels, carriers, supporting fibres and chemical helpers inside cells.
?? How do we know?
Chemical analysis reveals amino-acid sequences; structural methods reveal how their chains are arranged.
?? What it isn't
One particular food, or a chain whose units are all identical. Dal contains proteins too.
?? Where does it stop working?
Sequence alone is not a complete description of the working molecule's surroundings, partners or chemical modifications.
:::

Fats and oils provide a third pattern. In a ==triglyceride==, one glycerol is joined to three fatty acids. Their long, largely nonpolar chains help explain the oil-and-water puzzle. Fats are usually solid and oils liquid at the stated room temperature; chain structure and packing influence melting. A bend at a cis double bond can hinder close packing.

:::note Triglyceride
A fat or oil molecule containing one glycerol joined to three fatty acids by ester links. It is one member of the broader lipid family.
:::

:::five Fats, oils and lipids
? What is it?
Fats and oils contain triglycerides. Lipids are a broader group that also includes membrane phospholipids and steroids.
? Why is it called this?
Tri means three: a triglyceride has three fatty-acid attachments to glycerol. Fat and oil are everyday names distinguishing physical behaviour.
? What problem did it solve?
These structures provide concentrated chemical fuel stores; other lipids help form cell boundaries.
? Why does it work?
The long nonpolar parts interact poorly with water. Chain shape affects how closely neighbouring molecules pack.
? How does it help?
It links an oil droplet on lunch to storage molecules and the oily interior of a membrane.
?? How do we know?
Chemical analysis identifies glycerol and fatty acids in triglycerides; other lipid classes have different structures.
?? What it isn't
An ordinary polymer made by repeating a single monomer indefinitely. Three tails do not make every lipid a triglyceride.
?? Where does it stop working?
The triglyceride sketch cannot explain every lipid, and “plant oil, animal fat” is not a universal classification rule.
:::

:::

Predict, then gently turn each closed container over a few times and stand it upright. Sketch immediately and again after settling. Droplets may remain for a while; record what you actually see rather than promising a fixed settling time. This comparison shows mixing behaviour, not individual hydrogen bonds. ## 4.2 Folding the ribbon in Madras

:::scene Madras, a collagen proposal in 1954

A ribbon can bend in many ways. A protein chain seems to offer an enormous number of possibilities too. Perhaps any arrangement that looks neat on paper will do. But paper lines have no thickness; atoms occupy space.

At the University of Madras, now Chennai, G. N. Ramachandran and Gopinath Kartha investigated collagen, a supporting protein in tissues such as skin and tendons. Their 1954 paper proposed three helical chains arranged together. Their 1955 revision coiled the chains around a common axis, with further refinements through later work. A plausible drawing had to satisfy chemical dimensions and experimental evidence together.

:::predict
Two drawings contain the same amino-acid sequence. In one, nonbonded atoms are forced almost into the same space. Can both be equally plausible protein structures?
- Yes: sequence is the only thing that matters
- No: a structure must also satisfy spatial and chemical restrictions
- Yes: if both drawings keep the same covalent bonds, the distances between other atoms do not matter
???
The second answer. Bonded atoms have particular arrangements, and nonbonded atoms strongly resist being pushed too close. A drawing can preserve the sequence while proposing a physically implausible shape.
:::

:::evidence A map of arrangements that fit | 1963 publication
Ramachandran, C. Ramakrishnan and V. Sasisekharan calculated how neighbouring parts of a protein backbone could turn without forcing atoms too close together. They used known peptide geometry and sets of minimum nonbonded contact distances. Many pairs of turning angles were excluded; others remained possible.

They then compared those allowed ranges with arrangements in known small peptides and polypeptide structures. Those comparisons tested the restrictions against structural evidence. The map did not predict a complete protein from its sequence, and its boundaries depended on the geometric assumptions. It established a powerful constraint: a chain's local turns are not independent, unrestricted choices. This paper appeared in 1963; its submission had arrived in December 1962.
:::

:::story p-ramachandran

Rejecting crowded arrangements leaves another question: why does a whole sequence take a particular shape? Different side chains provide different chemical possibilities. Opposite charges can attract. Suitable groups form hydrogen bonds. In water, nonpolar regions often cluster away from the surrounding water. Some proteins have additional covalent links between sulfur-containing side chains. These interactions favour some arrangements over others.

:::fig c4_folding() | The three collagen strands and local crowding comparisons are original schematic projections. They are not atomic coordinates or a reproduction of the 1963 map. “Allowed locally” does not mean “the full fold has been predicted”.

A functioning protein is not frozen marble. It fluctuates, some regions remain flexible or disordered, and its surroundings help determine which structures are stable. Folding brings side chains that were far apart along the sequence into a shared pocket. That pocket can now present several chemical contacts to a visitor at once. Chapter 3's selective doorway is beginning to make sense.

:::raw

Collagen is a fibrous example, not a ball-shaped enzyme.</p></details>
:::

:::five Protein folding and the Ramachandran plot
? What is it?
Folding is the formation of a chain's three-dimensional arrangement. A Ramachandran plot maps pairs of local backbone turning angles.
? Why is it called this?
Folding describes a chain arranging in space. The plot bears Ramachandran's name; the 1963 paper also credits Ramakrishnan and Sasisekharan.
? What problem did it solve?
It helped reject proposed local arrangements that crowded atoms, even when a sketch looked convincing.
? Why does it work?
Known bond geometry and nonbonded contact distances restrict possible turns. Chemical interactions and surroundings also influence the full structure.
? How does it help?
It helps researchers inspect protein structures and connects an amino-acid sequence with a chemically useful surface.
?? How do we know?
Calculated angle restrictions were compared with known structures. Modern structural measurements greatly extend those comparisons.
?? What it isn't
A complete folding recipe, a perfectly rigid protein, or evidence that every protein is globular.
?? Where does it stop working?
A local angle map omits many distant interactions, partners and environmental effects. An unusual angle needs investigation, not automatic dismissal.
:::

:::careful
> “Once the amino acids are in the right order, the shape cannot change.”

The sequence restricts possibilities, but water, temperature, pH and binding partners affect the interactions stabilising a shape. Those conditions can change while the peptide sequence remains intact.
:::

## 4.3 The lock that bends the stick

:::scene A watery cell, where chemistry happens

A molecule bumps against a protein. If suitable groups meet in a suitable arrangement, it may bind briefly. The surroundings of that pocket can then help chemical bonds rearrange. A different visitor, even one with a similar outline, may lack the right chemical groups.

A rigid key entering a rigid lock makes a useful first picture of selection. It gives a poor explanation of chemistry. A door lock does not normally help change the key into products.

:::predict
An enzyme speeds a reaction. Which explanation could preserve the same reactants, products and overall energy account?
- The enzyme supplies a fresh packet of net energy on every cycle
- The enzyme provides a route with a lower difficult hurdle between reactants and products
- The enzyme makes the forward reaction faster while leaving the reverse reaction completely unaffected
???
The second answer. A catalyst changes the route and its activation barrier. It does not change the net free-energy difference between the same starting and finishing states.
:::

:::evidence Chemistry outside intact cells | Buchner, 1897
Eduard Buchner obtained press juice from yeast and found that it could ferment sugar even after filtration that retained yeast cells. His report also compared sugars: the preparation acted on some, while others did not show fermentation under the tested conditions. The activity was therefore more selective than a general ability to make any dissolved substance react.

The result separated chemical activity from the presence of intact living yeast cells. It did not isolate a single purified enzyme, and it did not reveal every step of fermentation. We now understand that fermentation involves a system of enzymes. For this chapter, the decisive point is smaller: molecules made by cells can keep assisting reactions outside the cells. Those helpers need not themselves be living organisms.
:::

A substance that increases reaction rate and is regenerated by the reaction is a **catalyst**. A biological catalyst is an ==enzyme==. Most enzymes are proteins; some RNA molecules also catalyse reactions and are called ribozymes. Here we follow the protein examples.

:::note Enzyme
A biological catalyst that speeds a chemical reaction without being used up by that reaction. Most enzymes are proteins.
:::

The reactant an enzyme acts on is its ==substrate==. It binds in a region called the ==active site==, forming a temporary ==enzyme–substrate complex==. The active site is a three-dimensional chemical environment, with groups contributed by the folded protein. Its geometry and chemistry favour particular partners and reactions: **specificity**. Some enzymes act on a family of related substrates rather than exactly one molecule type.

:::note Substrate
A reactant that binds to an enzyme and is acted on in its catalysed reaction.
:::

:::note Active site
The region of an enzyme where a substrate binds and the catalysed chemistry takes place. Its shape and chemical groups both matter.
:::

:::note Enzyme–substrate complex
The temporary association of an enzyme with its bound substrate or substrates.
:::

### Follow one catalytic cycle

Take the digestive enzyme **lactase** and its substrate **lactose**, the sugar in milk. A lactose molecule encounters lactase by molecular motion. If the molecule meets the active site in a suitable orientation, matching chemical groups hold it briefly. Lactase helps a reaction with water split lactose into the smaller sugars glucose and galactose. Those products leave, and lactase is available for another lactose molecule. The enzyme participates in the cycle and is regenerated; it is not a piece of food spent to make each pair of products.

Follow the sequence with your finger: **enzyme + substrate → bound complex → enzyme + products**. Water participates in this hydrolysis reaction even though that short sequence leaves it out. The changing contacts help explain the abstract binding cycle in the live model below; that model is not an atomic reconstruction of lactase. It also shows why binding a visitor and helping it react are different questions.

Binding can shift the enzyme's and substrate's shapes: **induced fit**. Bringing reactive groups close and pointing them correctly can help. The active site may also stabilise charges that develop during a reaction, exchange a proton with a reactant, or distort a substrate towards a difficult reactive arrangement. These are different chemical ways of making that arrangement easier to reach. The title's “bending stick” is one picture, not a rule that every enzyme physically bends every substrate.

:::five Enzymes and their active sites
? What is it?
An enzyme is a reusable biological catalyst. Its active site binds substrates and provides chemical interactions that help them react.
? Why is it called this?
Enzyme comes through German from Greek word-parts connected with leavening. Active site names the region where binding and chemistry occur.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains how selected reactions can run fast enough for life in watery conditions, without heating the whole cell enormously.
? Why does it work?
The folded site positions particular groups and stabilises difficult reactive arrangements. Binding can alter both partners' shapes.
? How does it help?
It links molecular recognition with digestion, synthesis and the many reactions that keep cells operating.
?? How do we know?
Experiments measure rates with and without enzymes, test substrates, and investigate binding and structure. Cell-free preparations show that intact living cells are not always required.
?? What it isn't
A conscious selector, a perfectly rigid lock or a reactant used up once for each product molecule.
?? Where does it stop working?
An unsuitable substrate, missing partner or altered chemical environment can prevent effective catalysis. Enzymes can also become damaged over time.
:::

### Why the cycle can run faster

A reaction often has to pass through a difficult arrangement before reaching products, even when the overall change releases usable energy. The hurdle is ==activation energy==. More precisely, the free-energy pathway has an activation barrier above the reactants. An enzyme supplies an alternative route with a lower barrier, so more reacting molecules can cross it under the same conditions.

:::note Activation energy
The energy hurdle involved in reaching a reactive arrangement. An enzyme lowers the activation barrier by providing a different pathway; it does not supply the reaction's net energy.
:::

:::fig c4_barrier() | Chosen energy levels illustrate a principle, not a measured reaction. Both routes begin at 20 and end at 10. The horizontal axis follows reaction progress, not seconds or a molecule climbing a literal mountain.
:::live enzyme_barrier {"lesson":true}

In this invented example, the uncatalysed forward hurdle is 90 − 20 = 70 units. The lower route gives 55 − 20 = 35 units. Both finish at 10, so the net change remains 10 − 20 = −10 units. Halving this hurdle does **not** mean halving the time; the drawing supplies no rate calculation.

A catalyst also accelerates the reverse reaction. It helps a closed system approach equilibrium faster without changing the equilibrium position. Making an overall unfavourable product still requires a suitable energy account, often coupling to another reaction. Remember [*The Moving World*](https://saxster.github.io/kym-physics/): energy is accounted for, not created by a helpful name. Breaking a chemical bond requires energy; an overall reaction can release energy when formation of new interactions more than compensates.

:::five Activation energy
? What is it?
The hurdle between reactants and a difficult reactive arrangement. It helps determine reaction speed.
? Why is it called this?
Activation refers to reaching the reactive arrangement. It does not mean switching an enzyme's life on.
? What problem did it solve?
It separates “this change can release energy” from “this change happens quickly”.
? Why does it work?
Only a fraction of molecular encounters reach the required arrangement. A lower-barrier pathway makes successful reactions more frequent.
? How does it help?
It explains catalysis without inventing an energy gift from the enzyme or a different equilibrium.
?? How do we know?
Reaction-rate measurements and pathway studies distinguish kinetic barriers from the free-energy difference between endpoints.
?? What it isn't
The net energy released, a fixed time delay or the enzyme's own fuel supply.
?? Where does it stop working?
Our single-hump sketch omits intermediate steps and supplies no measured rate. Real catalytic pathways can have several barriers.
:::

:::careful
> “The lock has used up its power after one reaction.”

During a catalytic cycle the enzyme can change temporarily, but it is regenerated when the cycle finishes. Products leave, allowing another cycle. Reuse does not make the enzyme indestructible: damage, inhibitors or unsuitable conditions can stop it working.
:::

## 4.4 Why enzymes do not “die” when heated

:::scene Mumbai, the washing instructions

A packet of biological washing powder promises help with stains. It can contain enzymes that break larger stain molecules into smaller, more washable pieces. Would hotter water always make those helpers work better?

Chapter 3 gives one tempting argument: warmer molecules move faster, so more encounters should mean more reactions. That argument has a missing condition. The enzyme must still have a functional active site.

:::predict
An enzyme reaction runs slowly after strong heat exposure. Which comparison best tests whether the enzyme has persistently lost activity?
- Compare the heated sample while hot with a fresh sample while cold
- Cool the heated sample, then compare it with fresh enzyme at the same assay temperature and pH
- Call the enzyme dead because no reaction is visible
???
The second comparison. Matching the assay conditions separates continuing loss of activity from the immediate effect of different temperatures. The enzyme is a molecule, not an organism that can die.
:::

:::evidence Separate the treatment from the measurement | A controlled school investigation
A teacher can prepare equal enzyme samples, expose them to different temperatures for the same time, then assay them under a common set of conditions. Keep the substrate amount, pH and measurement method comparable. Include an untreated enzyme sample and a no-enzyme comparison. Repeated measurements help distinguish a small effect from experimental variation.

A persistent activity loss after the samples return to the same assay conditions supports heat damage. It does not show every peptide bond has broken, nor does it establish a universal temperature at which all enzymes fail. A separate experiment measuring activity at different assay temperatures answers the immediate rate question. These are proposed comparisons, not measured results from our kitchen or the curves below.
:::

While the enzyme remains functional, warming usually increases molecular kinetic energy and the frequency of effective enzyme–substrate encounters. But heat can disrupt the interactions stabilising its structure. The active site may lose the arrangement needed for binding and catalysis. Unfolded proteins may also stick together into aggregates.

Loss of a protein's functional three-dimensional structure is ==denaturation==. The amino-acid chain can remain connected while its useful arrangement is lost. Cutting peptide bonds is chemical breakdown of the chain, a different change. The familiar change from runny to cooked egg white involves denaturation and aggregation. This is an explanation of cooking, not an activity: there is no need to handle raw egg or use heat for this chapter.

:::note Denaturation
Loss of a protein's functional three-dimensional structure. It does not necessarily break the peptide chain, and it can sometimes be reversed under suitable conditions.
:::

Cold often slows an enzyme reaction without unfolding the enzyme. Warming that sample can restore a faster rate. Cooling a badly heat-damaged sample is different: its protein may not refold, especially after aggregation. Some denatured proteins **can** recover under suitable conditions, so “denaturation is always irreversible” goes too far too.

:::live enzyme_conditions {"lesson":true}

For a particular enzyme and measurement procedure, the condition giving the highest measured rate is its ==optimum==. An apparent optimum depends on the enzyme, substrate, exposure time and other conditions. It is not a temperature printed into every protein. Our interactive example deliberately uses invented parameters and a fixed, unspecified exposure; it cannot diagnose a fever or choose a washing temperature.

:::note Optimum
The condition giving the highest activity in the particular enzyme assay being considered. Change the enzyme or measurement conditions and the optimum can change.
:::

Acidity matters through chemistry too. A change in **pH** changes whether some side groups carry a proton, and therefore their charges and interactions. A group that must exchange a proton during catalysis may now be in the wrong state. Binding or reaction rate can change even without the whole protein unfolding. More extreme pH can disrupt the structure and denature it. A low rate alone does not prove which of these changes occurred. Think back to acids, bases and charge in Book 1.

:::five Denaturation and optimum conditions
? What is it?
Denaturation is loss of functional protein structure. An optimum is the condition giving the highest activity under the chosen assay conditions.
? Why is it called this?
Denature means changing natural properties. Optimum means best: here, best for measured activity in a stated comparison.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why hotter can first mean faster, then slower, and why low activity need not mean an enzyme has died.
? Why does it work?
Temperature affects both encounters and structural stability. pH changes charges and catalytic chemistry, and extremes can disturb folding.
? How does it help?
It makes sense of cooling, heat damage and useful washing conditions without assigning every enzyme the same limit.
?? How do we know?
Compare rates while changing one condition, then test treated and untreated samples under the same assay conditions for persistent loss.
?? What it isn't
Death, automatic cutting of every peptide bond, or proof that every change is permanent.
?? Where does it stop working?
A schematic curve cannot predict a real enzyme's optimum. A low rate may also reflect little substrate, an inhibitor or poor measurement.
:::

:::careful
> “All enzymes are fine below one fever temperature, then all of them die together.”

Different proteins have different stabilities, and exposure time matters. Enzymes were never alive. Use “activity changed” or “denatured” when the evidence supports it; do not turn a classroom curve into a medical rule.
:::

:::recall 1
Can the seed's enzymes assist reactions even though an enzyme molecule does not carry out all the jobs of a living organism?
???
Yes. Chapter 1 distinguished a living cellular system from one process or component. An enzyme can catalyse a reaction without itself being a living organism; cell-free preparations help demonstrate the distinction.

:::check
Two samples react slowly: one is cold, the other has been strongly heated and then cooled. Why is testing both with fresh substrate at the same temperature and pH useful?
???
It removes different immediate assay temperatures and pH as explanations for their rate difference. Recovery of the cold sample is consistent with reversible slowing. Continuing loss in the heat-treated sample supports persistent damage, though more evidence is needed to identify the exact structural change. Neither result is an enzyme dying.
:::

## 4.5 Forensic food tests

:::scene A supervised school laboratory, five labelled portions

Return to lunch. “Rice contains starch” is a useful starting claim, but a detective should be able to ask what a particular sample reveals. A food may contain several kinds of molecules at once. Its natural colour can also confuse a test.

A tempting shortcut is to add something, notice any colour and announce a nutrient. First ask what the reagent detects, what counts as a positive observation and whether the comparison worked.

:::predict
An unknown food sample gives no positive result. The known positive tested alongside it also fails. What can you conclude about the unknown?
- The target substance is definitely absent
- The test has not shown that it can detect the target, so the unknown remains inconclusive
- The unknown is probably negative because it matched the known positive
???
The second answer. A failed positive control prevents a confident negative conclusion. Reagent quality, procedure or conditions may be wrong.
:::

:::evidence A result needs neighbours | The controlled comparison
Use separate portions of the sample for different tests. Beside each unknown, process a known target-containing sample in the same way. This positive control checks whether the reagent and method can give the expected response. A reagent blank, such as water treated with the same reagents, checks for a misleading background. An untreated sample helps identify the food's original colour or cloudiness.

If the known positive responds and the blank does not, the unknown's response becomes interpretable. If either control behaves unexpectedly, investigate before drawing the food conclusion. These are comparisons a class can make, not guaranteed outcomes for every lunch. Extraction, sample concentration, interfering substances and the eye's judgement all place limits on what a colour test establishes.
:::

A ==food test== uses a chemical response to look for a particular kind of substance. In this section, **positive** means the expected response was detected in a valid comparison. **Negative** means it was not detected under those conditions; a very small amount might escape detection.

:::note Food test
A chemical procedure used to investigate whether a sample contains a detectable target substance or molecular feature. Controls and the method's limitations are part of interpreting the result.
:::

**Benedict's test looks for reducing sugars**, including glucose. In a teacher-managed hot water bath, reducing sugars can change the blue reagent to a coloured precipitate, ranging from green through yellow and orange to brick-red under suitable conditions. A precipitate is a solid appearing in the liquid. Run glucose as a known positive. Untreated sucrose can give a negative result: “sugar” and “reducing sugar” are not interchangeable. Colour can support rough comparisons only when preparation, volumes, heating and standards are controlled; it is not a direct reading of grams in your lunch.

**Iodine solution looks for starch.** Its yellow-brown appearance changes to blue-black when enough starch is present under suitable conditions. Iodine-containing species interact with the coiled starch structure, changing light absorption. Use known starch alongside the unknown. Cellulose is a carbohydrate too, but this is not a test for all carbohydrates.

**Biuret reagent looks for peptide bonds**, the links in proteins. Copper ions in alkaline conditions form a coloured complex with suitable peptide groups: a blue reagent gives lilac or purple with a positive protein sample. A known protein solution is the positive control. Free amino acids need not give that response. Nor does a positive result prove a protein is correctly folded, alive or acting as an enzyme.

:::live food_detective {"lesson":true}

**The ethanol-emulsion test looks for fats and oils.** A suitable sample is extracted with ethanol; the clear extract is then added to water. Lipid that was dissolved in ethanol forms tiny droplets in the watery mixture, scattering light and making a white, cloudy emulsion. Compare with an ethanol-and-water blank and a known oil sample processed the same way. A food that was cloudy before testing does not establish a positive emulsion result by itself.

**DCPIP looks for vitamin C through a reducing reaction.** Vitamin C can make the blue dye lose its colour. Use known vitamin C and a water blank. In acidic conditions the dye may appear pink rather than blue, and the sample's own colour can hide the endpoint. Other reducing substances can interfere. A calibrated method with standards can estimate vitamin C; simply watching a coloured juice lighten is not an exact measurement, and this test does not detect all vitamins.

The tests ask different molecular questions. One sample can be positive for starch, protein and fat while showing no detectable reducing sugar. Do not force every food into one box. Our virtual unknown is an invented mixture for practising interpretation, not measured data from rice, dal or a named product.

:::five Controlled food tests
? What is it?
A set of chemical comparisons that look for reducing sugars, starch, peptide bonds, fats/oils or vitamin C.
? Why is it called this?
Each test is named for its reagent or procedure. Emulsion means droplets of one liquid dispersed in another; the visible scattering is the clue.
? What problem did it solve?
It provides evidence about a sample beyond its appearance or the name on a food packet.
? Why does it work?
Different molecular groups produce different chemical or physical responses. Controls check whether the response can be interpreted.
? How does it help?
It turns an unknown food into a set of testable claims, while keeping absence, failed testing and interference separate.
?? How do we know?
Known positives and blanks are processed beside unknowns, with repeated, consistent preparation. Standards are needed for quantitative estimates.
?? What it isn't
An exact nutrient balance from colour alone, a test of whether protein is folded, or permission to taste a reagent-treated sample.
?? Where does it stop working?
Amounts below detection, poor extraction, coloured samples, interfering substances and failed controls can defeat a simple conclusion.
:::

:::careful
> “No colour change proves there is none, and a stronger colour tells me the exact amount.”

First check the controls and the target. A valid negative means “not detected under these conditions”. Quantities require a calibrated method, standards and uncertainty; different sample colours or heating times can spoil a casual comparison.
:::

These are **school laboratory procedures, not home activities**. Follow the teacher's risk assessment and exact instructions. Wear eye protection, never taste samples or mouth-pipette, and use clean labelled equipment. A teacher manages hot water baths and reagent preparation. Ethanol is flammable: keep it away from flames and never heat it for this test. Biuret reagent is alkaline; its hazard depends on concentration. The teacher collects copper-containing and other reagent waste according to the laboratory's disposal rules. Wash hands afterwards. No household substitutes or mixtures are needed.

:::check
An unknown gives blue-black with iodine and purple with biuret; both sets of controls behave correctly. Does this prove it contains an active starch-digesting enzyme?
???
No. The results support detectable starch and protein/peptide bonds. They do not identify the protein, establish its fold or show catalysis. Testing enzyme activity would require a separate controlled comparison of reaction rate with an appropriate substrate.
:::

## The washing-powder case

:::case The hottest wash was not the best wash
Two matched stained cloth pieces are washed for the same time, with the same amount of biological washing powder and the same agitation. One wash follows the packet's recommended temperature range. The other uses excessively hot water. In this fictional comparison, the hotter wash leaves more of a protein-containing stain.

A second comparison provides a clue: equal enzyme samples from the two treatments are later tested with fresh substrate at the same suitable temperature and pH. The sample exposed to excessive heat has much less remaining activity. A no-enzyme washing comparison helps distinguish the detergent's other effects. Real washing also depends on the stain, fabric, formulation and time, so one cloth result alone would not identify the cause.

Explain why faster molecular motion did not guarantee better cleaning. Would adding “more energy” to the enzyme's name solve the problem?
???
The powder's protease can catalyse breakdown of protein in the stain. At a suitable temperature, encounters are frequent and the active site remains functional. Excessive heat can denature the enzyme and promote aggregation, leaving fewer functional active sites. Faster encounters then cannot compensate for the lost catalytic machinery; the lower residual activity at a common assay temperature supports persistent damage.

The enzyme was not killed or used up as fuel. It normally lowers activation barriers and is regenerated. The detergent's other ingredients may still work, which is why both activity controls and washing controls help. Follow product instructions; this is a reasoning case, not a request to handle hot detergent or reproduce a wash experiment.
