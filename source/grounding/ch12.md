## 3. Historical, Experimental, and Quantitative Grounding (`TRUE / HEDGE / LEGEND` Discipline)

### 3.1 Watson & Crick's 1953 Prediction and the Three Competing Replication Models (`§12.1`)
- **Primary Source:** Watson, J. D. & Crick, F. H. C. (1953), "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid", *Nature* `171`: 737–738 (25 April 1953), and "Genetical Implications of the Structure of Deoxyribonucleic Acid", *Nature* `171`: 964–967 (30 May 1953).
- **Exact Sentence (`TRUE`):** *"It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material."*
- **Three Competing Hypotheses in 1953–1957 (`TRUE`):**
  1. **Semi-conservative replication** (proposed by Watson & Crick): the two parental strands unzip; each serves as a template for a new complementary strand, so every daughter double helix consists of **one original parental strand and one newly synthesised strand**.
  2. **Conservative replication**: the original parental double helix stays intact (re-zips after templating or acts as an intact template), producing one 100% old double helix and one 100% brand-new double helix.
  3. **Dispersive replication** (championed as a theoretical test case by Max Delbrück, 1954): parental chains are cleaved at intervals during copying so every daughter strand is a patchwork mosaic of old and new DNA segments.

### 3.2 Meselson & Stahl (1958): Heavy Nitrogen (`¹⁵N`) and `CsCl` Density-Gradient Ultracentrifugation (`§12.1`)
- **Primary Source:** Meselson, M. & Stahl, F. W. (1958), "The Replication of DNA in *Escherichia coli*", *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the USA* (`PNAS`) `44`(7): 671–682 (communicated by Max Delbrück, 14 May 1958; published July 1958).
- **Where & Who (`TRUE`):** Graduate student **Matthew Meselson** (aged 27) and postdoctoral fellow **Franklin Stahl** (aged 28) working at the **California Institute of Technology (Caltech)** in Pasadena in 1957–1958 (having first met under a tree at Woods Hole, Massachusetts, in summer 1954).
- **Method & Quantitative Facts (`TRUE`):**
  - Cultured *Escherichia coli* strain B for `14` generations at `36 °C` in synthetic glucose minimal medium whose sole nitrogen source was heavy ammonium chloride (`¹⁵NH₄Cl`, `96.5%` isotopic purity). Note: `¹⁵N` is a **stable heavy isotope** (`7` protons, `8` neutrons; NOT radioactive), making nitrogenous bases denser without radiation damage.
  - Abruptly shifted exponentially growing cells (generation time ≈ `50 min` at `36 °C`, typically cited cleanly as `20–50 min` per bacterial doubling depending on medium) into an excess (`10-fold`) of light `¹⁴NH₄Cl` (`7` protons, `7` neutrons) plus `¹⁴N` ribosides/deoxyribosides, sampling every fraction of a generation.
  - Lysed cells with sodium dodecyl sulfate (`SDS`) and spun the DNA in concentrated **caesium chloride (`CsCl`) solution** in a Beckman Model E analytical **ultracentrifuge** at `44,770 rpm` (~`140,000 × g`) for `20–48 hours` until a stable equilibrium buoyant density gradient formed, photographed by ultraviolet absorption at `260 nm`.
  - **Buoyant Densities in `CsCl` (`TRUE`):**
    - Pure heavy `¹⁵N/¹⁵N` *E. coli* DNA: **`1.724 g/cm³`** (sinks lower toward bottom of tube).
    - Hybrid `¹⁵N/¹⁴N` *E. coli* DNA: **`1.717 g/cm³`** (exact intermediate band).
    - Pure light `¹⁴N/¹⁴N` *E. coli* DNA: **`1.710 g/cm³`** (floats higher toward top of tube).
  - **Generation-by-Generation Band Fractions (Semi-Conservative Ground Truth):**
    - **Generation 0 (`0` doublings, `1` duplex ratio):** `100%` Heavy (`¹⁵N/¹⁵N`, `1.724 g/cm³`), `0%` Hybrid, `0%` Light.
    - **Generation 1 (`1` doubling, `2` duplexes):** `0%` Heavy, **`100%` Hybrid (`¹⁵N/¹⁴N`, `1.717 g/cm³`)**, `0%` Light. **Eliminates the conservative hypothesis** (which predicted `50%` Heavy at `1.724 g/cm³` + `50%` Light at `1.710 g/cm³` and zero hybrid band).
    - **Generation 2 (`2` doublings, `4` duplexes):** `0%` Heavy, **`50%` Hybrid (`¹⁵N/¹⁴N`, `1.717 g/cm³`)** + **`50%` Light (`¹⁴N/¹⁴N`, `1.710 g/cm³`)**. **Eliminates the dispersive hypothesis** (which predicted a single intermediate band at `1.7135 g/cm³` halfway between hybrid and light, rather than two cleanly separated bands).
    - **Generation 3 (`3` doublings, `8` duplexes):** `0%` Heavy, **`25%` Hybrid (`2/8`)** + **`75%` Light (`6/8`)**.
    - **Generation 4 (`4` doublings, `16` duplexes):** `0%` Heavy, **`12.5%` Hybrid (`2/16`)** + **`87.5%` Light (`14/16`)**.
    - **General mathematical law for Generation `g ≥ 1`:** Number of hybrid (`¹⁵N/¹⁴N`) molecules remains invariant at `2` (holding the `2` original parental `¹⁵N` single strands), while total duplex molecules equal `2ᵍ`. Thus `Hybrid fraction = 2 / 2ᵍ = 1 / 2ᵍ⁻¹` and `Light fraction = 1 − (1 / 2ᵍ⁻¹)`.

### 3.3 DNA Helicase, DNA Polymerase, Multiple Replication Origins, and Proofreading (`§12.1`)
- **Enzymatic Mechanism (`TRUE`):**
  - **DNA helicase** unwinds the double helix at a **replication fork** by breaking the weak hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (`2` H-bonds for `A=T`, `3` H-bonds for `C≡G`), exposing two single-stranded templates without breaking the strong covalent sugar–phosphate backbones.
  - Free deoxynucleoside triphosphates (`dATP`, `dTTP`, `dCTP`, `dGTP`) in the nucleoplasm pair with exposed template bases by complementary hydrogen bonding (`A` with `T`, `C` with `G`).
  - **DNA polymerase** (first isolated from *E. coli* by **Arthur Kornberg** in 1956, Nobel Prize 1959; eukaryotic nuclear replication uses polymerases `α`, `δ`, and `ε`) links incoming nucleotides into a new covalent sugar–phosphate backbone.
  - **Three-Stage Proofreading Fidelity (`TRUE`):**
    1. **Base-pairing geometry + polymerase active-site steric selection:** ~`1` error per `10⁴` to `10⁵` bases (`10⁻⁴`–`10⁻⁵`).
    2. **Polymerase `3'→5'` exonuclease proofreading (backspace key):** detects a mismatched pair immediately after incorporation, excises the wrong nucleotide, and inserts the correct one, improving fidelity ~`100–1,000×` to ~`10⁻⁷` errors per base.
    3. **Post-replication mismatch repair (`MMR`):** scans the newly synthesised strand and fixes remaining mismatches, reaching a final net error rate of ~`10⁻⁹` to `10⁻¹⁰` per base copied (roughly `1–6` stray base substitutions across an entire `6.4 × 10⁹ bp` human diploid genome division).
  - **Why Human S-Phase Takes Only `6–8 Hours` (`TRUE`):** Eukaryotic DNA polymerase travels at ~`50 nucleotides per second`. If a single polymerase copied all `6.4 × 10⁹ bp` end-to-end from one starting point, it would take ~`1,500 days` (`> 4 years`). Instead, human chromosomes open **`30,000 to 50,000` bidirectional replication origins (replication bubbles)** simultaneously across the `46` chromosomes during **S phase (synthesis phase)** of interphase.

### 3.4 Chromosome vs Sister Chromatid Accounting (`§12.1` & `§12.2`)
- **Core Pedagogical Distinction (`TRUE`):** Biologists count **chromosomes by the number of functional centromeres**, whereas they count **DNA double-helix molecules (`chromatids`)** by the number of continuous duplexes:
  - **G₁ phase (unreplicated human diploid somatic cell):** `46` chromosomes (`23` homologous pairs), each consisting of `1` DNA double helix = **`46` chromosomes, `46` DNA molecules (`0` sister-chromatid pairs), mass `2C` (`~6.4 pg` DNA)**.
  - **After S-phase replication (G₂ phase & early mitosis):** Each chromosome has been duplicated into **two identical sister chromatids** joined at a single **centromere** = still **`46` chromosomes (`23` pairs)**, now comprising **`92` chromatids (`92` DNA double helices), mass `4C` (`~12.8 pg` DNA)**.
  - **During mitosis (anaphase centromere split → telophase/cytokinesis):** Each centromere splits so sister chromatids separate to opposite poles; once separated, each chromatid is an independent chromosome in its own right = **two genetically identical daughter nuclei**, each holding **`46` chromosomes (`46` DNA molecules, `2n = 46`, mass `2C`)**.

### 3.5 Walther Flemming (1879–1882) and Mitosis (`§12.2`)
- **Primary Source:** Flemming, W. (1879), "Beiträge zur Kenntniss der Zelle und ihrer Lebenserscheinungen", *Archiv für mikroskopische Anatomie* `16`: 302–436; and Flemming, W. (1882), *Zellsubstanz, Kern und Zelltheilung*, F. C. W. Vogel, Leipzig.
- **Historical Grounding (`TRUE`):** Working at the **University of Kiel**, German anatomist **Walther Flemming** (`1843–1905`) used synthetic **aniline dyes** to stain dividing cells in the transparent tail fins and gill epithelium of **fire salamander larvae (*Salamandra maculosa*)** and the cornea. Because the nuclear material absorbed the basophilic crimson dye strongly, Flemming named it **chromatin** (from Greek *khroma*, colour). Tracing the thread-like chromosomes condensing, splitting longitudinally into two equal halves, and being pulled to opposite poles by spindle fibres, he coined the term **mitosis** in `1882` (from Greek *mitos*, warp thread). (`HEDGE`: Flemming did not yet know Mendel's laws or that chromatin was DNA; the word *chromosome* was coined six years later in `1888` by Heinrich Wilhelm Waldeyer.)
- **Four IGCSE `0610 17.2.2` Roles of Mitosis (`TRUE`):**
  1. **Growth:** Increasing cell number from `1` diploid zygote (`2n = 46`) to ~`37 trillion` somatic cells in an adult human, or root-tip / shoot-tip **apical meristem** divisions elongating a germinating onion root or mung bean seedling.
  2. **Repair of damaged tissues:** Dividing dermal fibroblasts and epidermal basal cells closing a scraped knee or surgical wound.
  3. **Replacement of worn-out cells:** Small-intestinal crypt cells replacing villus epithelium every `3–5 days`; basal epidermal cells replacing shed outer skin cells every `~27 days`; bone-marrow haematopoietic stem cells replacing ~`2 million` red blood cells per second.
  4. **Asexual reproduction:** Single-parent production of genetically identical offspring (**clones**) via mitosis alone—e.g. potato stem tubers (*Solanum tuberosum*) sprouting from axillary buds ("eyes"), strawberry stolons/runners (*Fragaria × ananassa*), bryophyllum leaf-margin plantlets, binary/mitotic budding in yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), *Hydra* budding, and vegetative suckers in sugarcane and banana.

### 3.6 Stem Cells, Selective Gene Expression, Gurdon (1962) & Yamanaka (2006) (`§12.3`)
- **Selective Gene Expression (`0610 17.1.6` & `17.2.5`, `TRUE`):**
  - Every nucleated somatic cell in a person's body descends from the same zygote by mitosis and therefore contains the **exact same `46` chromosomes and ~`20,000` protein-coding genes** (`100%` of the genomic library).
  - Differentiation does **not** delete unused genes (except in terminal special cases like mature mammalian red blood cells or lens fibre cells that extrude their entire nucleus *after* stockpiling protein).
  - Instead, cells use regulatory proteins (**transcription factors**) and chromatin packaging to keep **housekeeping genes** (e.g. `ATP5F1A` for ATP synthase, `GAPDH` for glycolysis, `ACTB` for cytoskeletal actin) **switched ON** in all living cells, while switching **specialist tissue genes** ON only where needed:
    - **Pancreatic beta cell:** `INS` (insulin gene on Chromosome `11`) **ON** → `INS` mRNA → insulin hormone; `HBB` (β-globin) **OFF**; `SCN9A/CHAT` (neuronal sodium channel / choline acetyltransferase) **OFF**.
    - **Bone-marrow erythroblast (immature red blood cell precursor):** `HBB` and `HBA1` (haemoglobin globin genes on Chromosomes `11` and `16`) **ON** → globin mRNA → `270 million` haemoglobin molecules packed into cytoplasm before the nucleus is ejected; `INS` **OFF**; `CHAT` **OFF**.
    - **Spinal motor neurone:** `CHAT` and voltage-gated ion channel genes **ON** → axonal signalling machinery; `INS` **OFF**; `HBB` **OFF**.
- **John Gurdon's 1962 Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer Proof (`TRUE`):**
  - **Primary Source:** Gurdon, J. B. (1962), "The Developmental Capacity of Nuclei taken from Intestinal Epithelium Cells of Feeding Tadpoles", *Journal of Embryology and Experimental Morphology* `10`(4): 622–640.
  - Working at the **University of Oxford**, **John Gurdon** (`1933–2025`, shared 2012 Nobel Prize) destroyed the haploid nucleus of an unfertilised frog egg (*Xenopus laevis*) with ultraviolet light and injected the diploid nucleus extracted from a fully differentiated **tadpole intestinal epithelial cell**. The reprogrammed egg divided by mitosis and developed into a normal swimming tadpole and adult fertile frog—proving experimentally that **differentiated somatic cells retain the complete, intact genome required to build every tissue in the body**.
- **Shinya Yamanaka's 2006 Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (`iPSCs`, `TRUE`):**
  - **Primary Source:** Takahashi, K. & Yamanaka, S. (2006), "Induction of Pluripotent Stem Cells from Mouse Embryonic and Adult Fibroblast Cultures by Defined Factors", *Cell* `126`(4): 663–676 (human iPSCs followed in *Cell* `131`: 861–872, 2007).
  - At **Kyoto University**, **Shinya Yamanaka** (shared 2012 Nobel Prize with Gurdon) and **Kazutoshi Takahashi** screened `24` candidate transcription factors and proved that introducing just **four master transcription-factor genes—`Oct4` (`Pou5f1`), `Sox2`, `Klf4`, and `c-Myc` ("Yamanaka factors")**—into adult skin **fibroblasts** resets their gene-expression switches back to an embryonic-like **induced pluripotent stem cell (`iPSC`)** state capable of differentiating into beating heart muscle cells, neurones, or pancreatic beta cells.
- **Categories of Stem Cells (`TRUE`):**
  - **Embryonic stem cells (`pluripotent`):** Inner cell mass of an early embryo (blastocyst); can differentiate into virtually any of the `>200` cell types of the body.
  - **Adult tissue stem cells (`multipotent`):** Found in bone marrow (forming red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets), basal skin epidermis, and intestinal crypts; divide by mitosis for lifelong tissue renewal into a restricted family of cell types.
  - **Plant meristem cells (`totipotent / persistently pluripotent`):** Apical meristems (root and shoot tips) and lateral cambium retain lifelong capacity to divide by mitosis and differentiate into xylem, phloem, root hairs, leaves, or entire cloned plants in tissue culture.
- **Indian Tissue-Culture Pioneer (`TRUE`):** **Kamal Ranadive** (`1917–2001`) established India's first mammalian tissue-culture laboratory at the **Indian Cancer Research Centre (ICRC)** in Parel, Mumbai, in the 1950s–1960s, growing human and mouse cell lines in vitro to study how normal mitotic division and differentiation switches break down in cancer and leprosy. (Note: `Kamal Ranadive` is already owned as a story term by `source/src/stories/p-ranadive.md`, so she is listed in `people:` of the Chapter 12 companion story and linked via her existing story, keeping zero duplicate `terms:` ownership.)

### 3.7 Meiosis (`Reduction Division`), Haploid/Diploid Arithmetic, and E. K. Janaki Ammal (`§12.4`)
- **Historical Discovery of Meiosis & Fertilisation (`TRUE`):**
  - **Oscar Hertwig** (`1876`) observed in transparent Mediterranean sea-urchin eggs (*Toxopneustes lividus*) that **fertilisation is the physical fusion of one sperm nucleus and one egg nucleus** to form a single zygote nucleus.
  - **Edouard van Beneden** (`1883`) discovered in the parasitic roundworm *Ascaris megalocephala* (where `2n = 4` in *bivalens* or `2n = 2` in *univalens*) that sperm and egg nuclei each carry **exactly half** the chromosome count of body cells, and that fertilisation restores the diploid count (`n + n = 2n`).
  - **August Weismann** (`1887`) predicted theoretically that a special **reduction division** must occur during germ-cell maturation to prevent chromosome numbers from doubling every generation; **J. B. Farmer and J. E. S. Moore** (`1905`) coined the word **meiosis**.
- **Exact Quantitative Meiosis Mechanics (`TRUE`):**
  - Starts in a **diploid germ-line progenitor cell (`2n = 46`, `23` homologous pairs)** in human testes or ovaries (or anthers and ovules in flowering plants).
  - **One round of DNA replication** occurs before meiosis (`46` chromosomes duplicated to `92` chromatids), followed by **two consecutive nuclear and cell divisions (`Meiosis I` and `Meiosis II`)**:
    1. **Meiosis I (Reduction Division):** Maternal and paternal **homologous chromosomes pair up** side-by-side (`23` bivalents), swap corresponding DNA segments at **crossing-over points (`chiasmata`)**, and are pulled apart in **independent assortment** so each daughter nucleus receives **one chromosome from each homologous pair (`n = 23` chromosomes, `46` chromatids)**.
    2. **Meiosis II (Chromatid Separation):** Sister chromatids separate without further DNA replication, yielding **four haploid gametes (`n = 23` chromosomes, `23` DNA molecules each)**, all **genetically different** from one another and from the parent cell.
  - **Combinatorial Variation Math (`TRUE`):**
    - For `n` homologous pairs, random orientation of maternal vs paternal chromosomes at Meiosis I alone produces **`2ⁿ`** possible chromosome combinations per gamete.
    - In humans (`n = 23`): **`2²³ = 8,388,608`** (~`8.4 million`) distinct gamete combinations from independent assortment alone, before even counting crossing over.
    - Random fertilisation between one sperm (`2²³`) and one egg (`2²³`) yields **`2²³ × 2²³ = 2⁴⁶ ≈ 7.04 × 10¹³` (`~70 trillion`)** possible diploid zygote combinations from a single pair of parents.
- **E. K. Janaki Ammal (`1897–1984`) and Sugarcane Polyploidy in Coimbatore (`TRUE`):**
  - At the ** Imperial Sugarcane Breeding Institute in Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu** (`1934–1939`), Indian cytogeneticist **E. K. Janaki Ammal** stained and counted chromosome sets during meiosis in crosses between tropical noble sugarcane (*Saccharum officinarum*, typically octoploid `2n = 80`, sweet and thick-stemmed but cold-sensitive) and wild Indian kans grass (*Saccharum spontaneum*, `2n = 48–128`, hardy and disease-resistant) as well as intergeneric hybrids (`Saccharum × Zea`, `Saccharum × Imperata`, `Saccharum × Bambusa`).
  - She demonstrated that noble *S. officinarum* frequently transmits **unreduced (`2n`) maternal gametes (`80` chromosomes)** when pollinated by haploid (`n`) *S. spontaneum* pollen (`2n + n` transmission), producing high-yielding, cold-hardy **polyploid hybrid canes** (`Co` varieties) that ended India's reliance on imported sugar. She subsequently co-authored the *Chromosome Atlas of Cultivated Plants* (`1945`) with C. D. Darlington.

### 3.8 Case 12: The Seedless Cavendish Banana (`3n = 33`) & Panama Disease TR4 (`Case 12`)
- **Chromosomal & Agricultural Facts (`TRUE`):**
  - Wild ancestral bananas (*Musa acuminata* and *Musa balbisiana*) are **diploid (`2n = 22`, with basic chromosome set `x = 11`)**. Their cells undergo normal meiosis (`2n = 22 → n = 11` gametes), fertilise, and pack their small fruits with dozens of rock-hard, tooth-cracking black seeds.
  - Early farmers selected rare natural **triploid (`3n = 33`, `AAA` genome group)** banana plants (formed when an unreduced diploid gamete `2x = 22` fused with a normal haploid gamete `x = 11`).
  - Because `33` chromosomes consist of **`11` triplets (`3` homologous copies of each chromosome)**, they **cannot pair evenly two-by-two during Meiosis I**: at each of the `11` triplets, `2` chromosomes move to one pole and `1` moves to the other at random, producing unbalanced, aneuploid gametes that abort. As a result, triploid bananas are **meiotically sterile** and develop **parthenocarpically** into lush, **seedless yellow fruit** (containing only tiny dark brown vestigial ovule specks).
  - Because triploid Cavendish bananas (`Musa acuminata` cv. `'Cavendish'`, and Indian `'Grand Naine'` / `'Robusta'` selections grown across Maharashtra's Jalgaon belt, Tamil Nadu, and Gujarat) make no viable seeds, every commercial Cavendish plantation on Earth is propagated **asexually by mitosis**—either by slicing **underground rhizome suckers (corms)** or by **meristem tissue culture**.
  - **The Monoculture Trap (`0610 16.1.2` & `16.2.4`, `TRUE`):** Asexual mitotic cloning gives farmers `100%` uniform, predictable, seedless high-yield fruit (`advantage of asexual reproduction`), but zero genetic variation (`disadvantage of asexual reproduction`). In the 1950s, soil fungus *Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *cubense* **Race 1** wiped out the world's previous export banana clone (`'Gros Michel'`, also `3n = 33`). Today, **Tropical Race 4 (`TR4`)** of the same fungus is spreading across Asia, Africa, and Latin America because a pathogen adapted to infect one Cavendish clone can infect all billions of genetically identical Cavendish plants worldwide—driving breeders to return to wild **diploid (`2n = 22`)** seeded bananas and **meiosis/sexual crossing** to breed disease-resistant replacements.
